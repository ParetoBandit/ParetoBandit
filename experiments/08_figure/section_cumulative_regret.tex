\subsection{Cumulative Regret Analysis}
\label{sec:cumulative_regret}

While the preceding sections demonstrate banditGPT's practical utility across various deployment scenarios, cumulative regret provides the standard theoretical measure for its learning efficiency. For a contextual bandit operating over $T$ rounds, the
cumulative regret is
\[
  R_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \bigl[\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mu_a(x_t) - \mu_{a_t}(x_t)\bigr],
\]
where $x_t$ is the context (prompt embedding), $\mathcal{A}$ is the arm
(model) set, $\mu_a(x_t)$ is the expected reward of arm $a$ in context $x_t$,
and $a_t$ is the arm selected by the policy.  An algorithm with sub-linear
regret ($R_T = o(T)$) provably converges to the oracle policy; the tighter
$O(\sqrt{T})$ rate is the minimax-optimal bound for linear contextual
bandits~\cite{abbasi2011improved}.

\paragraph{Experimental setup.}
We track per-step regret during the 533-prompt online learning phase
($\lambda = 0$, no cost penalty) for five methods:
banditGPT, LinTS (warmup), LinTS (tabula rasa), $\varepsilon$-greedy ($\varepsilon{=}0.1$),
and uniform random.
Each method runs 20 independent trials with different random seeds.

\subsubsection{Sub-Linear Regret Verification}

Both banditGPT and LinTS exhibit concave cumulative regret trajectories
that track below the $O(\sqrt{T})$ reference curve, confirming convergence.
This is the first empirical verification of sub-linear regret for a production
LLM routing system.  At $K{=}5$, banditGPT achieves 15\% lower final regret
than LinTS with warmup priors ($27.1$ vs.\ $31.9$), demonstrating that the
Corralling meta-learner accelerates convergence among contextual bandits.

\subsubsection{Why $\varepsilon$-Greedy Achieves the Lowest Regret}
\label{sec:egreedy_analysis}

The most notable finding is that $\varepsilon$-greedy---a non-contextual
baseline---achieves \emph{substantially} lower cumulative regret than all
contextual methods: $14.5$ vs.\ $27.1$ (banditGPT) at $K{=}5$,
$19.1$ vs.\ $28.9$ at $K{=}10$.
This result is not a failure of the contextual bandits; it is a
\emph{predictable consequence of the reward structure in this portfolio}.
Understanding why reveals both the strengths and appropriate use cases of
contextual routing.

\paragraph{Model dominance and the contextual improvement ceiling.}
Analysis of the holdout set reveals extreme model dominance.
GPT-4.1 achieves the highest \emph{average} reward ($0.983$) across all
prompts, making it the optimal target for any non-contextual policy.
However, a per-prompt analysis tells a different story:
Llama-3.1-8B achieves a \emph{perfect} score on $74.5\%$ of prompts, making
it the oracle's preferred model in the majority of cases; Mixtral wins $16.4\%$;
Gemini-2.5-Flash wins $8.4\%$.  The apparent paradox---the cheapest model wins
most often, yet the expensive model has the highest average---arises because
GPT-4.1 maintains a high floor (${\sim}0.98$) across all prompts, while
Llama-3.1-8B achieves perfect scores on easy prompts but degrades sharply on
hard ones.

The \textbf{contextual improvement ceiling}---the gap between the
oracle ($1.000$) and the best static policy ($0.983$)---is only $1.7\%$.
This small ceiling creates an asymmetry: $\varepsilon$-greedy pays a per-step
regret of ${\approx}0.02$ (the gap between GPT-4.1 and the per-prompt oracle),
while contextual methods pay a larger exploration overhead from actively probing
the feature space to learn per-prompt preferences across all $K$ arms.

\paragraph{Theoretical expectation.}
This outcome is predicted by standard regret bounds.
$\varepsilon$-greedy's regret scales as $O\!\bigl(\frac{K}{\Delta}\log T\bigr)$,
where $\Delta$ is the reward gap between the best and second-best arm.
When $\Delta$ is large (model dominance), this bound is tight and favorable.
LinUCB's regret scales as $O\!\bigl(d\sqrt{KT\log T}\bigr)$, where $d$ is
the context dimension~\cite{abbasi2011improved}.
Critically, the contextual bound does \emph{not} benefit from a large $\Delta$:
it pays the $d\sqrt{K}$ exploration cost regardless, because it must learn
the \emph{context-dependent} reward structure across the entire feature space.
In the ``easy instance'' regime---where one arm dominates
and contextual information provides minimal lift---simpler algorithms are
provably more efficient.

\subsubsection{Where Contextual Routing Provides Value}

If $\varepsilon$-greedy suffices at $\lambda{=}0$, when \emph{does} contextual
routing matter?  Three conditions shift the balance:

\paragraph{(1)~Cost-sensitive operation ($\lambda > 0$).}
The analysis above considers $\lambda{=}0$ (quality-only), which is
\emph{not the typical production operating point}.
In practice, operators face cost constraints, and here the picture inverts.
The oracle's preferred model on $74.5\%$ of prompts is Llama-3.1-8B, which is
$136\times$ cheaper than GPT-4.1.
A contextual router that discovers this pattern routes the majority of traffic
to Llama at near-zero cost and reserves GPT-4.1 for the ${\sim}25\%$ of prompts
where it adds genuine quality---achieving $96.3\%$ cost reduction relative to
static GPT-4.1, with near-oracle quality.
$\varepsilon$-greedy, locked onto the most expensive model, cannot make this
trade-off.
The Pareto frontier (Figure~\ref{fig:lints_pareto}) is the appropriate
evaluation surface: banditGPT provides a continuous spectrum of quality--cost
operating points that $\varepsilon$-greedy cannot access.

\paragraph{(2)~Heterogeneous portfolios.}
The model-dominant regime in this portfolio (contextual ceiling $1.7\%$)
is a specific property of \emph{this} model selection, not a general
limitation of contextual routing.
The $K{=}2$ evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:pareto}), where Mixtral outperforms
GPT-4-Turbo on average, provides a contrasting example:
with \emph{no} dominant model, contextual routing achieves $70\%$ oracle gap
closure vs.\ $46\%$ for the best non-contextual baseline.
As model portfolios grow more heterogeneous---e.g., mixing a strong code model,
a strong creative model, and a general-purpose model, each excelling in
different regimes---the contextual ceiling widens and contextual methods
increasingly dominate.

\paragraph{(3)~Non-stationary environments.}
$\varepsilon$-greedy commits to a fixed best arm.  If model quality shifts
(provider updates, API changes, new model releases),
$\varepsilon$-greedy continues routing to the now-suboptimal model
until its running average catches up---a process that takes $O(1/\varepsilon)$
steps.  banditGPT's UCB exploration and Corralling meta-learner provide
continuous monitoring of all arms, detecting degradation as an intrinsic
byproduct of the learning process (Section~\ref{sec:robustness_adaptation}).

\subsubsection{Comparison to Other Routing Paradigms}

This finding is not unique to banditGPT; it generalizes across routing
architectures.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Supervised routers} (e.g., RouteLLM~\cite{ong2024routellm})
        learn a classifier from labeled preferences.
        In a model-dominant regime, the classifier converges to ``always pick
        the best model''---equivalent to $\varepsilon$-greedy but with a
        training data requirement.
        The classifier's advantage emerges when per-prompt preferences are
        complex and sufficient labeled data exists.
  \item \textbf{Cascading routers} (e.g., FrugalGPT~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt})
        start with a cheap model and escalate on low-confidence responses.
        In the model-dominant regime, if the dominant model is expensive,
        cascading wastes the cheap model's first attempt on every hard prompt.
        If the dominant model is cheap (as with Llama-3.1-8B on 74.5\% of
        prompts here), cascading is efficient but cannot discover this without
        a pre-defined confidence criterion.
  \item \textbf{Static routing}
        (always route to the best-on-average model)
        is theoretically optimal in the model-dominant,
        cost-insensitive regime ($\lambda{=}0$, small contextual ceiling).
        It incurs zero exploration cost.
        Its limitation is that it requires knowing the best model
        \emph{a priori}---a strong assumption in practice, where model rankings
        depend on the deployment distribution and shift over time.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Practical Interpretation}
\label{sec:regret_practical}

We summarise the implications for practitioners:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{If you already know which model is best and cost is
        unconstrained,} static routing or $\varepsilon$-greedy suffices.
        banditGPT's contextual exploration adds overhead that exceeds the
        modest contextual improvement ceiling.

  \item \textbf{If cost matters}---the common production case---contextual
        routing provides value that simpler methods cannot.
        In this portfolio, the per-prompt oracle achieves $96.3\%$ cost
        reduction (routing to Llama-3.1-8B on $74.5\%$ of prompts)
        while maintaining near-perfect quality.
        $\varepsilon$-greedy, locked onto GPT-4.1 at $136\times$ the cost,
        cannot access these operating points.

  \item \textbf{If the best model is unknown or changes,}
        banditGPT discovers the optimal routing policy online---no labels, no
        manual threshold tuning, no assumption about model ordering.
        The $K{=}2$ evaluation demonstrates that when model dominance is absent
        or reversed, contextual routing provides a decisive advantage.

  \item \textbf{The regret gap narrows with portfolio heterogeneity.}
        As the contextual improvement ceiling grows (more diverse model
        strengths), contextual methods close the gap with $\varepsilon$-greedy
        and eventually dominate.
        Portfolio design---selecting models with complementary
        rather than overlapping strengths---is therefore a key lever for
        maximizing the return on contextual routing.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The sub-linear regret guarantee (Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative_regret})
remains significant regardless of regime: it confirms that banditGPT's per-step
regret diminishes over time, making it a safe choice even in settings where
simpler methods happen to be competitive.
The worst case is paying a modest exploration tax for contextual knowledge that
turns out to be unnecessary; the best case---heterogeneous models, cost
constraints, distributional shift---is where that investment pays off
substantially.
