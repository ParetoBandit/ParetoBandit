\subsection{Cold-Start vs Warmup Priors (Figure~\ref{fig:warmup_ablation})}
\label{sec:warmup_ablation}

We isolate the contribution of warmup priors by comparing
ParetoBandit with offline priors against a tabula-rasa cold start
(identity covariance, zero bias) on the $K{=}3$ stationary portfolio
under three budget regimes: unconstrained (no pacer), tight
($B{=}\$3.0{\times}10^{-4}$/req), and moderate
($B{=}\$6.6{\times}10^{-4}$/req).
Each condition uses the optimal hyperparameters from the Experiment~04
sweep: ParetoBandit uses $\alpha{=}0.1$, $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$,
$\gamma{=}0.995$; Tabula Rasa uses $\alpha{=}0.1$,
$n_\mathit{eff}{=}1$, $\gamma{=}0.995$ (disjoint LinUCB).
Budget-constrained conditions use the Primal-Dual BudgetPacer
with the same learning rate and $\lambda_{\max}$ as Experiment~01.
Following the standard contextual-bandit cumulative-regret
protocol, the router learns and is evaluated simultaneously on
the held-out test split ($n{=}1{,}824$, disjoint from warmup-prior
training on \texttt{train.jsonl} and hyperparameter selection on
\texttt{val.jsonl}).

\paragraph{Unconstrained.}
Without a budget pacer, warmup priors reduce total regret by 10\%
($74.5$ vs.\ $83.1$; $\Delta{=}-8.7$, $p < 10^{-5}$) and
early regret (R@200) by 47\%
($6.6$ vs.\ $12.5$).
The advantage is concentrated in the first ${\sim}200$~steps
(Figure~\ref{fig:warmup_ablation}), where the warmup router begins
with informed arm-quality estimates and avoids the exploratory phase
that the cold-start router must perform.  Beyond step~${\sim}400$,
the two routers converge, but the regret gap established early
persists through the full horizon.

\paragraph{Tight budget.}
Under a tight budget the warmup advantage persists
($170.8$ vs.\ $179.9$; $\Delta{=}-9.1$, $p{=}0.059$; 5\%
reduction).
The budget pacer penalises expensive arms, so the cold-start
router's early UCB-driven exploration of Gemini-Pro
(${\sim}29{\times}$ costlier than Mistral-Large:
\$0.0151/req vs.\ \$0.0005/req in our dataset) consumes real
budget without delivering proportionate information gain.
The warmup router, already knowing each arm's quality profile,
allocates its limited budget more efficiently from step~1.

\paragraph{Moderate budget.}
At moderate budgets the warmup advantage vanishes
($151.4$ vs.\ $151.7$; $\Delta{=}-0.3$, $p{=}0.94$, not
significant).
The budget constraint forces both routers toward similar
affordable arm mixes: at the final step the warmup router
allocates 59\% Mistral-Large / 39\% Llama-8B / 2\% Gemini-Pro,
and the tabula-rasa router allocates 67\% / 31\% / 2\%
(from logged arm fractions).
Within this constrained decision space the routing problem is
simple enough for the cold-start router to solve quickly, and the
prior adds no incremental value.

\paragraph{Discussion.}
The budget-stratified results sharpen the recommendation:
warmup priors matter most when the router has freedom to
explore expensive arms (unconstrained, $p < 10^{-5}$); under
tight budgets the effect persists ($p{=}0.059$), though the
budget constraint attenuates the penalty of uninformed early
exploration by limiting how much the cold-start router can
spend on expensive arms.
When the budget collapses the feasible arm set to a small,
affordable subset (moderate), the prior's knowledge about
expensive arms becomes irrelevant.

Importantly, the warmup benefit is \emph{transient by design}.
The convergence beyond step~${\sim}400$ visible in
Figure~\ref{fig:warmup_ablation} is a direct consequence of
geometric forgetting ($\gamma{=}0.995$): after ${\sim}200$
effective-memory steps, online evidence dominates and the
initialisation is irrelevant.
Appendix~\ref{appendix:val_burnin_ablation} confirms this
quantitatively: after a full validation burn-in
($1{,}785$ steps), warmup and tabula-rasa conditions achieve
identical test regret ($74.4$ vs.\ $75.4$, $p{=}0.29$) because
the prior has decayed by $\gamma^{1785} \approx 10^{-4}$.
Warmup priors are therefore best understood as a
\emph{deployment latency optimisation}---they eliminate the
cold-start transient where uninformed exploration dominates,
but the router's steady-state quality is determined by online
learning alone.

\paragraph{Business perspective.}
Intelligent LLM routing delivers simultaneous quality and cost
gains because no single model dominates all queries.
In our $K{=}3$ portfolio, oracle routing---selecting the per-query
best model---achieves 3.3\% higher quality than routing all
traffic to the frontier model
($(0.963 - 0.932)/0.932$; mean reward on the test split)
while reducing per-request cost by 66\%
($(1 - 0.005/0.015)$; mean oracle cost vs.\ mean Gemini-2.5-Pro
cost on the test split, at March 2026 API pricing of
\$1.25/M input and \$10.00/M output
tokens\footnote{Google Cloud Vertex AI pricing,
\url{https://cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/pricing},
accessed March 2026.}).
This dual improvement---better quality \emph{and} lower
cost---arises because the frontier model is not universally
best: in our test split, a cheaper model strictly outscores
Gemini-2.5-Pro on 35\% of prompts (and ties on a further
37\%), so Gemini is the unique best choice on only 27\% of
queries.
The direction of the result is consistent with RouteLLM, which
shows that routers can maintain $>$95\% of frontier quality at
$>$50\% lower cost in a $K{=}2$
setting~\cite{ong2024routellm}.

To illustrate the scale at which these per-request differences
compound, we project to enterprise traffic volumes.
Azure OpenAI Service alone reports 230{,}000 organisational
deployments processing over 100~trillion tokens per
quarter~\cite{azure2025stats}; a mid-sized deployment
handling $10^5$--$10^6$ queries/day is well within this range.
The per-request cost gap between frontier-only and oracle
routing in our portfolio is
$\$0.0151 - \$0.0051 = \$0.0100$/req, so
$10^5 \times \$0.010 \times 365 = \$365\text{K/year}$,
scaling linearly to ${\sim}\$3.7\text{M}$ at $10^6$ daily
requests---with no quality sacrifice, since oracle routing
\emph{improves} quality over the single-model baseline.

Within this context, warmup priors protect routing quality during
the critical---but transient---cold-start window.
Under tight budgets, the 5\% regret reduction
($(179.9 - 170.8)/179.9$; $p{=}0.059$) represents 5\% of the
router's total quality--cost value recovered solely by starting
with informed priors.
Applying the same projection:
$0.05 \times \$365\text{K} \approx \$18\text{K/year}$ at $10^5$
daily requests, rising to ${\sim}\$183\text{K}$ at $10^6$.
This captures the cost of cold-start exploration, where every
uninformed call on a \$0.015 frontier arm under a tight
budget simultaneously degrades quality \emph{and} consumes
budget that would otherwise fund later high-value routing
decisions.
The early regret reduction (R@200) is even starker:
47\% in the unconstrained regime
($6.6\,[6.2,\,7.1]$ vs.\ $12.5\,[10.9,\,14.1]$)
and 8\% under a tight budget
($21.8\,[20.0,\,23.6]$ vs.\ $23.8\,[21.7,\,26.0]$).
In production, these first few hundred queries correspond to
the initial deployment window where user trust is most
fragile---precisely when suboptimal routing is costliest.

These projections are illustrative: the per-request costs are
specific to our three-model portfolio and March 2026 pricing,
and the traffic volumes are drawn from public platform
statistics rather than a single organisation's logs.
Actual savings depend on the specific model mix, prompt
distribution, and pricing at deployment time.
Nevertheless, warmup priors are easy to justify when
calibrated on representative data: computing them from a
labelled offline dataset takes minutes and requires no ongoing
maintenance~\cite{kveton2023warmstart}.
The key practical lever is $n_\mathit{eff}$, which controls
how strongly the prior anchors the router.
With well-calibrated priors (as in our experiments),
$n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$ lets the router exploit prior
knowledge immediately.
When prior quality is uncertain---e.g., the offline dataset
comes from a different prompt distribution or from stale model
versions---a lower $n_\mathit{eff}$ (e.g., 10--100) gives
the router a gentle initial nudge while preserving enough
exploration for rapid online correction.
A dedicated prior-mismatch sensitivity analysis
(Appendix~\ref{appendix:prior_mismatch}) quantifies these
trade-offs across five quality levels---from well-calibrated
through domain-restricted to actively inverted priors.
The results show that \emph{directionally correct} priors
(including domain-mismatched priors trained exclusively on MMLU
or GSM8K prompts) consistently reduce regret relative to a cold
start at every $n_\mathit{eff}$ tested ($p < 0.05$ for
$n_\mathit{eff} \geq 100$), confirming that partial domain
coverage does not harm the router.
However, \emph{systematically wrong} priors---where the best
and worst models are inverted---cause measurable harm that
\emph{scales with} $n_\mathit{eff}$: at $n_\mathit{eff}{=}10$
the damage is statistically non-significant ($p{=}0.055$),
but at $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$ regret increases by 13\%
over cold start ($p < 10^{-6}$).
The practical recommendation is therefore conditional:
enable warmup priors whenever the prior is known to be
directionally correct (i.e., the model-quality ranking it
encodes is not systematically inverted), setting
$n_\mathit{eff}$ to reflect confidence in calibration
quality---high when the offline data closely matches deployment
traffic, low otherwise.
If prior quality is entirely unknown, a conservative
$n_\mathit{eff}$ (10--100) limits worst-case exposure while
still offering early-regret reduction on any non-adversarial
prior (Appendix~\ref{appendix:prior_mismatch},
Figure~\ref{fig:prior_mismatch_heatmap}).
The computational cost of warmup priors is negligible, and the
upside---at enterprise scale, where LLM API spend surpassed
\$8.4B by mid-2025~\cite{menlo2025llmmarket}---is economically
meaningful.

\paragraph{Uncertainty evolution (Figure~\ref{fig:warmup_uncertainty}).}
To visualise the mechanism behind the early regret gap, Figure~\ref{fig:warmup_uncertainty} plots $\mathrm{tr}(A_a^{-1})$---the total uncertainty per arm---for the unconstrained conditions across the trajectory.
The context dimension is $d{=}26$ (PCA components) and the
regularisation is $\lambda{=}1$ (default LinUCB).
Cold-started arms (dashed lines) therefore begin at
$\mathrm{tr}(A_a^{-1}) = \mathrm{tr}((\lambda I)^{-1}) = d / \lambda = 26$.
Warmup-initialised arms (solid lines) start at
$\mathrm{tr}(A_a^{-1}) = 0.72$, reflecting the
$n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$ pseudo-observations encoded in the
offline prior---a $26 / 0.72 = 36{\times}$ uncertainty gap.
Because the UCB exploration bonus scales as $\alpha\sqrt{x^\top A_a^{-1} x}$, this gap means the cold-start router must spend hundreds of steps exploring before it accumulates enough information to route as confidently as the warmup router does from step~1.
With geometric forgetting ($\gamma{=}0.995$), arms that receive few observations see their uncertainty \emph{grow} over time as the prior decays; the warmup Llama-8B trace rises from 0.72 to ${\sim}10$, illustrating how forgetting trades long-term certainty for adaptability.
The two conditions largely converge by step~${\sim}400$ for frequently selected arms (Gemini-Pro, Mistral-Large), consistent with the regret convergence in Figure~\ref{fig:warmup_ablation}.
