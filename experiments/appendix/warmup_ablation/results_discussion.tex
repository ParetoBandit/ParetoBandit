\subsection{Cold-Start vs Warmup Priors (Figure~\ref{fig:warmup_ablation})}
\label{sec:warmup_ablation}

We isolate the contribution of warmup priors by comparing
ParetoBandit with offline priors against a tabula-rasa cold start
(identity covariance, zero bias) on the $K{=}3$ stationary portfolio
under four budget regimes: unconstrained (no pacer), tight
($B{=}\$3.0{\times}10^{-4}$/req), moderate
($B{=}\$6.6{\times}10^{-4}$/req), and loose
($B{=}\$1.87{\times}10^{-3}$/req).
Each condition uses its own optimal hyperparameters from the
Experiment~05 Pareto knee-point sweep: ParetoBandit uses
$\alpha{=}0.01$, $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}163.9$,
$\gamma{=}0.997$; Tabula Rasa uses $\alpha{=}0.01$,
$n_\mathit{eff}{=}1$, $\gamma{=}0.995$ (disjoint LinUCB).
Budget-constrained conditions use the Primal-Dual BudgetPacer
with the same learning rate and $\lambda_{\max}$ as Experiment~01.
Following the standard contextual-bandit cumulative-regret
protocol, the router learns and is evaluated simultaneously on
the held-out test split ($n{=}1{,}824$, disjoint from warmup-prior
training on \texttt{train.jsonl} and hyperparameter selection on
\texttt{val.jsonl}).

\noindent
Twenty seeds; 95\% bootstrap CI; test split ($n{=}1{,}824$).
\textbf{Cat.}\ = catastrophic failures (regret $> 2\times$ warmup median).
$p_\text{sign}$: exact sign test on per-seed regret
(H$_0$: P(warmup wins)${}= 0.5$); tests whether warmup has
lower regret seed-by-seed (location).
$p_\text{Fisher}$: one-sided Fisher exact test on the $2{\times}2$
catastrophic-failure table (H$_0$: equal failure rates); tests whether
warmup reduces tail risk.
The two tests answer complementary questions: at moderate and loose
budgets, warmup does \emph{not} consistently beat tabula rasa
seed-by-seed ($p_\text{sign} > 0.05$), but it \emph{does} significantly
reduce catastrophic failure ($p_\text{Fisher} < 0.005$).
Tabula rasa's bimodal regret distribution precludes Wilcoxon's
symmetry assumption.

\paragraph{Unconstrained.}
Without a budget pacer, warmup priors reduce total regret by 10\%
($74.5$ vs.\ $83.1$; $\Delta{=}-8.7$, $p < 10^{-5}$) and
early regret (R@200) by 47\%
($6.6$ vs.\ $12.5$).
The advantage is concentrated in the first ${\sim}200$~steps
(Figure~\ref{fig:warmup_ablation}), where the warmup router begins
with informed arm-quality estimates and avoids the exploratory phase
that the cold-start router must perform.  Beyond step~${\sim}400$,
the two routers converge, but the regret gap established early
persists through the full horizon.

\paragraph{Tight budget.}
Under a tight budget warmup produces dramatically more reliable
outcomes ($161.2$ vs.\ $229.5$; $\Delta{=}-68.3$, $p < 10^{-3}$).
The mean comparison understates the effect: Tabula Rasa's per-seed
regret is \emph{bimodal}, splitting into 11 ``good'' seeds
(regret~${\sim}160$--$179$, comparable to warmup) and 9
``catastrophic'' seeds (regret~${\sim}300$--$310$).
The mechanism is the budget pacer: when early UCB-driven exploration
probes Gemini-Pro (${\sim}29{\times}$ costlier than Mistral-Large:
\$0.0151/req vs.\ \$0.0005/req in our dataset), the dual variable
$\lambda$ spikes, the pacer penalises all future expensive-arm
selections, and the router becomes locked into a suboptimal cheap
arm for the remainder of the horizon.
Seeds where early exploration happens to avoid the expensive arm
converge normally.
Warmup priors, already knowing each arm's quality profile, avoid
this trap entirely: all 20~seeds fall within a $4.6$-std band.

\paragraph{Moderate budget.}
The sign test reports no significant location shift
($11/20$ warmup wins, $p_\text{sign}{=}0.41$)---warmup does
\emph{not} consistently beat Tabula Rasa seed-by-seed.
This is because the ``good'' Tabula Rasa seeds (15/20,
regret~$73$--$121$) actually achieve \emph{lower} regret than
warmup's $114$--$155$, benefiting from a fortunate exploration
path through cheap arms and enough budget headroom for
convergence.
The remaining 5~seeds catastrophically fail at $222$--$307$,
producing a 35\% catastrophic failure rate that the Fisher test
detects as highly significant ($p_\text{Fisher}{=}0.004$).
The mean comparison ($133.9$ vs.\ $182.4$) averages excellent
lucky outcomes with catastrophic unlucky ones; it is neither a
reliable summary of Tabula Rasa's typical performance nor
evidence that warmup is unnecessary.

Warmup's $\mathit{std}{=}11.0$ at moderate budget is notably
wider than at tight ($4.6$) or loose ($5.1$).
The arm fractions explain why: at tight budget, the constraint is
so binding that all seeds converge to the same cheap-arm mix
(Llama 45\%, Mistral 55\%, Gemini 0\%)---no room for
seed-dependent variation.
At loose budget, the constraint barely binds and warmup routes
near-optimally.
But at moderate budget, the pacer's dual variable $\lambda$ sits
at the margin between two operating regimes: small
seed-dependent differences in early exploration lead to different
$\lambda$ trajectories, which compound into a wider spread of
final regret.
This is the same mechanism that produces Tabula Rasa's
catastrophic failures, heavily attenuated because warmup's priors
prevent the worst-case $\lambda$ spike.
The violin plot (Figure~\ref{fig:warmup_ablation}) may suggest
apparent bimodality in warmup's moderate-budget distribution, but
this is a KDE smoothing artifact: warmup's 40-point range with a
9-point largest gap is well within normal sampling variation for
$n{=}20$ ($\mathit{CV}{=}8.2\%$ vs.\ Tabula Rasa's
$52.6\%$).

\paragraph{Loose budget.}
Under a loose budget ($B{=}\$1.87{\times}10^{-3}$/req) the
constraint is permissive enough that the router can freely
select most arms, yet the bimodal failure mode persists.
The sign test again shows no significant location shift
($12/20$ warmup wins, $p_\text{sign}{=}0.25$), because
12~of 20 Tabula Rasa seeds achieve $73$--$172$---comparable to
or better than warmup's consistently moderate $83.6$
($\mathit{std}{=}5.1$).
However, the remaining 8~seeds catastrophically fail
(regret~${\sim}269$--$304$), producing a 40\% catastrophic
failure rate ($p_\text{Fisher}{=}0.002$).
Even a loose budget constraint is sufficient to trigger the
pacer--exploration interaction that produces catastrophic seeds.

\paragraph{Cold-start bimodality.}
The budget-stratified results reveal a qualitative finding
beyond mean regret reduction: Tabula Rasa's per-seed regret is
\emph{bimodal} under every budget constraint tested.
Figure~\ref{fig:warmup_ablation} visualises this directly:
under tight constraints, 9/20 seeds ($45\%$) catastrophically
fail; under moderate constraints, 5/20 ($25\%$); under loose
constraints, 7/20 ($35\%$).
The bimodality is not noise---it is a deterministic consequence of
the interaction between UCB exploration and the budget pacer.
The cold-start router has high initial uncertainty over all arms,
so UCB occasionally selects the expensive frontier arm
(Gemini-Pro) during early exploration.  This triggers a
dual-variable spike in the primal-dual pacer, which then penalises
\emph{all} future expensive-arm selections.
Because information about the expensive arm was incomplete when the
pacer locked it out, the router is unable to learn the arm's true
quality and remains stuck exploiting a suboptimal cheap arm.
Seeds where early exploration happens to probe cheap arms first
avoid this trap and converge normally---producing the ``good'' mode.

Warmup priors break this dynamic by providing informed initial
quality estimates, so the router never needs to waste budget
on uninformative early exploration of expensive arms.
The result is zero catastrophic seeds across all 80 budget-warmup
trials ($4 \text{ regimes} \times 20 \text{ seeds}$).

\paragraph{Statistical summary.}
Two complementary tests formalise these findings:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item The \textbf{sign test} asks whether warmup has lower regret
    seed-by-seed (location shift).  It is significant for
    unconstrained ($p < 10^{-6}$) and tight ($p < 10^{-3}$)
    budgets---where warmup consistently wins---but not for moderate
    ($p{=}0.41$) or loose ($p{=}0.25$), where ``lucky'' Tabula
    Rasa seeds outperform warmup.
  \item The \textbf{Fisher exact test} asks whether warmup has
    fewer catastrophic failures (tail risk).  It is significant at
    moderate ($p{=}0.004$) and loose ($p{=}0.002$) budgets---precisely
    where the sign test is not---because 35--40\% of Tabula Rasa
    seeds catastrophically fail while warmup has zero failures.
\end{itemize}
These tests are not contradictory: they answer different questions.
At moderate budget, warmup does not consistently beat Tabula Rasa
seed-by-seed (the lucky TR seeds do fine), but it \emph{does}
eliminate the 35\% chance of catastrophic failure.
The mean $\pm$ CI presentation obscures this structure: a
``non-significant'' mean comparison averages over a bimodal
baseline, not evidence that warmup is unnecessary.
The operationally relevant metric is the catastrophic failure
rate, which warmup reduces from 25--45\% to zero.

\paragraph{Discussion.}
The budget-stratified results sharpen the recommendation:
warmup priors deliver both mean regret reduction \emph{and}
catastrophic failure elimination.
The former is largest in the unconstrained regime ($p < 10^{-5}$)
where early exploration freely wastes budget on expensive arms;
under budget constraints the mean advantage grows further
(e.g.\ $\Delta{=}-68$ at tight budget) because catastrophic
seeds inflate the Tabula Rasa mean.
The latter---zero catastrophic seeds vs.\ 25--45\%---is arguably
the more deployment-relevant benefit, since production systems
cannot re-roll their random seed.

Importantly, the warmup benefit is \emph{transient by design}.
The convergence beyond step~${\sim}400$ visible in the
unconstrained regime is a direct consequence of
geometric forgetting ($\gamma{=}0.997$): after ${\sim}333$
effective-memory steps, online evidence dominates and the
initialisation is irrelevant.
Appendix~\ref{appendix:val_burnin_ablation} confirms this
quantitatively: after a full validation burn-in
($1{,}785$ steps), warmup and tabula-rasa conditions achieve
identical test regret ($74.4$ vs.\ $75.4$, $p{=}0.29$) because
the prior has decayed by $\gamma^{1785} \approx 5{\times}10^{-3}$.
Under budget constraints, however, the catastrophic seeds
\emph{never} recover, because the budget pacer's dual variable
permanently locks out the expensive arm.
Warmup priors are therefore best understood as a
\emph{deployment reliability mechanism}---they eliminate both
the cold-start transient and the catastrophic pacer-interaction
failure mode.

\paragraph{Business perspective.}
Intelligent LLM routing delivers simultaneous quality and cost
gains because no single model dominates all queries.
In our $K{=}3$ portfolio, oracle routing---selecting the per-query
best model---achieves 3.3\% higher quality than routing all
traffic to the frontier model
($(0.963 - 0.932)/0.932$; mean reward on the test split)
while reducing per-request cost by 66\%
($(1 - 0.005/0.015)$; mean oracle cost vs.\ mean Gemini-2.5-Pro
cost on the test split, at March 2026 API pricing of
\$1.25/M input and \$10.00/M output
tokens\footnote{Google Cloud Vertex AI pricing,
\url{https://cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/pricing},
accessed March 2026.}).
This dual improvement---better quality \emph{and} lower
cost---arises because the frontier model is not universally
best: in our test split, a cheaper model strictly outscores
Gemini-2.5-Pro on 35\% of prompts (and ties on a further
37\%), so Gemini is the unique best choice on only 27\% of
queries.
The direction of the result is consistent with RouteLLM, which
shows that routers can maintain $>$95\% of frontier quality at
$>$50\% lower cost in a $K{=}2$
setting~\cite{ong2024routellm}.

To illustrate the scale at which these per-request differences
compound, we project to enterprise traffic volumes.
Azure OpenAI Service alone reports 230{,}000 organisational
deployments processing over 100~trillion tokens per
quarter~\cite{azure2025stats}; a mid-sized deployment
handling $10^5$--$10^6$ queries/day is well within this range.
The per-request cost gap between frontier-only and oracle
routing in our portfolio is
$\$0.0151 - \$0.0051 = \$0.0100$/req, so
$10^5 \times \$0.010 \times 365 = \$365\text{K/year}$,
scaling linearly to ${\sim}\$3.7\text{M}$ at $10^6$ daily
requests---with no quality sacrifice, since oracle routing
\emph{improves} quality over the single-model baseline.

Within this context, warmup priors protect routing quality during
the critical---but transient---cold-start window.
Under tight budgets, the 30\% regret reduction
($(229.5 - 161.2)/229.5$; $p < 10^{-3}$) represents a substantial
fraction of the router's total quality--cost value recovered solely
by starting with informed priors.
Applying the same projection:
$0.30 \times \$365\text{K} \approx \$110\text{K/year}$ at $10^5$
daily requests, rising to ${\sim}\$1.1\text{M}$ at $10^6$.
Critically, the mean understates the deployment risk:
45\% of Tabula Rasa seeds incur ${\sim}2{\times}$ the regret of
successful seeds, so a cold-start deployment is effectively a
coin flip on whether the router enters a catastrophic failure
mode.
The early regret reduction (R@200) is even starker:
47\% in the unconstrained regime
($6.6\,[6.2,\,7.1]$ vs.\ $12.5\,[10.9,\,14.1]$)
and 8\% under a tight budget
($21.8\,[20.0,\,23.6]$ vs.\ $23.8\,[21.7,\,26.0]$).
In production, these first few hundred queries correspond to
the initial deployment window where user trust is most
fragile---precisely when suboptimal routing is costliest.

These projections are illustrative: the per-request costs are
specific to our three-model portfolio and March 2026 pricing,
and the traffic volumes are drawn from public platform
statistics rather than a single organisation's logs.
Actual savings depend on the specific model mix, prompt
distribution, and pricing at deployment time.
Nevertheless, warmup priors are easy to justify when
calibrated on representative data: computing them from a
labelled offline dataset takes minutes and requires no ongoing
maintenance~\cite{kveton2023warmstart}.
The key practical lever is $n_\mathit{eff}$, which controls
how strongly the prior anchors the router.
With well-calibrated priors (as in our experiments),
$n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}164$ lets the router exploit prior
knowledge immediately.
When prior quality is uncertain---e.g., the offline dataset
comes from a different prompt distribution or from stale model
versions---a lower $n_\mathit{eff}$ (e.g., 10--100) gives
the router a gentle initial nudge while preserving enough
exploration for rapid online correction.
A dedicated prior-mismatch sensitivity analysis
(Appendix~\ref{appendix:prior_mismatch}) quantifies these
trade-offs across five quality levels---from well-calibrated
through domain-restricted to actively inverted priors.
The results show that \emph{directionally correct} priors
(including domain-mismatched priors trained exclusively on MMLU
or GSM8K prompts) consistently reduce regret relative to a cold
start at every $n_\mathit{eff}$ tested ($p < 0.05$ for
$n_\mathit{eff} \geq 100$), confirming that partial domain
coverage does not harm the router.
However, \emph{systematically wrong} priors---where the best
and worst models are inverted---cause measurable harm that
\emph{scales with} $n_\mathit{eff}$: at $n_\mathit{eff}{=}10$
the damage is statistically non-significant ($p{=}0.055$),
but at $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$ regret increases by 13\%
over cold start ($p < 10^{-6}$).
The practical recommendation is therefore conditional:
enable warmup priors whenever the prior is known to be
directionally correct (i.e., the model-quality ranking it
encodes is not systematically inverted), setting
$n_\mathit{eff}$ to reflect confidence in calibration
quality---high when the offline data closely matches deployment
traffic, low otherwise.
If prior quality is entirely unknown, a conservative
$n_\mathit{eff}$ (10--100) limits worst-case exposure while
still offering early-regret reduction on any non-adversarial
prior (Appendix~\ref{appendix:prior_mismatch},
Figure~\ref{fig:prior_mismatch_heatmap}).
The computational cost of warmup priors is negligible, and the
upside---at enterprise scale, where LLM API spend surpassed
\$8.4B by mid-2025~\cite{menlo2025llmmarket}---is economically
meaningful.
