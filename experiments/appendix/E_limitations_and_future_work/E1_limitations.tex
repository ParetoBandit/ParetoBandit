\subsection{Limitations and Applicability}
\label{sec:limitations}

To establish the scope and generalizability of our approach, we systematically
evaluated the architecture under varying conditions---from two-model routing
through twenty-model portfolios, across the full cost--quality Pareto spectrum,
and under catastrophic model failure.
Several important considerations emerged.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 1: Contextual Improvement Ceiling
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Contextual Improvement Ceiling.}
banditGPT's value derives from routing prompts to different models based on
context.
When one model dominates across nearly all prompts, the contextual improvement
ceiling---the gap between the per-prompt oracle and the best static
model---shrinks, and the exploration overhead of contextual methods exceeds
their routing gains.
In our $K{=}3$ portfolio, GPT-4.1 achieves high average
reward, leaving a small contextual ceiling.
At $\lambda{=}0$ (quality-only), $\epsilon$-greedy may achieve
lower cumulative regret than banditGPT \textbf{TODO: update with K=3 regret numbers}.

\emph{Implication:}
banditGPT's advantage is realized when cost matters ($\lambda > 0$), where it
discovers per-prompt cost savings that non-contextual methods cannot.
At $\lambda{=}1.0$ ($K{=}3$), banditGPT achieves substantial cost reduction over
static GPT-4.1 \textbf{TODO: update with K=3 cost reduction numbers}.
Practitioners who already know the best model and have no cost constraint
should prefer static routing or $\epsilon$-greedy over a full contextual
bandit.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 2: Prior Quality Dependency
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Prior Quality Dependency.}
Our two-model evaluation (Figure~3) demonstrates a challenging deployment
scenario: severe domain mismatch where 68.6\% of warmup training prompts are
classified as ``hard,'' but only 13.7\% of holdout prompts fall into this
category.
Under these conditions, constant exploration ($\alpha{=}2.0$) prevents
premature commitment to incorrect beliefs.

The prior degradation sweep ($N{=}20$ seeds) shows that Corralling provides
a modest advantage over tabula rasa at low corruption (regret 48.0 vs.\
51.3 at $\alpha{=}0$, a ${\sim}6\%$ reduction) but this advantage erodes
as corruption increases.  By $\alpha{\approx}0.3$ the two strategies perform
comparably, and at higher corruption Corralling's regret exceeds tabula
rasa's (54--59 vs.\ 51.3 at $\alpha \geq 0.6$, $\sigma \approx 4{-}6$).
This finding may not generalize to scenarios with \emph{accurate} priors: when
warmup data closely matches the deployment distribution, adaptive exploration
schedules (e.g., $\alpha_t = \alpha_0/\sqrt{t}$) may improve sample efficiency
by reducing unnecessary exploration.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 3: Strategy Selection Trade-offs
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Strategy Selection Trade-offs.}
Corralling provides a modest advantage at low corruption (regret
48.0 at $\alpha{=}0$ vs.\ 51.3 for tabula rasa), but when priors are severely
misspecified ($\alpha \geq 0.6$), tabula rasa is the safer choice (regret
51.3 vs.\ 54--59 for Corralling).

The value of Corralling lies primarily in its advantage over warmup-only:
Corralling consistently outperforms warmup-only across all corruption levels
(e.g., 48.0 vs.\ 51.6 at $\alpha{=}0$; 58.9 vs.\ 67.7 at $\alpha{=}1.0$),
a 7--13\% regret reduction.
Warmup-only is \emph{never} the best strategy at any corruption level and
should be migrated to Corralling in all cases.  Tabula rasa provides a
safe, prior-independent baseline (regret ${\sim}51.3$) for deployments where
prior quality is truly unknown.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 4: Portfolio Scaling Trade-offs
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Portfolio Scaling Trade-offs.}
The $K$-scaling experiments (Figure~5) reveal that larger portfolios offer
smoother cost--quality frontiers and provider-diversity resilience, but at the
cost of slower convergence and reduced gap closure.
Hybrid LinUCB with family sharing partially mitigates convergence challenges at larger $K$

However, the data-driven family assignment (tetrachoric correlation,
$r_{\text{tet}} \geq 0.6$) requires all candidate models to have been
evaluated on a shared prompt set---practical for offline analysis but not for
runtime family reassignment when new models arrive without overlap data.

\emph{Implication:}
Practitioners with very large portfolios ($K > 3$) should expect longer
burn-in periods and should ensure complementary model strengths rather than
overlapping capabilities, as the latter inflates the action space without
expanding the contextual ceiling.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 5: Exploration Overhead at Quality Ceiling
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Exploration Overhead at the Quality Ceiling.}
banditGPT's peak quality at $K{=}3$ \textbf{TODO: update with K=3 peak quality} falls
below the best static model (GPT-4.1).
The constant exploration coefficient ($\alpha{=}2.0$) in the warmup expert is
necessary for distribution-shift detection but imposes a quality tax in
stationary, model-dominant regimes.
The architectural ablation
(Appendix~\ref{appendix:extended_results}, Table~\ref{tab:arch_ablation})
confirms this trade-off: plain LinUCB achieves 0.923 at $\lambda{=}0$ while
banditGPT-Hybrid achieves 0.901---but at moderate cost penalties
($\lambda{=}0.2$), banditGPT-Hybrid \emph{outperforms} LinUCB
(0.888 vs.\ 0.861), validating the Corralling overhead where cost-quality
trade-offs matter.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 6: Mechanism Validation Through Ablation
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Mechanism Validation Through Ablation.}
Our ablation study (Appendix~\ref{appendix:mathematical_foundations},
Table~\ref{tab:corralling_ablation}) validated that the system's robustness
in the Figure~3 mismatch scenario stems from \emph{constant} exploration
($\alpha{=}2.0$ for both experts) rather than \emph{heterogeneous} exploration
schedules.
Homogeneous constant exploration outperforms mixed schedules by 6.3\%.
The architectural ablation further shows that context features are the dominant
contributor to routing quality (+10.4\% over the non-contextual baseline),
while UCB exploration adds +0.7\% over $\epsilon$-greedy and warmup priors
contribute no additional benefit after full burn-in (1,121 steps completely
overwhelm $\neff{=}10$).

Note that the ablation grid covers 15 configurations with 3 seeds each
(Remark~\ref{remark:ablation_scope}); broader grids may reveal additional
interactions.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 7: Linear Reward Assumption
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Linear Reward Assumption.}
LinUCB models $E[r \mid x, a] = x^\top \theta_a$---a linear relationship
between PCA-compressed prompt embeddings and reward.
Non-linear reward surfaces (e.g., model performance that varies non-linearly
with prompt complexity, or interaction effects between embedding dimensions)
are not captured.
The empirical regret exponent of 0.669 $\pm$ 0.002 (vs.\ theoretical 0.5)
from the log-log regression analysis
(Appendix~\ref{appendix:mathematical_foundations}) suggests approximate
rather than exact linear realizability.
Additionally, isotropic regularization ($A_0 = \lambda I$) in PCA space treats
all principal components equally, which over-shrinks low-variance components.
Anisotropic or variance-matched regularization is left as future work.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 8: Outcome Variance and Reproducibility
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Outcome Variance and Reproducibility.}
Multi-seed validation reveals substantial variance in expert weight evolution:
final warmup weight is 0.382 $\pm$ 0.471 (standard deviation exceeds mean).
This high variance indicates that outcomes are seed-dependent and influenced
by the stochastic ordering of requests.
Practitioners cannot rely on deterministic predictions (e.g., ``weight will be
0.2 after 200 requests'') and must instead monitor their specific deployment's
weight trajectory.

This variance also has implications for experimental methodology: single-seed
evaluations are insufficient for characterizing bandit algorithm performance.
Our main experiments use 20 seeds per configuration (50 seeds for Figure~4).
The ablation grid (Table~\ref{tab:corralling_ablation}) uses 3 seeds per cell;
while it provides directional evidence, variance-sensitive conclusions
(e.g., precise configuration rankings) would benefit from larger sample sizes.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 9: Regime-Dependent Behavior
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Regime-Dependent Behavior.}
Multi-seed validation reveals two distinct operating regimes: a
warmup-dominant regime (approximately 33\% of data orderings) where Corralling
maintains the semantic transfer expert, and a tabula rasa-dominant regime
(approximately 67\%) where it abandons semantic transfer in favor of cold-start
exploration.
This regime dependence is a feature of Corralling's adaptive capacity: when
warmup priors match the deployment distribution, hyperparameter choice
($\neff$) significantly affects performance; when priors mismatch, Corralling
correctly detects over-confidence and switches strategies, rendering
$\neff$ choice less consequential.

The hyperparameter sensitivity analysis
(Appendix~\ref{appendix:extended_results}) confirms remarkable robustness:
rewards vary less than 0.5\% across a 50$\times$ range of $\eta$
(0.1--5.0) and less than 0.2\% across an 8$\times$ range of $\alpha_{\text{start}}$ (0.5--4.0).
The specific regime frequencies observed here reflect our LMSYS Arena test
distribution and may differ for other model portfolios or task distributions.

% Limitation 10: Catastrophic Failure Detection Is Secondary
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Catastrophic Failure Detection as a Secondary Benefit.}
The catastrophic failure experiment
(Appendix~\ref{appendix:extended_results}) demonstrates that
banditGPT provides automatic failover when a model degrades catastrophically.
The formal meta-learner detection rate is low at $K{=}3$; banditGPT's actual response operates primarily through
per-arm UCB updates within each expert, which implicitly reduce traffic to
the failed model.
This is a secondary safety benefit, not the primary design objective.

detection mechanism.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 11: Engineering Gaps
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Engineering and Persistence Gaps.}
While the router adds negligible latency ($<$1ms) and modest memory overhead
(2$\times$ for maintaining dual experts), two engineering gaps remain:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Corralling state not persisted:}
    The \texttt{save\_state}/\texttt{load\_state} API persists per-arm
    $A/b$ matrices but not the Corralling meta-weights or expert state.
    On restart, the meta-learner resets to uniform weights and experts lose
    online learning progress.
    For long-lived deployments, this means the system re-learns the warmup
    vs.\ tabula rasa preference from scratch after each restart.
    \item \textbf{Embedding pipeline coupling:}
    The feature pipeline (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 $\to$ PCA) is a coupled pair:
    replacing the encoder requires retraining PCA.
    Different model portfolios may benefit from different PCA bases, but the
    current architecture trains PCA once on 80K RouteLLM battles and applies
    it to all portfolios.
    Integrating a learned embedding layer (as in PILOT) is a natural
    extension.
\end{enumerate}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 12: Reward Signal Dependency
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Reward Signal Dependency.}
All routing decisions ultimately depend on an LLM judge (GPT-4o) that
produces the training signal.
The ternary reward scale (\{0.0, 0.5, 1.0\}) cannot express preference
magnitude, and single-judge evaluation masks within-prompt uncertainty.
Judge biases---preference for verbose outputs, position bias, cultural
biases---propagate through the reward signal into the router's learned policy
(Appendix~\ref{appendix:reward_signal}).

The framework is reward-agnostic by design: operators can replace the LLM
judge with human evaluations, A/B test outcomes, or an ensemble of judges
without changing the routing algorithm.
This modularity partially mitigates judge dependency but does not eliminate it
during the learning phase.
Additionally, a minor judge mismatch exists between the warmup priors
(GPT-4 judge from RouteLLM) and the online evaluation (GPT-4o judge), though
this is attenuated by $\neff{=}10$ normalization and Corralling's adaptive
weighting.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Limitation 13: Burn-In and Cold-Start Requirements
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Burn-In and Data Requirements.}
Learning curve analysis
(Appendix~\ref{appendix:extended_results}) shows that cold-start
priors alone achieve 0.839 holdout reward (above random at 0.814 and static
Mixtral at 0.823), confirming out-of-box deployability.
However, reaching competitive quality requires online adaptation:
approximately 400 prompts for the 95\% CI lower bound to surpass RouteLLM-MF's peak of 0.883,
and 500--900 prompts for convergence ($0.897$--$0.905$), with diminishing
returns beyond 500 steps.
For newly registered models, semantic bootstrapping plus confidence reset
achieves full calibration within approximately 600 routing decisions
(Appendix~\ref{appendix:extended_results}).

These requirements are modest for high-traffic deployments but may be
constraining for low-volume applications (e.g., internal tools with
$<$100 queries/day), where the burn-in period could span days.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Generalizability
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Generalizability.}
Our evaluation spans a single dataset (LMSYS Arena) but tests portfolio sizes
from $K{=}2$ (Llama-3.1-8B, GPT-4.1) to $K{=}3$
(Llama-3.1-8B, Gemini-2.5-Flash, GPT-4.1), providing breadth across
portfolio configurations.
Statistical significance is strong: Cohen's $d{=}1.64$ vs.\ RouteLLM-MF
with bootstrap 95\% CI [0.896, 0.906] entirely excluding the baseline.

However, the evaluation operates on a single domain (chatbot arena
conversations) with one mismatch pattern (warmup $\to$ holdout distribution
shift) and one embedding pipeline (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 + PCA).
Additional validation on diverse domains (e.g., code generation,
summarization, multilingual), alternative embedding architectures, and
non-LMSYS reward sources would strengthen generalizability claims.
The PCA space trained on 80K battles captures 35.14\% of embedding variance;
while the remaining 65\% appears to be routing-irrelevant variation, this has
been validated only for the model pairs present in the RouteLLM dataset.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Summary
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Despite these limitations, our core contributions remain robust:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Cost-aware routing is the primary value:}
    banditGPT excels when cost--quality trade-offs matter ($\lambda > 0$),
    delivering up to 89\% cost savings with $<$6\% quality loss, rather
    than as a pure quality maximizer where simpler methods suffice.
    \item \textbf{Corralling provides safety under prior uncertainty:}
    7--13\% regret reduction over warmup-only across all corruption levels
    and a formal $O(\sqrt{T})$ regret bound even under severe prior mismatch.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid family sharing enables portfolio scaling:}
    narrower confidence intervals at $K{=}3$ when per-model data is scarce
    \textbf{TODO: update with K=3 family sharing results}.
    \item \textbf{Zero-Shot Readiness:}
    Out-of-box deployment with warmup priors (0.839 reward) requires no
    online data and no hyperparameter tuning ($<$0.5\% sensitivity across
    50$\times$ parameter ranges).
    \item \textbf{Strategy selection based on validated prior quality}
    optimizes performance; rigorous ablation studies are essential for
    validating architectural hypotheses.
\end{enumerate}
These insights provide actionable guidance for practitioners deploying
adaptive LLM routing systems across the cost--quality spectrum.
