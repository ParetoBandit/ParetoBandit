\section{Reward Signal Robustness (Three-Judge Validation)}
\label{sec:judge_robustness}

All main-paper experiments use DeepSeek-R1 as the sole evaluator.
A natural concern is whether the paper's conclusions---the relative
ordering of methods and the qualitative behaviour of the router---are
artefacts of this particular judge.  We address this with a structured
three-judge validation designed to answer the reviewer's question
directly: \emph{would the paper's conclusions change with a different
evaluator?}

\subsection{Setup}

We draw a stratified random sample of $n = 2{,}000$ prompts from the full
12{,}000-prompt benchmark, preserving proportional representation of all
source benchmarks.  Each prompt has existing responses
from all three candidate models (Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-Large-2512,
Gemini-2.5-Pro), yielding $6{,}000$ (prompt, model) evaluation pairs.  We
re-evaluate the \emph{same responses}---no new model outputs are
generated---with two supplementary judges chosen for provider diversity:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{GPT-4.1-mini} (OpenAI),
    \item \textbf{Claude-3.7-Sonnet} (Anthropic).
\end{itemize}

\noindent All three judges receive the same base structured
chain-of-thought (CoT) evaluation prompt (Figure~\ref{fig:judge_prompt});
DeepSeek-R1 additionally receives a brevity instruction to control its
verbose chain-of-thought (see Figure~\ref{fig:judge_prompt} caption).
The composite reward is the weighted average
$r = 0.4\,s_{\text{reason}} + 0.3\,s_{\text{instr}} + 0.3\,s_{\text{comm}}$.
The judge panel spans three independent providers (DeepSeek, OpenAI,
Anthropic), none of which overlaps with the model providers being routed
(Meta, Mistral, Google).

\subsection{Population-Level Agreement}

The bandit's converged policy depends on the expected reward ordering
$\mathbb{E}[r(x, a)]$ across arms---not on individual per-prompt scores.
If the expected ordering is preserved across judges, the converged policy
is the same regardless of evaluator.

Table~\ref{tab:expected_ordering} shows that all three judges rank
the models identically: Gemini-Pro $>$ Mistral-Large $>$ Llama-8B.
Every pairwise expected reward difference is consistent in sign across
judges, with all nine Bonferroni-corrected bootstrap CIs excluding zero
(narrowest lower bound: \jrOrderingWorstCILo; 9 comparisons,
$B = 10{,}000$).  This invariance
extends beyond the marginal ordering: gap-conditioned Kendall's~$W$
(Table~\ref{tab:routing_diagnostics}) reaches 0.71 on prompts with large
inter-model gaps, providing evidence that the conditional ordering
$\mathbb{E}[r(x,a) \mid x]$ is also largely preserved in the regions
where routing decisions have the greatest impact.
Because all main-paper comparisons are within-judge (same R1 rewards,
same R1 oracle), judge choice can affect the \emph{magnitude} of regret
differences but not their sign.  R1's oracle lift
(\jrROneOracleLift{} per step) is the largest among the three judges
(\jrGPTOracleLift--\jrClaudeOracleLift{} for the supplementary judges),
so our main-paper effect sizes represent the most favourable case for
adaptive routing; all qualitative conclusions hold under every judge
tested.

\paragraph{Cross-judge routing regret.}
Even where per-prompt routing decisions differ, the practical cost is
small.  Following R1's oracle policy---selecting the model that R1 scores
highest on each prompt---and evaluating by the \emph{other} judges'
scores yields ${\geq}$\jrROneMinCapture\% of their own oracle reward
(Table~\ref{tab:cross_regret}).  The reverse is worse: following
GPT-4.1-mini's or Claude's oracle and evaluating by R1 captures only
\jrOtherOnROneMinCapture\%.  The asymmetry is partly mechanical: judges
with smaller inter-model gaps inflate capture fractions for \emph{any}
routing policy, while R1's larger gaps penalise misrouting more heavily.

\paragraph{Why not a panel average?}
A natural alternative is to average scores across the three-judge
panel~\cite{zheng2023judging}.  Panel averaging compresses the
routing margin from \jrROneMargin{} (R1 alone) to \jrPanelMargin{}
(\jrMarginCompressionPct\% compression), halving the fraction of
actionable prompts (31\% vs.\ 17\% at a 0.05 threshold).  Z-score
calibration~\cite{hedges1985statistical} and multi-judge aggregation
methods (PoLL~\cite{verga2024poll}, CARE~\cite{zhao2025care}) recovered
fewer than 3\,pp of this compression, confirming genuine inter-judge
disagreement rather than scale mismatch.  R1 alone provides the largest
margins and the lowest per-evaluation cost.

Kendall's~$W$ increases monotonically with gap size
(0.17\,$\to$\,0.71): where routing matters most (gap $\geq 0.20$,
37\% of prompts), all three judges largely agree ($W = 0.57$--$0.71$,
best-model agreement 53--58\%).  Where they disagree most
(gap $< 0.05$, 30\% of prompts), the models are near-identical in
quality, so routing the ``wrong'' model costs at most 0.05
reward---less than per-response judge noise
(MAD ${\approx}$ 0.075).  In short, disagreement is concentrated
precisely where it is cheap.

\subsection{End-to-End Regret Under Alternative Judges}
\label{sec:cross_judge_regret}

We now test whether the bandit's \emph{learning dynamics}---convergence
shape, sample complexity, and relative method ordering---are preserved
when the reward signal comes from a different judge entirely, by
re-running the bandit simulation under GPT-4.1-mini and
Claude-3.7-Sonnet scores.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We split the 2{,}000 judge-robustness prompts into a stratified
$\frac{1}{3}$~validation / $\frac{2}{3}$~test partition (672 / 1{,}328
prompts).  The bandit performs online learning during the validation burn-in
(no metrics), then cumulative regret is evaluated on the held-out test
split.  Crucially, we use \textbf{cold-start} (Tabula~Rasa) only---no
warmup priors---to eliminate any data-leakage concern between the
judge-robustness subset and the training set used for prior estimation.
This is deliberately the harder test: if the bandit converges from a cold
start, warm-start robustness follows.  We compare Tabula~Rasa against a
Random baseline (uniform $1/K$) under four budget regimes (unconstrained,
tight, moderate, loose), with \jrNseeds{} seeds per condition.
All three judges share the same hyperparameters
($\alpha = \jrTRAlpha$, $\gamma = \jrTRGamma$), tuned originally on R1;
reusing them for the supplementary judges is a deliberate choice---if
anything, judge-specific tuning would further improve their regret,
making this test conservative.

\paragraph{The bandit converges under all three judges.}
Figure~\ref{fig:cross_judge_regret} shows per-step cumulative regret under
unconstrained routing.  Under R1, Tabula~Rasa achieves a final cumulative
regret of $\jrROneUncTRRegret$ [$\jrROneUncTRRegretCILo$, $\jrROneUncTRRegretCIHi$]
(mean [95\% bootstrap CI]), compared to
$\jrROneUncRandRegret$ [$\jrROneUncRandRegretCILo$, $\jrROneUncRandRegretCIHi$]
for Random---a \textbf{\jrROneUncReductionPct\% reduction}.  Under
GPT-4.1-mini, the reduction is \textbf{\jrGPTUncReductionPct\%}
($\jrGPTUncTRRegret$ vs.\ $\jrGPTUncRandRegret$); under Claude, it is
\textbf{\jrClaudeUncReductionPct\%}
($\jrClaudeUncTRRegret$ vs.\ $\jrClaudeUncRandRegret$).
The similar reduction percentages
(\jrROneUncReductionPct\%/\jrGPTUncReductionPct\%/\jrClaudeUncReductionPct\%)
demonstrate that the bandit's learning effectiveness is preserved across
all three judges.  All curves are sublinear with decreasing slope,
consistent with the bandit progressively learning the reward structure
regardless of evaluator.

\paragraph{Effect sizes are compressed; learning dynamics are not.}
Absolute regret scales with inter-model margins: GPT-4.1-mini's Random
baseline (\jrGPTUncRandRegret) is \jrRandRegretCompressionPct\% lower
than R1's (\jrROneUncRandRegret), with Claude intermediate
(\jrClaudeUncRandRegret).  The supplementary/R1 regret ratios are
stable across budget regimes (GPT:
\jrUncGPTROneRatio\,[$\jrUncGPTROneRatioCILo$, $\jrUncGPTROneRatioCIHi$]
to \jrTightGPTROneRatio\,[$\jrTightGPTROneRatioCILo$, $\jrTightGPTROneRatioCIHi$];
Claude: \jrUncClaudeROneRatio\,[$\jrUncClaudeROneRatioCILo$,
$\jrUncClaudeROneRatioCIHi$] to \jrTightClaudeROneRatio\,[$\jrTightClaudeROneRatioCILo$, $\jrTightClaudeROneRatioCIHi$];
Figure~\ref{fig:cross_judge_regret}), confirming
proportional compression rather than a structural change in learning
dynamics.  Tabula~Rasa CIs are wider than the Random baseline's, reflecting
higher per-seed variance from cold-start exploration; despite this,
the paired difference (Random~$-$~Tabula~Rasa) is significant for
all three judges.  Under budget constraints, the qualitative
pattern is identical: tighter budgets force more regret,
and loose-budget regret approaches the unconstrained level.

\subsection{Summary}

Three independent judges unanimously agree on the expected reward
ordering (Table~\ref{tab:expected_ordering}), R1's oracle captures
${\geq}$\jrROneMinCapture\% of other judges' oracle reward
(Table~\ref{tab:cross_regret}), and the bandit's learning dynamics
replicate under both supplementary judges
(Figure~\ref{fig:cross_judge_regret}), with regret reductions of
\jrROneUncReductionPct\%/\jrGPTUncReductionPct\%/\jrClaudeUncReductionPct\%
(R1/GPT/Claude).
R1 is therefore well-justified as the sole evaluator: it provides the
largest inter-model margins and the lowest per-evaluation cost.  Correlating LLM-as-judge scores with human
ground-truth preferences remains an open direction orthogonal to the
consistency validated here.
