\section{Reward Signal Robustness (Three-Judge Validation)}
\label{sec:judge_robustness}

All main-paper experiments use DeepSeek-R1 as the sole evaluator.
A natural concern is whether the paper's conclusions---the relative
ordering of methods and the qualitative behaviour of the router---are
artefacts of this particular judge.  We address this with a structured
three-judge validation designed to answer the reviewer's question
directly: \emph{would the paper's conclusions change with a different
evaluator?}

\subsection{Setup}

We draw a stratified random sample of $n = 2{,}000$ prompts from the full
12{,}000-prompt benchmark, preserving proportional representation of all
source benchmarks and difficulty levels.  Each prompt has existing responses
from all three candidate models (Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-Large-2512,
Gemini-2.5-Pro), yielding $6{,}000$ (prompt, model) evaluation pairs.  We
re-evaluate the \emph{same responses}---no new model outputs are
generated---with two supplementary judges chosen for provider diversity:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{GPT-4.1-mini} (OpenAI),
    \item \textbf{Claude-3.7-Sonnet} (Anthropic).
\end{itemize}

\noindent All three judges receive the \emph{identical} structured
chain-of-thought (CoT) evaluation prompt.  The prompt instructs the judge
to first produce a concise reasoning analysis of the candidate response,
then score three continuous dimensions on a $[0,1]$ scale: \emph{Reasoning
Quality} (weight 0.4), \emph{Instruction Following} (weight 0.3), and
\emph{Communication Quality} (weight 0.3).  The composite reward is the
weighted average
$r = 0.4\,s_{\text{reason}} + 0.3\,s_{\text{instr}} + 0.3\,s_{\text{comm}}$.
The judge panel spans three independent providers (DeepSeek, OpenAI,
Anthropic), none of which overlaps with the model providers being routed
(Meta, Mistral, Google).  The structured CoT prompt instructs each judge
to produce a reasoning analysis, then score three dimensions on $[0,1]$
with five-level anchor descriptions at 0.2 intervals.  The exact template
is available in the reproducibility code.

\subsection{All Judges Agree on the Expected Reward Ordering}

The bandit's converged policy depends on the expected reward ordering
$\mathbb{E}[r(x, a)]$ across arms---not on individual per-prompt scores.
If the expected ordering is preserved across judges, the converged policy
is the same regardless of evaluator.

Table~\ref{tab:expected_ordering} shows that all three judges rank
the models identically: Gemini-Pro $>$ Mistral-Large $>$ Llama-8B.
Every pairwise expected reward difference is consistent in sign across
judges and statistically significant ($p < 10^{-5}$).  This invariance
extends beyond the marginal ordering: gap-conditioned Kendall's~$W$
(Table~\ref{tab:routing_diagnostics}) reaches 0.71 on prompts with large
inter-model gaps, providing evidence that the conditional ordering
$\mathbb{E}[r(x,a) \mid x]$ is also largely preserved in the regions
where routing decisions have the greatest impact.

\subsection{R1's Oracle Captures 97.5\% of Other Judges' Oracle Reward}

Even where per-prompt routing decisions differ, the practical cost is
small.  Following R1's oracle policy---selecting the model that R1 scores
highest on each prompt---and evaluating by the \emph{other} judges'
scores yields 97.5\% of their own oracle reward
(Table~\ref{tab:cross_regret}).  The reverse is worse: following
GPT-4.1-mini's or Claude's oracle and evaluating by R1 captures only
95.8--95.9\%.

\paragraph{Interpreting the asymmetry.}
Part of this asymmetry is mechanical: judges with smaller inter-model
gaps (GPT-4.1-mini, Claude) impose lower penalties for ``wrong'' routing,
so \emph{any} routing policy appears to capture a high fraction of their
oracle reward.  Conversely, R1 produces the largest gaps and therefore
penalises misrouted prompts more, explaining why the reverse direction
(other judges' oracle evaluated by R1) is lower.  The 97.5\% figure is
therefore a composite of R1's routing quality \emph{and} the
supplementary judges' reduced discriminability.  Nevertheless, R1's
off-diagonal capture fractions are the highest in every column of
Table~\ref{tab:cross_regret}, indicating that R1's routing decisions
are genuinely more consensus-compatible, not merely benefiting from a
scale effect.

\subsection{The Paper's Claims Are About Relative Method Performance}

The main paper compares ParetoBandit against baselines (Tabula~Rasa, Random,
static policies) using cumulative regret---a \emph{relative} measure.
These comparisons are conducted within a single judge: all methods receive
the same R1 rewards and are evaluated against the same R1 oracle.  Judge
choice therefore cannot affect the relative ordering of methods; it can
only affect the \emph{magnitude} of regret differences.

The effect sizes we report are large relative to judge noise.
For reference, the warmup ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:warmup_ablation})
shows a cumulative regret of \waUncTRRegret{} for Tabula~Rasa vs.\
\waUncWarmupRegret{} for ParetoBandit over \waNPrompts{} steps
(\waUncRegretReductionPct\% reduction).
R1's oracle lift over the best-fixed policy is 0.031 per step; under
GPT-4.1-mini the same quantity is 0.016, and under Claude it is 0.020.
Using a different judge would therefore compress absolute effect sizes
by up to ${\sim}50\%$, but the qualitative conclusions---ParetoBandit
outperforms Tabula~Rasa, budget pacing converges, drift adaptation
recovers quality---depend on the expected reward ordering
(Section~\ref{sec:judge_robustness}, Table~\ref{tab:expected_ordering}),
which is invariant across judges.

\subsection{Why R1 Rather Than a Panel Average}

A natural alternative is to average scores across the three-judge
panel~\cite{zheng2023judging}.  Panel averaging compresses the
\emph{routing margin}---the gap between the best and second-best model
on a given prompt---from \jrROneMargin{} (R1 alone) to \jrPanelMargin{}
(\textbf{\jrMarginCompressionPct\% compression}), with \jrFracCompressed\%
of prompts losing margin.  At a margin
threshold of 0.05, R1 provides 31\% actionable prompts vs.\ only 17\%
for the panel---nearly \textbf{half as many informative observations}.
The signal-to-noise ratio (mean margin / std) is similar (\jrROneSNR{} vs.\
\jrPanelSNR), so averaging compresses noise in proportion to signal; the
issue is not degraded SNR but smaller absolute gaps, which directly
increase per-step regret for UCB-style bandits.

The compression is driven by GPT-4.1-mini, whose inter-model gaps are
37\% smaller than R1's; when averaged with R1 and Claude, its compressed
signal pulls the margin toward indifference on prompts where the other
judges see a clear winner.
We also evaluated z-score calibration~\cite{hedges1985statistical} and
recent multi-judge aggregation methods
(PoLL~\cite{verga2024poll}, CARE~\cite{zhao2025care}); z-score
calibration recovered fewer than 3 percentage points of the 30\%
compression, confirming that the reduction reflects genuine inter-judge
disagreement in model preferences, not scale mismatch.

Because R1 sees the largest oracle lift (0.031 per step vs.\
0.016--0.020 for the supplementary judges), our main-paper results
present the most favourable case for adaptive routing.  This appendix
fully characterises the degree to which effect sizes depend on judge
choice.

\subsection{Per-Response Agreement Metrics}

For completeness, Table~\ref{tab:judge_agreement} reports per-response
agreement statistics.  We use Lin's
CCC~\cite{lin1989concordance}---the standard method-comparison
measure---and MAD (mean absolute deviation) rather than Pearson~$r$,
because the reward distribution is ceiling-compressed (most scores in
$[0.8, 1.0]$) and discretised by the rubric's weighted components, both
of which attenuate Pearson.  Bland--Altman analysis confirms that
disagreement does not vary systematically with score level (mean bias
$+\jrGPTBias$ for GPT-4.1-mini, $\jrClaudeBias$ for Claude; neither exhibits
proportional bias).

\subsection{Gap-Conditioned Concordance}

Per-prompt best-model agreement is ${\sim}50\%$---the judges disagree on
which model to route to for roughly half of all prompts.  However, this
disagreement is concentrated where inter-model quality differences are
negligible.

On the 736 prompts (37\%) where R1 sees a gap $\geq 0.20$---where
the routing decision meaningfully affects reward---Kendall's $W$
averages 0.57--0.71 and best-model agreement with R1 reaches
53--58\%.  On the 603 prompts (30\%) where the gap is $< 0.05$,
$W$ drops to 0.17, but routing the ``wrong'' model on these prompts
costs at most 0.05 reward---less than the typical per-response judge
noise (MAD ${\approx}$ 0.075).

Best-model agreement dips in the $[0.05, 0.10)$ bin because R1 expresses
a weak preference that the other judges may not share, while near-zero-gap
prompts inflate agreement through shared biases on effectively tied outputs.
Kendall's~$W$ increases monotonically across all bins
(0.17\,$\to$\,0.71), confirming that full-ranking concordance improves
steadily with gap size.
Population-level model selection frequencies are consistent across
judges, confirming that per-prompt disagreement does not propagate to
the aggregate routing policy: all three judges route 33--35\% of
prompts to Llama-8B, 39--42\% to Mistral-Large, and 23--28\% to
Gemini-Pro ($L_1$ distance from R1 $\leq 0.10$).

\subsection{End-to-End Regret Under an Alternative Judge}
\label{sec:cross_judge_regret}

The preceding analysis establishes that judges agree on the expected reward
ordering and that cross-judge routing regret is small.  A remaining question
is whether the bandit's \emph{learning dynamics}---convergence shape, sample
complexity, and relative method ordering---are preserved when the reward
signal comes from a different judge entirely.  We address this by re-running
the bandit simulation under GPT-4.1-mini scores on the same prompts,
comparing directly against R1.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We split the 2{,}000 judge-robustness prompts into a stratified
$\frac{1}{3}$~validation / $\frac{2}{3}$~test partition (672 / 1{,}328
prompts).  The bandit performs online learning during the validation burn-in
(no metrics), then cumulative regret is evaluated on the held-out test
split.  Crucially, we use \textbf{cold-start} (Tabula~Rasa) only---no
warmup priors---to eliminate any data-leakage concern between the
judge-robustness subset and the training set used for prior estimation.
This is deliberately the harder test: if the bandit converges from a cold
start, warm-start robustness follows.  We compare Tabula~Rasa against a
Random baseline (uniform $1/K$) under four budget regimes (unconstrained,
tight, moderate, loose), with 20 seeds per condition.

\paragraph{The bandit converges under both judges.}
Figure~\ref{fig:cross_judge_regret} shows per-step cumulative regret under
unconstrained routing.  Under R1, Tabula~Rasa achieves a final cumulative
regret of $\jrROneUncTRRegret$ [$\jrROneUncTRRegretCILo$, $\jrROneUncTRRegretCIHi$]
(mean [95\% bootstrap CI]), compared to
$\jrROneUncRandRegret$ [$\jrROneUncRandRegretCILo$, $\jrROneUncRandRegretCIHi$]
for Random---a \textbf{\jrROneUncReductionPct\% reduction}.  Under
GPT-4.1-mini, Tabula~Rasa achieves $\jrGPTUncTRRegret$ [$\jrGPTUncTRRegretCILo$,
$\jrGPTUncTRRegretCIHi$] vs.\ $\jrGPTUncRandRegret$
[$\jrGPTUncRandRegretCILo$, $\jrGPTUncRandRegretCIHi$] for Random---a
\textbf{\jrGPTUncReductionPct\% reduction}.  The
similar reduction percentages (\jrROneUncReductionPct\% vs.\ \jrGPTUncReductionPct\%)
demonstrate that the bandit's learning effectiveness is preserved across judges.  Both curves are
sublinear with decreasing slope, indicating that the bandit progressively
learns the reward structure regardless of evaluator.

\paragraph{Effect sizes are compressed; learning dynamics are not.}
The Random baseline's cumulative regret under GPT-4.1-mini (\jrGPTUncRandRegret)
is 32\% lower than under R1 (\jrROneUncRandRegret), consistent with
GPT-4.1-mini's smaller inter-model margins.  The GPT/R1 regret ratio is
stable across budget regimes (\jrUncGPTROneRatio\,[$\jrUncGPTROneRatioCILo$, $\jrUncGPTROneRatioCIHi$] to \jrTightGPTROneRatio\,[$\jrTightGPTROneRatioCILo$, $\jrTightGPTROneRatioCIHi$];
Figure~\ref{fig:cross_judge_regret}), confirming
proportional compression rather than a structural change in learning
dynamics.  The wide Tabula~Rasa CIs reflect the bimodal cold-start
distribution documented in Appendix~\ref{sec:warmup_ablation}; despite
wide marginal CIs, the paired difference (Random~$-$~Tabula~Rasa) is
significant for both judges.  Under budget constraints, the qualitative
pattern is identical across judges: tighter budgets force more regret,
and loose-budget regret approaches the unconstrained level.

\paragraph{Why these results justify R1 as the sole judge.}
R1 provides the largest inter-model margins (faster convergence, greater
statistical power) and is substantially cheaper per evaluation than
GPT-4.1-mini.  This experiment demonstrates that the paper's
conclusions---method rankings, convergence behaviour, budget-pacing
dynamics---are not artefacts of R1's scoring; the findings generalise
beyond the choice of evaluator.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}

This analysis validates reward \emph{consistency} across judges, not
reward \emph{accuracy} against ground-truth human preferences.  All three
judges share the same rubric and may exhibit correlated blind spots.
Per-prompt best-model agreement of ${\sim}50\%$ is an honest limitation
of the LLM-as-judge paradigm, but this noise does not compromise the
paper's conclusions: the expected ordering is invariant, cross-judge
routing regret is bounded (${\geq}97\%$ oracle capture), and learning
dynamics replicate under GPT-4.1-mini
(Figure~\ref{fig:cross_judge_regret}).  Because R1 sees the largest
oracle lift (0.031 vs.\ 0.016--0.020), our effect sizes represent an
upper bound; the relative method ordering is preserved under all judges.
