\subsection{Reward Signal Robustness (Three-Judge Validation)}
\label{sec:judge_robustness}

All main-paper experiments use DeepSeek-R1 as the sole evaluator.
A natural concern is whether the paper's conclusions---the relative
ordering of methods and the qualitative behaviour of the router---are
artefacts of this particular judge.  We address this with a structured
three-judge validation designed to answer the reviewer's question
directly: \emph{would the paper's conclusions change with a different
evaluator?}

\subsubsection{Setup}

We draw a stratified random sample of $n = 2{,}000$ prompts from the full
12{,}000-prompt benchmark, preserving proportional representation of all
source benchmarks and difficulty levels.  Each prompt has existing responses
from all three candidate models (Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-Large-2512,
Gemini-2.5-Pro), yielding $6{,}000$ (prompt, model) evaluation pairs.  We
re-evaluate the \emph{same responses}---no new model outputs are
generated---with two supplementary judges chosen for provider diversity:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{GPT-4.1-mini} (OpenAI),
    \item \textbf{Claude-3.7-Sonnet} (Anthropic).
\end{itemize}

\noindent All three judges receive the \emph{identical} structured
chain-of-thought (CoT) evaluation prompt.  The prompt instructs the judge
to first produce a concise reasoning analysis of the candidate response,
then score three continuous dimensions on a $[0,1]$ scale: \emph{Reasoning
Quality} (weight 0.4), \emph{Instruction Following} (weight 0.3), and
\emph{Communication Quality} (weight 0.3).  The composite reward is the
weighted average
$r = 0.4\,s_{\text{reason}} + 0.3\,s_{\text{instr}} + 0.3\,s_{\text{comm}}$.
The judge panel spans three independent providers (DeepSeek, OpenAI,
Anthropic), none of which overlaps with the model providers being routed
(Meta, Mistral, Google).  The full prompt template is reproduced below for
reproducibility.

\begin{quote}
\small\ttfamily
You are a Discriminative Router Judge.\ Your goal is to evaluate how well
an LLM response addresses the given prompt.

\medskip
Score on three continuous dimensions (0.0--1.0).\ Use the FULL range; do
NOT default to 0 or 1.

\medskip
1.\ \textbf{Reasoning Quality (40\,\%)} --- How sound is the reasoning?\\
\hspace*{1em}0.9--1.0\; Flawless; every step correct and clearly justified.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.7--0.8\; Sound overall; minor inefficiency or a trivial error
that does not change the conclusion.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.5--0.6\; Partially correct; approach is reasonable but
important steps are wrong or missing.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.3--0.4\; Weak; only fragments of correct logic.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.0--0.2\; No coherent reasoning, or completely wrong approach.\\
If the prompt needs no multi-step reasoning, score factual accuracy and
depth of explanation.

\medskip
2.\ \textbf{Instruction Following (30\,\%)} --- Were all explicit and
implicit constraints satisfied?\\
\hspace*{1em}0.9--1.0\; Every constraint followed precisely.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.7--0.8\; All major constraints met; one minor instruction
partially missed.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.5--0.6\; Some important instructions missed or only
partially addressed.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.3--0.4\; Multiple instructions ignored or misinterpreted.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.0--0.2\; Response largely ignores the prompt's requirements.

\medskip
3.\ \textbf{Communication Quality (30\,\%)} --- How clear,
well-structured, and useful is the response?\\
\hspace*{1em}0.9--1.0\; Exceptionally clear, well-organized, appropriate
detail.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.7--0.8\; Clear and competent; minor improvements possible.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.5--0.6\; Adequate but noticeably unclear, verbose, or
poorly organized.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.3--0.4\; Hard to follow; significant clarity issues.\\
\hspace*{1em}0.0--0.2\; Unintelligible, unhelpful, or inappropriate tone.

\medskip
Format your response EXACTLY as follows:

\medskip
\#\# Reasoning\\
<Concise chain-of-thought analysis>

\medskip
\#\# Reasoning Quality\\
<0.0 to 1.0>

\medskip
\#\# Instruction Following\\
<0.0 to 1.0>

\medskip
\#\# Communication Quality\\
<0.0 to 1.0>
\end{quote}

\subsubsection{All Judges Agree on the Expected Reward Ordering}

The bandit's converged policy depends on the expected reward ordering
$\mathbb{E}[r(x, a)]$ across arms---not on individual per-prompt scores.
If the expected ordering is preserved across judges, the converged policy
is the same regardless of evaluator.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Expected reward ordering under each judge.  All three judges
produce the identical ranking Gemini-Pro $>$ Mistral-Large $>$ Llama-8B,
with every pairwise difference significant at $p < 10^{-5}$ (paired
$t$-test, $n = 2{,}000$).}
\label{tab:expected_ordering}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Judge} & \textbf{Gemini-Pro} & \textbf{Mistral-Large} & \textbf{Llama-8B} \\
\midrule
DeepSeek-R1       & 0.935 & 0.923 & 0.799 \\
GPT-4.1-mini      & 0.964 & 0.952 & 0.857 \\
Claude-3.7-Sonnet & 0.928 & 0.915 & 0.779 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:expected_ordering} shows that all three judges rank
the models identically: Gemini-Pro $>$ Mistral-Large $>$ Llama-8B.
Every pairwise expected reward difference is consistent in sign across
judges and statistically significant ($p < 10^{-5}$).  This invariance
extends beyond the marginal ordering: gap-conditioned Kendall's~$W$
(Table~\ref{tab:routing_diagnostics}) reaches 0.71 on prompts with large
inter-model gaps, providing evidence that the conditional ordering
$\mathbb{E}[r(x,a) \mid x]$ is also largely preserved in the regions
where routing decisions have the greatest impact.

\subsubsection{R1's Oracle Captures 97.5\% of Other Judges' Oracle Reward}

Even where per-prompt routing decisions differ, the practical cost is
small.  Following R1's oracle policy---selecting the model that R1 scores
highest on each prompt---and evaluating by the \emph{other} judges'
scores yields 97.5\% of their own oracle reward
(Table~\ref{tab:cross_regret}).  The reverse is worse: following
GPT-4.1-mini's or Claude's oracle and evaluating by R1 captures only
95.8--95.9\%.  This asymmetry means R1's routing decisions are not merely
defensible but are the most consensus-compatible of the three judges.

\subsubsection{The Paper's Claims Are About Relative Method Performance}

The main paper compares ParetoBandit against baselines (Tabula~Rasa, Random,
static policies) using cumulative regret---a \emph{relative} measure.
These comparisons are conducted within a single judge: all methods receive
the same R1 rewards and are evaluated against the same R1 oracle.  Judge
choice therefore cannot affect the relative ordering of methods; it can
only affect the \emph{magnitude} of regret differences.

The effect sizes we report are large relative to judge noise.
For reference, the warmup ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:warmup_ablation})
shows a regret gap of 32.1 between ParetoBandit and Tabula~Rasa over
1{,}785 steps, corresponding to a per-step advantage of 0.018.
R1's oracle lift over the best-fixed policy is 0.031 per step; under
GPT-4.1-mini the same quantity is 0.016, and under Claude it is 0.020.
Using a different judge would therefore compress absolute effect sizes
by up to ${\sim}50\%$, but the qualitative conclusions---ParetoBandit
outperforms Tabula~Rasa, budget pacing converges, drift adaptation
recovers quality---depend on the expected reward ordering
(Section~\ref{sec:judge_robustness}, Table~\ref{tab:expected_ordering}),
which is invariant across judges.

\subsubsection{Why R1 Rather Than a Panel Average}

A natural alternative to using a single judge is to average scores across
the three-judge panel, which is standard practice for reducing evaluator
variance~\cite{zheng2023judging}.  We show concretely that this would
\emph{harm} the bandit's ability to learn routing preferences.

\paragraph{Panel averaging compresses the routing margin.}
The \emph{routing margin}---the gap between the best and second-best model
on a given prompt---is the signal the bandit must detect to make the
correct routing decision.  Under R1 alone, the mean routing margin is
0.049; under the three-judge panel average, it drops to 0.034---a
\textbf{30\% compression} (Figure~\ref{fig:panel_signal}a).  This
compression is not uniform: 63\% of prompts have a smaller gap under
averaging, and only 34\% have a larger gap.

\paragraph{Averaging halves the number of actionable prompts.}
Define an ``actionable'' prompt as one where the routing margin exceeds a
threshold---i.e., the bandit has enough signal to distinguish between the
best and second-best arm.  At a margin threshold of 0.05, R1 provides
31\% actionable prompts; the panel average provides only 17\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:panel_signal}b).  The bandit has nearly
\textbf{half as many informative observations} to learn from.  At a
threshold of 0.02, the gap is 54\% (R1) vs.\ 41\% (panel)---a loss of
13~percentage points.

\paragraph{The mechanism.}
GPT-4.1-mini's inter-model gaps are 37\% smaller than R1's.  When its
scores are averaged with R1 and Claude, the compressed GPT-4.1-mini
signal pulls the mean toward indifference.  On prompts where R1 and
Claude see a clear winner but GPT-4.1-mini sees models as nearly tied,
the panel average dilutes the margin below the bandit's detection
threshold.  This is the worst-case failure mode: the prompts where
averaging \emph{most} compresses the signal are exactly the marginal
prompts where routing adds the most value.

\paragraph{R1 is also the most consensus-compatible.}
Beyond signal strength, R1's oracle routing decisions capture
97.4--97.5\% of the other judges' oracle reward---the highest
off-diagonal fraction in Table~\ref{tab:cross_regret}.  The reverse
direction is worse (95.8--95.9\%), confirming that R1's per-prompt
decisions are not merely sharper but are also more aligned with the
consensus than either alternative judge's decisions.

\paragraph{Why not a more sophisticated aggregation?}
Recent multi-judge aggregation methods---PoLL~\cite{verga2024poll}
(diverse-panel averaging) and CARE~\cite{zhao2025care}
(confounder-aware tensor decomposition)---target biases from shared
confounders such as verbosity or style.  In our setting the three judges
already use an identical rubric and come from independent providers,
so confounder-induced correlation is minimal; the residual disagreement
is genuine and irreducible.  We additionally evaluated z-score
calibration~\cite{hedges1985statistical} (location-scale standardisation
before averaging), which recovered fewer than 3 percentage points of the
30\% margin compression, confirming that the signal loss is caused by
inter-judge disagreement, not scale mismatch.

\paragraph{Transparency.}
Because R1 sees the largest oracle lift from contextual routing (0.031
per step vs.\ 0.016--0.020 for the supplementary judges), our main-paper
results present the most favourable case for the value of adaptive
routing.  We consider this an appropriate trade-off: the mechanism is
demonstrated under conditions that maximise its visibility, while this
appendix fully characterises the extent of agreement with alternative
evaluators and the degree to which effect sizes depend on judge choice.

\subsubsection{Per-Response Agreement Metrics}

For completeness, Table~\ref{tab:judge_agreement} reports per-response
agreement statistics.  We use Lin's
CCC~\cite{lin1989concordance}---the standard method-comparison
measure---and MAD (mean absolute deviation) rather than Pearson~$r$,
because the reward distribution is ceiling-compressed (most scores in
$[0.8, 1.0]$) and discretised by the rubric's weighted components, both
of which attenuate Pearson.  Bland--Altman plots
(Figure~\ref{fig:judge_ba}) confirm that disagreement does not vary
systematically with score level.

CCC values of 0.65--0.72 reflect moderate per-response agreement.

substantial noise.  The key finding is not that the judges agree on every
response, but that their disagreement is structured in a way that preserves
the paper's conclusions: the expected ordering is invariant, the routing
regret is bounded, and the population-level policy is stable.

\subsubsection{Scope and Limitations}

This analysis validates reward \emph{consistency} across judges, not
reward \emph{accuracy} against ground-truth human preferences.  All three
judges share the same rubric and may exhibit correlated blind spots.  The
per-prompt best-model agreement of ${\sim}50\%$ is an honest limitation
of the LLM-as-judge paradigm: individual reward assignments are noisy.
We have shown that this noise does not compromise the paper's
conclusions, because the expected ordering is invariant, the cross-judge
routing regret is small and bounded, and R1's decisions capture
$\geq 97\%$ of any alternative judge's oracle reward.

We also note that R1 sees the largest contextual-routing benefit (oracle
lift 0.031 vs.\ 0.016--0.020), so our main-paper effect sizes represent
an upper bound on the absolute advantage of adaptive routing.  The
relative ordering of methods (ParetoBandit $>$ Tabula~Rasa $>$ Random) is
preserved under all judges, but the magnitude of improvement would be
smaller under a less discriminative evaluator.
