\subsection{Impact of Hard Per-Request Constraints}
\label{app:constraint_impact}

A core architectural feature of banditGPT is Layer~1 constraint filtering (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), which prunes models violating user-specified cost ceilings or latency limits \emph{before} the bandit selects.
This appendix validates that the mechanism (i)~produces graceful quality degradation as constraints tighten, (ii)~preserves the bandit's advantage over static baselines within the feasible set, and (iii)~interacts constructively with the soft cost penalty~$\lambda$.

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup}

We use the $K{=}10$ portfolio (Section~\ref{sec:multimodel}) spanning a $520\times$ per-request cost range (\$0.000025--\$0.013).
All conditions use the full Corralling architecture with warmup priors, 533-prompt online learning, and 750-prompt holdout evaluation---identical to the primary multi-model experiments.
Unless otherwise noted, constraints are active during \emph{both} training and evaluation, reflecting production deployment.
We report mean $\pm$ 95\% CI over $n{=}50$ independent seeds.

\paragraph{Latency metadata.}
Per-model time-to-first-token (TTFT) values for Mixtral-8x7B (0.62\,s) and GPT-4-Turbo (0.59\,s) are measured from 100-sample benchmarks stored in the model registry.
TTFT values for the remaining eight models are estimates based on provider documentation and public OpenRouter benchmarks; they are used to demonstrate the \emph{mechanism} of latency filtering rather than to claim precise latency characterization.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
For each constraint configuration, we report three baselines restricted to the same eligible set $K'$: a per-prompt \textbf{oracle} (upper bound), the \textbf{best eligible static model} (always route to the single best model in $K'$), and \textbf{uniform random} routing over $K'$.
This ensures comparisons with the bandit are fair---all methods face the same feasible set.

We evaluate along five dimensions: (A)~cost ceiling sweep, (B)~latency ceiling sweep, (C)~combined production scenarios, (D)~constrained Pareto frontiers, and (E)~an ablation separating constrained training from constrained evaluation.

% ============================================================================
\subsubsection{Results}
% ============================================================================

\paragraph{Constraint enforcement.}
By construction, Layer~1 filtering removes ineligible models before the bandit selects, so constraint satisfaction is guaranteed for any threshold that admits at least one model.
We verify this empirically: across all conditions ($1{,}650{,}000$ routing decisions), the violation counter is exactly zero, confirming the implementation matches the specification.

\paragraph{Graceful degradation under cost constraints.}
Figure~\ref{fig:constraint_impact}a shows mean holdout reward as a function of the per-request cost ceiling.
As the budget tightens from unconstrained ($K'{=}10$, $\bar{r}{=}0.895$) to the strictest threshold of \$0.0001/request ($K'{=}2$, $\bar{r}{=}0.885$), reward degrades by only $0.010$---a $1.1\%$ relative drop.
Even under the tightest budget, the bandit closes $68.9\%$ of the oracle--random gap within the two-model feasible set, demonstrating that contextual routing adds value even when the portfolio is severely constrained.
At moderate budgets ($K'{=}8$, cost $\leq$ \$0.005), the bandit achieves $\bar{r}{=}0.896$, \emph{matching} the unconstrained baseline.

\paragraph{Latency--quality trade-off.}
Figure~\ref{fig:constraint_impact}b reveals a similar pattern for latency constraints.
Restricting to sub-0.4\,s TTFT models ($K'{=}3$) yields $\bar{r}{=}0.887$, since several high-quality models (Gemini-2.5-Flash at 0.30\,s, Claude-Haiku-4.5 at 0.40\,s) satisfy strict latency requirements.
Relaxing the ceiling to 1.0\,s ($K'{=}9$) recovers $\bar{r}{=}0.899$, which exceeds the unconstrained mean.
This inversion occurs because the 1.0\,s ceiling excludes only DeepSeek-V3 (TTFT~1.10\,s), which has below-median quality; removing it slightly improves the average eligible model quality.

\paragraph{Hard constraints shift the Pareto frontier; $\lambda$ traces it.}
Figure~\ref{fig:constraint_impact}c overlays Pareto frontiers under three constraint regimes.
Hard constraints determine the \emph{feasible region} by excluding expensive models; the soft cost penalty $\lambda$ then traces the cost--quality trade-off within that region.
Under tight constraints ($K'{=}6$), the frontier is compressed leftward---realized cost ranges from \$0.00005 to \$0.00026---but the bandit still achieves $\bar{r}{=}0.889$ at $\lambda{=}0$.
Under moderate constraints ($K'{=}8$), the frontier extends to higher costs (\$0.00097 at $\lambda{=}0$) and peak reward rises to $\bar{r}{=}0.896$.
At high $\lambda$ ($\geq 5.0$), all three regimes converge to the cheapest model, yielding $\bar{r} \approx 0.886$ at \$0.00005/request.
This demonstrates that hard constraints and soft penalties serve complementary roles: constraints enforce SLA compliance while $\lambda$ provides fine-grained economic control within the feasible set.

\paragraph{Production deployment scenarios.}
Table~\ref{tab:constraint_scenarios} summarises six realistic deployment profiles with combined cost and latency constraints.
The \emph{Premium-SLA} scenario (cost $\leq$ \$0.01, latency $\leq$ 1.0\,s, $K'{=}8$) achieves the highest reward ($\bar{r}{=}0.900$), demonstrating that moderate combined constraints can \emph{improve} over the unconstrained baseline ($\bar{r}{=}0.895$) by pruning low-quality models.
The \emph{Enterprise-SLA} scenario (cost $\leq$ \$0.005, latency $\leq$ 0.7\,s, $K'{=}7$) achieves $\bar{r}{=}0.896$ at roughly half the cost (\$0.0012 vs.\ \$0.0022), confirming that joint constraints can enforce budget and latency SLAs simultaneously with negligible quality loss.
Even the most restrictive profile (\emph{Budget-Micro}, $K'{=}5$) retains $98.6\%$ of unconstrained quality at $13\times$ lower cost.

\paragraph{Ablation: always-on vs.\ eval-only constraints.}
A natural question is whether applying constraints during training---which restricts the bandit's exploration to the feasible subset---hurts learning compared to training on the full portfolio.
We test this at cost $\leq$ \$0.0005 ($K'{=}6$) with paired 50-seed trials.
In the \emph{always-on} condition, constraints are active during both online learning and holdout evaluation.
In the \emph{eval-only} condition, the bandit trains unconstrained on all $K{=}10$ models, but holdout evaluation enforces the cost ceiling.
The always-on condition achieves $\bar{r}{=}0.889 \pm 0.003$; eval-only achieves $\bar{r}{=}0.887 \pm 0.004$.
A Wilcoxon signed-rank test finds no significant difference ($p{=}0.553$, Cohen's $d{=}0.08$).
This is a reassuring result for production deployment: constraining exploration to the feasible set during training does \emph{not} sacrifice quality.
The bandit learns effective contextual policies within the restricted portfolio, and there is no benefit to training on models that will be unavailable at serving time.

\subsubsection{Discussion}
% ============================================================================

The results in this appendix validate hard per-request constraints as a practical and lossless mechanism for production LLM routing.
Three findings are particularly relevant for deployment:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Near-lossless constraint enforcement.}
  Tightening the cost ceiling by $520\times$ (from unconstrained to \$0.0001/request, $K'{=}2$) reduces reward by only $1.1\%$, while the bandit still closes $68.9\%$ of the oracle--random gap within the feasible set.
  For more realistic constraint levels ($K' \geq 5$), the quality loss is within the noise floor of the unconstrained baseline.

  \item \textbf{Complementary hard and soft controls.}
  Hard constraints and the $\lambda$ cost penalty are not redundant.
  Constraints enforce \emph{compliance} (no request exceeds a dollar or latency budget), while $\lambda$ provides continuous \emph{economic tuning} within the compliant set.
  Figure~\ref{fig:constraint_impact}c shows that at high $\lambda$, all constraint regimes converge, but at low $\lambda$ they diverge---confirming the two mechanisms control different aspects of routing behaviour.

  \item \textbf{No exploration penalty from constraints.}
  The ablation (always-on vs.\ eval-only, $p{=}0.553$) demonstrates that restricting exploration to the feasible set does not impair learning.
  This is practically important: operators can enable constraints from day one without a burn-in phase on the unconstrained portfolio.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these results support the abstract's claim that banditGPT enforces hard per-request budget and latency constraints while maintaining adaptive, near-optimal routing quality---a capability required for production SLA compliance that is absent from prior LLM routing frameworks.
