% Appendix: Prior Mismatch Sensitivity Analysis

\section{Prior Mismatch Sensitivity}
\label{appendix:prior_mismatch}

The warmup ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:warmup_ablation}) demonstrated
that well-calibrated priors substantially reduce regret and eliminate
catastrophic cold-start failures under stationary conditions.
All experiments in this paper use priors trained on a representative
training set drawn from the same distribution as the test traffic.
In practice, however, priors may be trained on stale
data, a different prompt mix, or an outdated model portfolio.
This experiment stress-tests warmup priors under progressively worse
calibration to quantify the cost of bad priors and identify when
tabula rasa is the safer choice.

\paragraph{Design.}
We construct five prior-quality levels forming a mismatch gradient:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Well-calibrated:} priors from the full training set
    ($n{=}8{,}373$)---the shipped default.
  \item \textbf{Random-1680:} a random subsample of 1,680 training
    prompts, matching the GSM8K-only count while preserving the full
    distribution.  This is a \emph{sample-size control}: any gap
    between Random-1680 and Well-calibrated isolates covariance
    estimation noise from domain effects.
  \item \textbf{MMLU-only:} priors from the 1,855 MMLU prompts only.
    The prior preserves the correct model ranking (Gemini~$>$~Mistral~$\gg$~Llama)
    but with knowledge-domain-specific magnitudes.
  \item \textbf{GSM8K-only:} priors from the 1,680 GSM8K (math) prompts only.
    All models score ${\sim}0.95{+}$ on math, so the prior encodes
    near-zero arm differentiation.
  \item \textbf{Inverted:} priors from the full training set with
    Llama and Gemini rewards swapped, so the prior believes the
    cheapest model is best and vice versa.
\end{enumerate}
Each level is tested at three $n_\mathit{eff}$ values (10, 100, 1000).
Two no-prior baselines are included:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Tabula Rasa} ($\gamma{=}\prmTRGamma$): the independently
    optimised production default (Experiment~05 Pareto knee-point
    sweep).
  \item \textbf{Tabula Rasa ($\gamma$-matched)} ($\gamma{=}\prmGamma$,
    $\alpha{=}\prmAlpha$): no priors, but the same forgetting factor as all
    warmup conditions.  This control isolates the prior's informational
    benefit from the different effective memory that $\gamma{=}\prmGamma$
    provides (${\sim}25\%$ shorter than $\gamma{=}\prmTRGamma$).
\end{itemize}
This yields $5 \times 3 + 2 = 17$ conditions.
All warmup conditions use $\alpha{=}\prmAlpha$, $\gamma{=}\prmGamma$ (disjoint
LinUCB).  The warmup hyperparameters were \emph{not} re-optimised per
prior-quality level---matching the production scenario where prior
quality degrades after deployment without hyperparameter adjustment.
Throughout, the $\gamma$-matched Tabula Rasa serves as the primary
controlled baseline; the production Tabula Rasa ($\gamma{=}\prmTRGamma$) is
reported for operational relevance.
Evaluation follows the same cumulative-regret protocol as the warmup
ablation: held-out test split ($n{=}1{,}824$), 20 seeds
(offset${=}9{,}000$, aligned for paired comparisons), unconstrained
(no budget pacer).
We deliberately restrict this experiment to the unconstrained regime
to isolate the effect of prior quality from the budget-pacer
interaction.
The warmup ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:warmup_ablation}) already
characterises the pacer interaction for well-calibrated priors vs.\
tabula rasa; adding budget constraints here would conflate two
failure modes (prior mismatch and pacer-induced catastrophic
failures) without additional insight, since the prior-quality
threshold for harm is determined by the bandit's learning dynamics,
not the pacer.

\paragraph{Statistical methodology.}
We use the same paired-comparison protocol as
Appendix~\ref{sec:warmup_ablation}: exact binomial sign test for
location shift, Fisher exact test on a $2{\times}2$
catastrophic-failure table for tail risk, with Holm--Bonferroni
correction across all 15 pairwise comparisons per baseline.
A seed is classified as catastrophic if its regret exceeds
$2{\times}$ the $\gamma$-matched Tabula Rasa's median.

\paragraph{Results.}
Figure~\ref{fig:prior_mismatch_heatmap} summarises total regret
across the quality--strength grid.

\emph{The forgetting-factor confound is negligible.}
The two Tabula Rasa baselines yield identical medians ($\prmTRMatchedRegretMedian$) and
nearly identical means ($\prmTRRegretMean$ vs.\ $\prmTRMatchedRegretMean$) with overlapping
per-seed distributions ($\mathit{std}{=}\prmTRRegretStd$ vs.\ $\prmTRMatchedRegretStd$).
The exact binomial sign test finds no significant difference
($p{=}\prmGammaSignP$, \prmGammaCondWins~condition wins, \prmGammaBaseWins~baseline wins, \prmGammaTies~ties out of
\prmNseeds~seeds).  The $\gamma$-matched baseline has \prmGammaCondCatCount/\prmNseeds{} catastrophic failures (\prmGammaCondCatPct\%) vs.\ \prmGammaBaseCatCount/\prmNseeds{} for the production Tabula Rasa (Fisher $p{=}\prmGammaFisherP$).
This confirms that the $\gamma{=}\prmGamma$ vs.\ $\gamma{=}\prmTRGamma$
difference has no material effect, and the comparisons below
reflect the prior's informational contribution rather than a
memory-length artefact.

\emph{Directionally correct priors help at every strength.}
Well-calibrated priors reduce median total regret monotonically with
$n_\mathit{eff}$: $\prmWellCalTenRegretMedian$ at $n_\mathit{eff}{=}10$,
$\prmWellCalHundredRegretMedian$ at $100$, and $\prmWellCalThousandRegretMedian$ at $1{,}000$
(vs.\ the $\gamma$-matched Tabula Rasa median of $\prmCatRefMedian$; reductions
of $\prmWellCalTenReductionPct\%$, $\prmWellCalHundredReductionPct\%$, and $\prmWellCalThousandReductionPct\%$).
At $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$, Well-calibrated wins \prmTestWellCalThousandCondWins{} of \prmNseeds{} seeds
(Holm-corrected sign $p{=}\prmTestWellCalThousandSignPHolm$);
Random-1680 wins \prmTestRandThousandCondWins{} of \prmNseeds{} (Holm $p{=}\prmTestRandThousandSignPHolm$).
At lower $n_\mathit{eff}$ values, the per-seed improvements are
directionally consistent but do not survive Holm correction with
20~seeds.

\emph{Sample size does not matter---distributional match does.}
The sample-size control (Random-1680) performs comparably to
Well-calibrated at every $n_\mathit{eff}$ (e.g., median $\prmRandThousandRegretMedian$
vs.\ $\prmWellCalThousandRegretMedian$ at $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$).
This confirms two things: (i)~the $n_\mathit{eff}$ scaling normalises
prior strength independently of training-set size, so the
domain-mismatch findings below are not confounded by sample-count
effects; and (ii)~covariance estimation saturates quickly---$1{,}680$
representative prompts are sufficient for $d{=}26$ features, and
collecting more data from the same distribution yields no additional
benefit.
The practical implication is that operators need a \emph{representative}
training sample, not a large one.

\emph{Domain-mismatched priors still beat cold start.}
MMLU-only priors reduce median regret to $\prmMMLUHundredRegretMedian$ at
$n_\mathit{eff}{=}100$ and $\prmMMLUThousandRegretMedian$ at $1{,}000$ (vs.\ baseline $\prmCatRefMedian$),
despite being trained on a single knowledge domain.
GSM8K-only priors---which encode near-zero arm differentiation---lower
median regret to $\prmGSMTenRegretMedian$--$\prmGSMThousandRegretMedian$ across $n_\mathit{eff}$ values.
After Holm correction, these reductions are directionally consistent
but individually non-significant at 20~seeds
(Holm-corrected $p \geq 0.15$ for all domain-mismatched conditions).
Crucially, no domain-mismatched prior \emph{increases} median regret
at any $n_\mathit{eff}$: partial knowledge is always at least as good
as no knowledge.

\emph{Inverted priors cause harm that scales with $n_\mathit{eff}$.}
This is the only condition where priors hurt.
At $n_\mathit{eff}{=}10$, the damage is modest
(median $\prmInvTenRegretMedian$ vs.\ baseline $\prmCatRefMedian$);
at $n_\mathit{eff}{=}100$, median regret rises to $\prmInvHundredRegretMedian$;
and at $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$, to $\prmInvThousandRegretMedian$---a $\prmInvThousandIncreasePct\%$ increase over
the baseline.
The mechanism is clear: the inverted prior confidently routes traffic
to the worst model, and high $n_\mathit{eff}$ forces the router to
accumulate many online observations before overriding the prior.
The baseline wins \prmTestInvThousandBaseWins{} of \prmNseeds{} seeds at $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$
(Holm $p{=}\prmTestInvThousandSignPHolm$, not significant at \prmNseeds~seeds), but the practical
magnitude ($+33$~regret units) is operationally meaningful.

\paragraph{Reliability: the more important finding.}
The mean and median comparisons above tell only half the story.
Figure~\ref{fig:prior_mismatch_distribution} reveals the other half:
both Tabula Rasa baselines exhibit wide, heavy-tailed per-seed regret
distributions ($\mathit{std} \approx \prmTRRegretStd$--$\prmTRMatchedRegretStd$), while \emph{every}
warmup condition---including domain-mismatched and even inverted
priors---exhibits a tight, unimodal distribution.
Non-inverted conditions have $\mathit{std} \leq 3.8$ (range:
$1.5$--$3.8$); even the worst condition (Inverted at
$n_\mathit{eff}{=}10$) achieves $\mathit{std}{=}\prmInvTenRegretStd$.
The $\gamma$-matched baseline's $\mathit{std}{=}\prmTRMatchedRegretStd$ confirms that
this variance collapse is attributable to the prior itself, not to
the longer effective memory of $\gamma{=}\prmGamma$: the control has the
same forgetting factor but the same heavy-tailed distribution as
production Tabula Rasa.

Catastrophic failures (regret $> 2{\times}$ the $\gamma$-matched
baseline median, i.e., $> \prmCatThreshold$) occur in \prmGammaCondCatCount/\prmNseeds{} seeds (\prmGammaCondCatPct\%)
for the $\gamma$-matched Tabula Rasa baseline and in \emph{zero} seeds for all
15~warmup conditions.
After Holm correction across 15~comparisons, individual Fisher exact tests are
non-significant ($p_\text{Holm}{=}\prmTestWellCalTenFisherPHolm$).
The aggregate pattern, however, is striking: the probability
that all 15 independent conditions would show zero catastrophic
failures if the true rate were \prmGammaCondCatPct\% is
$(1{-}\prmGammaCondCatPct/100)^{15 \times \prmNseeds} \approx 10^{-38}$---strong evidence that
the prior confers a genuine reliability benefit regardless of
calibration quality.

This reliability guarantee is arguably more deployment-relevant
than the regret reduction.
The warmup ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:warmup_ablation}) shows that
under budget constraints, Tabula Rasa's heavy tail becomes
catastrophic: 40\% of seeds fail at moderate and loose budgets
($p_\text{Fisher}{=}0.002$), while warmup priors eliminate
this failure mode entirely.
The present experiment extends that finding: the reliability
guarantee is \emph{robust to prior degradation}.
Operators who can only collect 1,680 prompts from a roughly
representative distribution, or who must reuse priors from a
related domain, retain the same variance reduction as those
with a perfectly calibrated 8K-prompt training set.

\paragraph{Practical implications.}
The prior-quality threshold for harm is high: priors must be
\emph{actively wrong} (inverting model rankings) before they degrade
performance.  The reliability guarantee---tight, unimodal distributions
vs.\ Tabula Rasa's heavy tail---is robust across the entire quality
gradient.
The $n_\mathit{eff}$ parameter serves as a safety knob:
$1{,}000$ for representative priors ($\prmWellCalThousandReductionPct\%$ regret
reduction), $100$ as a safe default for domain-shifted priors
($\prmWellCalHundredReductionPct\%$ reduction), and $10$ when quality is
entirely uncertain (worst-case damage limited to ${\sim}5\%$, while
non-adversarial priors still reduce regret by $\prmGSMTenReductionPct$--$\prmRandTenReductionPct\%$).
Tabula rasa is appropriate only when the prior is known to be
systematically inverted.
