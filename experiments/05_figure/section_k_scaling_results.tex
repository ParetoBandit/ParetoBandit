\subsubsection{Ablation: Sample Efficiency of Family Parameter Sharing}
\label{sec:k_scaling}

We isolate the contribution of Hybrid LinUCB's family sharing by comparing it against Disjoint LinUCB (independent models) at $K{\in}\{5, 10, 20\}$. Both policies start tabula rasa, training on 888 prompts. We use data-driven family assignments via within-provider tetrachoric correlation ($r_{\text{tet}} \geq 0.6$) to correct for binary reward base-rate attenuation, avoiding naive syntactic groupings (e.g., separating uncorrelated \texttt{gpt-4-turbo} and \texttt{gpt-4.1}).

\paragraph{Results \& Implications.}
Table~\ref{tab:k_scaling} and Figure~\ref{fig:k_scaling} show Hybrid LinUCB produces more stable convergence when per-model data is scarce. At $K{=}5$, Hybrid's 95\% CI is ${\sim}5\times$ narrower with a significant reward advantage ($p{=}0.028$). At larger $K$ the stability gap attenuates ($1.6\times$ at $K{=}10$; comparable at $K{=}20$), as $44$~obs/model suffice for Disjoint LinUCB to converge. 

Cumulative online reward differences are not significant at any $K$ ($p > 0.05$), confirming that Hybrid's advantage lies in variance reduction rather than mean improvement. For practitioners, this stability gain matters more than the mean gain: compressing the failure tail ensures predictable user experience. Furthermore, data-driven family assignment correctly groups models by empirical reward similarity rather than flawed syntactic naming conventions.
