\subsubsection{Sample Efficiency of Family Parameter Sharing}
\label{sec:k_scaling}

The two-model Pareto evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:pareto}) does not exercise Hybrid LinUCB's family sharing mechanism, because both models belong to different providers.
To isolate and measure this mechanism, we compare Hybrid LinUCB (with data-driven family assignments) against Disjoint LinUCB (each model independent) at three portfolio sizes: $K{=}5$, $10$, and $20$.

\paragraph{Experimental design.}
Both policies start tabula rasa (no warmup priors) to isolate the sharing effect.
Training uses 888 real prompts with judge-scored binary rewards; a held-out set of 750 prompts provides frozen evaluation at 25-step intervals.
All three $K$ configurations use the \emph{same} prompt set (the intersection of the $K{=}20$ model superset) to control dataset size across conditions.
Family assignments are determined by within-provider tetrachoric correlation on the holdout reward vectors ($r_{\text{tet}} \geq 0.6$, connected-components clustering), yielding families such as \{gpt-5, gpt-5.1, gpt-oss-120b, gpt-oss-20b\} and \{llama-3.1-405b, llama-3.1-70b, llama-3.1-8b, llama-3.2-1b\}.
Each condition runs 20 paired seeds; significance is assessed via paired $t$-tests.

\paragraph{Why tetrachoric correlation.}
Binary rewards induce a ceiling effect on Pearson~$r$ (the phi coefficient): two models both succeeding 98\% of the time can have $\phi_{\max} \ll 1$ even with perfectly correlated failure patterns.
The tetrachoric correlation estimates the latent continuous correlation underlying binary observations, correcting for base-rate attenuation~\cite{drasgow1986polychoric}.
For example, gpt-5.1 vs.\ o3 has $\phi{=}0.517$ but $r_{\text{tet}}{=}0.887$; gpt-4-turbo vs.\ gpt-4.1 has $\phi{=}{-}0.012$, $r_{\text{tet}}{=}{-}0.054$---confirming that the syntactic ``gpt-4'' family label would incorrectly group uncorrelated models.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:k_scaling} and Figure~\ref{fig:k_scaling} summarize the findings.
Three patterns emerge:

\textbf{(1)~Hybrid produces significantly more stable convergence.}
At $K{=}5$ and $K{=}20$, Hybrid's 95\% CI is $3\times$ narrower than Disjoint's ($\pm 0.006$ vs.\ $\pm 0.020$ at $K{=}5$; $\pm 0.007$ vs.\ $\pm 0.022$ at $K{=}20$).
This stability advantage arises because family-shared $\betavec_F$ parameters pool observations from all family members, providing a well-estimated baseline that per-model $\thetavec_m$ parameters refine.
When per-model data is scarce (44~obs/model at $K{=}20$), this pooling prevents the erratic convergence trajectories visible in Disjoint's wide confidence bands (Figure~\ref{fig:k_scaling}, bottom row).

\textbf{(2)~Hybrid achieves significantly higher holdout quality at $K{=}5$.}
The only statistically significant mean difference occurs at $K{=}5$ ($+0.029$, $p{=}0.008$), where Hybrid's single shared Llama family pools data across the 70B and 8B variants.
At $K{=}10$ and $K{=}20$, mean holdout quality is statistically indistinguishable ($p{>}0.3$), but Hybrid's tighter CIs mean it is more \emph{reliably} near the converged value across random seed variations.

\textbf{(3)~The stability benefit does not require a mean advantage to be valuable.}
At $K{=}20$, Hybrid and Disjoint achieve similar mean holdout quality ($0.959$ vs.\ $0.946$, $p{=}0.32$), yet Hybrid's CI is $3.1\times$ narrower.
In production, where a single deployment corresponds to a single seed, this means Hybrid is far less likely to land on a poor convergence trajectory.
The worst-case outcome---the lower bound of the 95\% CI---is $0.952$ for Hybrid vs.\ $0.924$ for Disjoint, a practically meaningful $+2.8$~pp floor improvement.

\paragraph{Family assignment matters.}
The data-driven approach (tetrachoric correlation within providers) produces different families from the syntactic heuristic (\texttt{infer\_model\_family}).
For example, the syntactic approach groups gpt-4-turbo and gpt-4.1 together (both strip to ``gpt-4''), but their tetrachoric correlation is $r_{\text{tet}}{=}{-}0.054$---uncorrelated.
Forcing them into the same family would cause the shared $\betavec$ to learn a compromise that helps neither model.
Conversely, gpt-5.1 and gpt-oss-120b ($r_{\text{tet}}{=}0.637$) are grouped despite different naming conventions, because their actual reward patterns are similar.

\paragraph{Practical implications for library users.}
For practitioners deploying banditGPT with $K{\geq}3$ models, the K-scaling experiment yields three actionable conclusions:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Family sharing is most valuable when it is least obvious.}
    The benefit is largest not when families are well-separated (where both policies learn quickly), but when per-model data is scarce---exactly the regime where practitioners are least confident in their routing decisions.
    At 44~observations per model ($K{=}20$), Hybrid's shared families ensure that the router converges to a stable policy rather than oscillating across seeds.

\item \textbf{The stability gain matters more than the mean gain.}
    Across three portfolio sizes, Hybrid's primary advantage is \emph{consistency}: $3\times$ narrower confidence intervals at $K{=}5$ and $K{=}20$.
    A deployment running Disjoint has a meaningful probability (${\sim}2.5\%$) of landing on a seed where holdout quality is $2{-}4$~pp below the mean; Hybrid compresses this tail.
    For a service routing thousands of requests per day, this consistency translates directly to predictable user experience.

\item \textbf{Data-driven family assignment avoids the pitfalls of syntactic grouping.}
    The \texttt{compute\_correlation\_families()} utility---which uses tetrachoric correlation within providers at a threshold of $r_{\text{tet}} {\geq} 0.6$---identifies families that reflect actual reward similarity rather than naming conventions.
    When reward data is unavailable (e.g., a newly registered model), the syntactic \texttt{infer\_model\_family()} heuristic provides a reasonable fallback.
    As online data accumulates, the family map can be refined to reflect observed reward structure.
\end{enumerate}
