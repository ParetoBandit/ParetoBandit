\paragraph{Setup.}
We evaluate \textit{banditGPT}'s model onboarding capability by
adding Mistral-Large-2512 to a pre-trained $K{=}2$ portfolio
(Llama-3.1-8B + Gemini-2.5-Pro).
Mistral is an ideal onboarding candidate: it achieves 99\% of
Gemini's reward ($0.923$ vs.\ $0.932$) at $1/29$th the cost
(\$0.00052/req vs.\ \$0.015/req), and it comes from a different
provider family (Mistral AI), so any knowledge transfer must work
across provider boundaries.

The router is first trained on the canonical training split
($n{=}8{,}374$) with $K{=}2$ arms using the best
configuration ($\alpha{=}0.05$, $\neff{=}50$, Hybrid LinUCB).
After training, Mistral is registered via
\texttt{register\_model(``mistral-large-2512'', speed=``balanced'')},
and the router processes the test split ($n{=}1{,}824$) with all
three arms.  We compare two conditions:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Hybrid LinUCB}: the shared $\beta$ learned during
          $K{=}2$ training transfers to the newcomer.
          Our staged warm-prior decomposition
          (\S\ref{sec:warm_priors}) sets
          $\hat{\beta} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_a A_a^{-1}b_a$ after
          calibration, so a new arm inherits the mean quality profile
          of the incumbents as its zero-shot prediction
          ($\hat{r} \approx 0.76$).
    \item \textbf{Disjoint LinUCB} (ablation): independent per-arm
          parameters; the newcomer's $A_a, b_a$ start from
          the T-shirt--size identity prior ($\hat{r} = 0$).
          Both conditions share identical $K{=}2$ warmup priors for
          the original arms---only the shared $\beta$ differs.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent All hyperparameters ($\alpha{=}0.05$, $\neff{=}50$,
$\lambda{=}0.20$) were val-tuned for the Hybrid policy and kept
fixed across conditions, matching the production scenario where
operators call \texttt{register\_model()} without re-tuning.
No artificial exploration boost is applied; the
experiment tests whether the shared $\beta$ alone is sufficient for
zero-shot onboarding at the production exploration rate.
The Corralling meta-learner uses an adaptive $\gamma$ schedule:
upon \texttt{register\_model()}, $\gamma$ drops from its
steady-state value (0.05) to~0 and ramps linearly back over 500
update steps.  This suppresses the uninformed tabula rasa expert
during the critical discovery window, giving the warm-started
expert full control when it matters most, then gradually restoring
the safety net as the newcomer's statistics stabilize.
Results are averaged over 20 seeds.

\paragraph{Hybrid LinUCB enables zero-shot onboarding.}
The top-left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:onboarding_k3} shows the
routing mix under Hybrid LinUCB.
Because the newcomer inherits $\hat{\beta}$, its UCB score is
competitive from the very first prompt.
Mistral's cumulative routing share reaches $52.0\%$ by the end of
the test horizon, while Gemini collapses to $17.2\%$---the router
autonomously discovers that Gemini is rarely worth $29{\times}$ more
than Mistral.
The full-sequence CostSave@95\% is $79.0 \pm 2.1\%$ and the
late-stage CostSave@95\% (last 500 prompts) reaches $83.9 \pm 2.0\%$
as the routing mix converges.

\paragraph{Disjoint LinUCB falls into a cold-start trap.}
Without the shared $\beta$ (top-right panel), the newcomer starts
with $\hat{r} = 0$.
At $\alpha{=}0.05$, the UCB exploration bonus
($\alpha\sqrt{x^\top A_a^{-1} x}$) is far too small to
overcome the incumbents' learned advantage, so Mistral is never
selected---its routing share remains at exactly $0\%$ for the
entire $1{,}824$-prompt test horizon across all 20 seeds.
This is not a convergence-speed issue; Disjoint LinUCB
\emph{cannot} discover Mistral without either a higher $\alpha$
(which degrades steady-state efficiency for the incumbents) or a
dedicated exploration protocol.
Its CostSave@95\% is only $36.3 \pm 0.7\%$, limited to savings
from routing easy prompts to Llama.

\paragraph{Autonomous redundancy detection.}
Under Hybrid LinUCB, the router not only adopts the newcomer
but autonomously identifies that Gemini-2.5-Pro has become
redundant.
Gemini's windowed routing share drops below $10\%$ within
approximately 735 prompts (displacement point: $735 \pm 173$).
This validates a practical production narrative:
\emph{``We added a mid-tier model via a one-line API call.
The router incorporated it without retraining and autonomously
stopped using the expensive frontier model.''}

\paragraph{Cumulative reward comparison.}
The bottom panel overlays the cumulative average reward for both
conditions.
Hybrid's reward rises after the onboarding point as Mistral
substitutes for costly Gemini selections while maintaining
comparable quality.
Disjoint's reward remains flat, unable to benefit from the new
arm it never selects.
During the $K{=}2$ pre-training phase, Disjoint shows a
marginally higher cumulative reward (${\sim}0.01$)---a consequence
of Hybrid's overparameterisation when $z = x$, where the shared
and arm-specific components compete for the same signal.
This small ``cost of transfer readiness'' is dwarfed by Hybrid's
post-onboarding gains: cumulative regret is $112.8 \pm 2.2$
for Hybrid vs.\ $132.3 \pm 2.4$ for Disjoint, and the transition
overhead (excess cost in the first 500 prompts) is $+139\%$ for
Disjoint relative to Hybrid.

\paragraph{The shared $\beta$ is the key mechanism.}
This experiment isolates parameter sharing as the sole
differentiator: both conditions use identical priors, costs,
exploration rates, and data streams.
The result demonstrates that the shared $\beta$ in Hybrid LinUCB
is not merely a speed advantage---it is \emph{necessary} for
practical cold-start onboarding at production exploration rates.
