\subsection{Non-stationary Adaptation (Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative_regret})}
\label{sec:nonstationarity}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
LLM providers routinely update their models: OpenAI's GPT-4 has
received multiple mid-cycle quality changes, Anthropic's Claude
models are refreshed quarterly, and open-weight models on inference
APIs (e.g., Together, Fireworks) may be swapped to newer
checkpoints without notice.
When a model's quality changes, a router trained on historical data
inherits a \emph{stale policy}: it continues routing to a model
whose quality has degraded.
We ask: \emph{can BanditGPT adapt to non-stationary environments
automatically, without manual re-calibration?}

\paragraph{Why this matters.}
Every organisation deploying an LLM router faces this problem.
A static routing policy---the dominant production pattern---becomes
stale whenever a provider updates a model, changes pricing, or
deprecates an endpoint.
The cost of staleness is invisible until it accumulates:
requests continue to be routed to a degraded model for days or
weeks until a human re-runs evaluation and re-deploys.
An adaptive router that detects and responds to quality changes
\emph{online}, without manual intervention, eliminates this
operational lag and the quality loss it incurs.
The experiment below quantifies the dollar cost of that lag
under a controlled model quality shift.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
BanditGPT addresses non-stationarity through a \emph{forgetting
factor} $\gamma \in (0, 1]$ applied to the disjoint
LinUCB~\cite{li2010contextual} sufficient statistics.
At each step $t$, before incorporating the new observation
$(x_t, a_t, r_t)$, the design matrix and reward accumulator for
each arm $a$ are discounted:
\begin{align}
    A_a &\leftarrow \gamma \, A_a + x_t x_t^\top
    \label{eq:forget_A} \\
    b_a &\leftarrow \gamma \, b_a + r_t \, x_t
    \label{eq:forget_b}
\end{align}
This exponential weighting, originally proposed for
switching bandits by Garivier and
Moulines~\cite{garivier2011switching}, gives observations an
effective half-life of $\ln 2 / (1 - \gamma)$ steps.
With $\gamma{=}0.995$, the effective memory window is
$\sim\!200$ steps: recent evidence dominates while historical
observations---including warmup priors---are geometrically
discounted.
The parameter $\gamma$ thus controls a
bias--variance tradeoff between \emph{stability} (trusting
historical evidence) and \emph{plasticity} (responding to new
evidence).

We evaluate this mechanism under a reward shift that captures
the dominant failure mode in production LLM routing: a model
quality change that invalidates the router's learned policy.

\subsubsection{Experiment 02: Model Quality Shift}
\label{sec:reward_shift}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We construct a controlled model quality shift using the $K{=}3$
portfolio on real benchmark data.
The router learns online on the validation split
($n{=}1{,}785$; 32~NLP benchmarks) and priors are trained on a
disjoint training split ($n_{\mathrm{eff}}{=}1{,}000$, $\alpha{=}0.1$;
selected via joint epsilon-constraint tuning,
Section~\ref{sec:hparam_sweep}).
The 1{,}785-step trajectory has two phases:

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Phase~1} (steps 1--893): Normal reward landscape.
    Warmup priors are well-calibrated.
    Mistral-Large is utility-best
    (mean utility $0.840 = 0.917 - 0.20 \times 0.384$).

  \item \textbf{Phase~2} (steps 894--1{,}785): \textbf{Reward swap}.
    Llama-8B and Mistral-Large exchange their per-prompt reward
    and cost columns, simulating simultaneous API quality changes.
    Post-swap, Llama-8B is utility-best
    ($0.922 = 0.922 - 0$) while Mistral-Large drops to worst
    ($0.727 = 0.804 - 0.077$).  Gemini-Pro is unchanged (anchor).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why these baselines.}
A convincing non-stationarity experiment must isolate the
contribution of each design decision.
We select four conditions that form an \emph{ablation ladder}---each
adds exactly one capability---plus an \emph{external baseline} from
the non-stationary bandit literature.
This structure lets the reader attribute regret differences to
specific mechanisms rather than to confounded design choices.

\noindent
Four conditions are compared across 40~seeds:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Fixed Policy (offline):}
    Warmup priors deployed without online learning.
    This is the dominant production pattern: train a router offline,
    deploy, and retrain quarterly.
    It establishes the \emph{cost of not adapting}---the baseline
    regret that any online method must improve upon to justify its
    operational complexity.

  \item \textbf{Naive Bandit ($\gamma{=}1.0$):}
    LinUCB with warmup priors and online learning, but
    infinite memory (no forgetting).
    This is the obvious first attempt at adaptive routing, and
    the approach taken by existing bandit-based LLM routers
    (PILOT~\cite{panda2025pilot}, BaRP~\cite{wang2025barp},
    LLM Bandit~\cite{li2025llmbandit}).
    It isolates the contribution of \emph{online learning alone},
    separate from forgetting.

  \item \textbf{SW-UCB ($W{=}200$):}
    Sliding-Window LinUCB~\cite{garivier2011switching} without
    warmup priors ($\alpha{=}0.01$).  Retains only the most
    recent $W$ observations with equal weighting; older
    observations receive zero weight.  Window size is matched to
    BanditGPT's effective memory (${\sim}200$ steps) for a
    controlled comparison of the forgetting mechanism.
    This is a well-studied non-stationary bandit algorithm with
    known minimax regret bounds~\cite{garivier2011switching}; its
    inclusion ensures we compare against a method specifically
    designed for changing environments, not just stationary
    baselines.

  \item \textbf{BanditGPT ($\gamma{=}0.995$):}
    Warmup priors with jointly-tuned geometric forgetting
    (effective memory ${\sim}200$ steps; $\gamma$ selected
    jointly with $\alpha$ and $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ via
    epsilon-constraint, Section~\ref{sec:hparam_sweep}).
    The proposed method combines all three ingredients:
    warmup priors (low cold-start regret), online learning
    (continuous adaptation), and geometric forgetting
    (non-stationary tracking).
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
Conditions 1, 2, and 4 share $\alpha{=}0.1$ and
$n_{\mathrm{eff}}{=}1{,}000$.
SW-UCB uses $\alpha{=}0.01$ (appropriate for a cold-start
policy without priors; the lower exploration rate avoids
excessive early regret).
The Naive Bandit's stationary-optimal configuration
($\alpha{=}0.01$, $n_{\mathrm{eff}}{=}1{,}000$;
Appendix~\ref{app:hparam_sweep}) would perform \emph{worse}
under the shift due to stronger prior inertia; the shared
hyperparameters represent a conservative comparison.

\paragraph{Regret definition.}
Cumulative regret is measured against the \emph{cost-adjusted oracle}:
at each step $t$, the oracle selects
$a^*_t = \arg\max_a [r_{a,t} - \lambda_s \tilde{c}_a]$
where $\lambda_s{=}0.20$ is the shared static cost penalty
and $\tilde{c}_a$ is the normalized cost.
Because all three conditions face the same cost penalty, this
oracle defines a common comparator.

\paragraph{Results (Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative_regret}).}
The four conditions produce clearly separated regret trajectories:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Condition & Phase~1 & Phase~2 & Total & $\sigma$ \\
\midrule
Fixed Policy (offline) & 46.6 & 169.1 & 215.6 & 0.0 \\
Naive Bandit ($\gamma{=}1.0$) & 56.1 & 97.1 & 153.2 & 9.1 \\
SW-UCB ($W{=}200$) & 100.1 & 32.5 & 132.7 & 34.4 \\
\textbf{BanditGPT ($\gamma{=}0.995$)} & 61.5 & \textbf{53.1} & \textbf{114.6} & \textbf{7.5} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The regret decomposes cleanly by phase.  Reading the table
column-by-column reveals \emph{why each design decision matters}:

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Fixed Policy $\to$ Naive Bandit ($-29\%$ total):}
    Online learning alone reduces total regret from $215.6$ to
    $153.2$.  The Naive Bandit adapts in Phase~2 (regret
    $97.1$ vs.\ $169.1$), but infinite memory means Phase~1
    inertia dilutes the new signal: the Naive Bandit accumulates
    $83\%$ more Phase~2 regret than BanditGPT.
    \textbf{For the practitioner:} this is the gap between
    ``we have online learning'' and ``we have online learning
    that actually works under change.''
    Every existing bandit-based LLM router
    (PILOT, BaRP, LLM Bandit) lives in this gap.

  \item \textbf{SW-UCB: the strongest non-stationary baseline.}
    SW-UCB~\cite{garivier2011switching} achieves the
    \emph{lowest Phase~2 regret} of any condition ($32.5$),
    demonstrating that a purpose-built non-stationary algorithm
    adapts faster than BanditGPT ($53.1$) once the shift occurs.
    However, without warmup priors SW-UCB pays a $2.1\times$
    higher Phase~1 cost ($100.1$ vs.\ $46.6$--$61.5$), and its
    cross-seed standard deviation ($\sigma{=}34.4$) is
    $4.6\times$ that of BanditGPT ($7.5$).

    \emph{Why is SW-UCB so costly in Phase~1?}
    Without priors, SW-UCB starts from $A{=}\lambda I$, $b{=}0$:
    it has no evidence to prefer any arm, so the UCB exploration
    bonus dominates early decisions and the policy samples arms
    near-uniformly.
    During the first ${\sim}200$ steps (one full window fill),
    a large fraction of traffic goes to suboptimal arms
    (e.g.\ Gemini-Pro, whose high cost penalty makes it the
    worst-utility arm), accumulating regret on every such
    decision.
    The prior-initialised conditions skip this phase entirely:
    their matrices encode offline evaluation data
    ($n_{\mathrm{eff}}{=}1{,}000$), so from step~1 they already
    know Mistral-Large is the utility-best arm and route
    ${\sim}78\%$ of traffic there.
    The lower $\alpha{=}0.01$ used for SW-UCB (vs.\ $0.1$ for
    prior conditions) partially mitigates the cold start---higher
    exploration would make it worse---but cannot close the gap.
    The $100.1$-unit Phase~1 regret is the irreducible price of
    having no historical knowledge.

    \textbf{For the practitioner:} SW-UCB is the better choice
    \emph{only if} you know the environment will shift and you
    can tolerate high variance; BanditGPT is preferable when
    the environment may or may not shift, because its warmup
    priors protect the common case (stability) while forgetting
    handles the uncommon case (drift).

  \item \textbf{Naive Bandit $\to$ BanditGPT ($-25\%$ total):}
    Adding geometric forgetting reduces regret from $153.2$ to
    $114.6$.  The gain is concentrated in Phase~2 ($53.1$ vs.\
    $97.1$, a $45\%$ reduction): the 200-step effective memory
    window discounts stale Phase~1 evidence, allowing BanditGPT
    to re-learn the new optimum within ${\sim}200$ steps of
    the swap.
    \textbf{For the practitioner:} forgetting is the single
    highest-leverage addition for production non-stationarity.
    It costs essentially nothing under stationarity
    (${<}0.3\%$ AUC loss; Section~\ref{sec:hparam_sweep})
    and provides a $25\%$ regret reduction when the environment
    changes.

  \item \textbf{Phase~1 is ``free'' with warmup priors.}
    Conditions with warmup priors (Fixed, Naive, BanditGPT)
    achieve similar Phase~1 regret ($46.6$--$61.5$), while
    SW-UCB (no priors) incurs $100.1$---a cold-start tax of
    ${\sim}40$--$54$ regret units.
    \textbf{For the practitioner:} if you have historical
    evaluation data (which most production deployments do),
    warmup priors eliminate the cold-start cost entirely.
    The combination of priors + forgetting gives the best of
    both worlds: low initial regret \emph{and} fast adaptation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The Fixed Policy failure mode.}
The Fixed Policy has zero cross-seed variance ($\sigma{=}0$)
because it routes deterministically from its frozen priors.
Without online feedback, it continues routing 78\%~Mistral-Large
and 21\%~Llama throughout the entire trajectory---even after
the swap makes Mistral the worst arm.
Its Phase~2 regret ($169.1$) accounts for 78\% of its total regret,
illustrating the cost of deploying a static router in a
non-stationary environment.

\paragraph{Deployment reliability.}
Beyond mean regret, the \emph{variance} across seeds reveals a
critical distinction.
BanditGPT ($\gamma{=}0.995$) has the lowest cross-seed standard
deviation among learning conditions ($\sigma{=}7.5$), comparable
to the Naive Bandit ($\sigma{=}9.1$) but far below SW-UCB
($\sigma{=}34.4$).
Geometric forgetting regularizes this variance by ensuring that
the effective sample size never grows beyond ${\sim}200$
observations, regardless of the data ordering.
A production team gets one deployment---BanditGPT's low variance
means every deployment reliably adapts.

\subsubsection{Statistical Significance}
\label{sec:nonstat_significance}

All conditions share the same prompt stream within each seed
(randomised identically), making paired comparisons appropriate.
We report two-sided paired $t$-tests (39~d.f.) on terminal
cumulative regret, with Holm--Bonferroni
correction~\cite{holm1979simple} for all pairwise comparisons
(6~tests for 4~conditions).

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrcl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Comparison} & \textbf{$\Delta$} & \textbf{95\% CI} & \textbf{$p$} \\
\midrule
BanditGPT vs.\ Fixed Policy   & $-101.0$ & $[-103.4, -98.6]$  & $< 10^{-45}$ \\
BanditGPT vs.\ Naive Bandit   & $-38.5$  & $[-42.2, -34.8]$   & $< 10^{-22}$ \\
BanditGPT vs.\ SW-UCB         & $-18.0$  & $[-29.5, -6.6]$    & $< 10^{-2}$  \\
SW-UCB vs.\ Naive Bandit      & $-20.5$  & $[-32.6, -8.4]$    & $< 10^{-2}$  \\
SW-UCB vs.\ Fixed Policy      & $-83.0$  & $[-94.1, -71.8]$   & $< 10^{-17}$ \\
Naive Bandit vs.\ Fixed Policy & $-62.5$ & $[-65.4, -59.5]$   & $< 10^{-33}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
All six pairwise comparisons remain significant after
Holm--Bonferroni correction ($p < 10^{-2}$).
The widest confidence interval is BanditGPT vs.\ SW-UCB
($[-29.5, -6.6]$), reflecting SW-UCB's high cross-seed
variance ($\sigma{=}34.4$); even so, the effect is significant
at $p < 0.003$.

\subsubsection{Synthesis}
\label{sec:nonstat_synthesis}

\paragraph{Takeaway.}
The four conditions form a \emph{capability ladder} that
isolates the contribution of each design decision:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Online learning} (Fixed $\to$ Naive):
    reduces total regret by $29\%$, but infinite memory limits
    adaptation speed ($p < 10^{-33}$).
    Every existing bandit-based LLM router operates at this
    rung.
  \item \textbf{Non-stationary bandit theory} (SW-UCB):
    achieves the lowest Phase~2 regret ($32.5$) but suffers
    a $100.1$-unit cold-start tax in Phase~1 and exhibits
    $4.6\times$ higher variance than BanditGPT.
    SW-UCB demonstrates that forgetting \emph{alone} is not
    enough---warmup priors are essential for reliable
    performance across both phases.
  \item \textbf{Geometric forgetting} (Naive $\to$ BanditGPT):
    reduces total regret by a further $25\%$ ($p < 10^{-22}$).
    This is the single mechanism that separates BanditGPT from
    all prior work, and its benefit is concentrated exactly where
    it matters: the post-shift Phase~2.
  \item \textbf{Warmup priors + forgetting} (BanditGPT):
    deliver the lowest total regret ($114.6$) \emph{and} the
    lowest variance ($\sigma{=}7.5$) of any learning condition.
    The combination provides the best of both worlds: fast
    Phase~1 convergence from priors and fast Phase~2 adaptation
    from forgetting.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why this ordering matters for practitioners.}
A production team deploying an LLM router faces a choice:
(a)~deploy a static policy and accept the cost of staleness
(Fixed Policy: $215.6$ regret);
(b)~add online learning and hope models don't change
(Naive Bandit: $153.2$ regret);
(c)~adopt a non-stationary algorithm but sacrifice cold-start
performance and accept high variance (SW-UCB: $132.7 \pm 34.4$);
or (d)~combine priors + forgetting for the best total regret
\emph{and} the lowest deployment risk (BanditGPT:
$114.6 \pm 7.5$).
The BanditGPT combination costs \emph{nothing} to add:
forgetting reduces stationary AUC by ${<}0.3\%$
(Section~\ref{sec:hparam_sweep}), warmup priors are a one-time
offline computation, and the $\gamma$ hyperparameter is selected
automatically by the $\epsilon$-constraint protocol.

\noindent
The jointly-tuned $\gamma{=}0.995$ (effective memory ${\sim}200$
steps) strikes the right balance: it forgets stale evidence
fast enough to track the new optimum while retaining enough
observations for confident exploitation.
An ablation studying the sensitivity to $\gamma$ (including
Tabula Rasa and Adaptive-$\gamma$ variants) is provided
in Appendix~\ref{app:forgetting_ablation}.
