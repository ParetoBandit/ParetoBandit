\subsection{Multi-Model Cost--Quality Trade-off}
\label{sec:multimodel}

The two-model Pareto evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:pareto}) validates banditGPT against a supervised baseline on a controlled topology.
However, the contextual bandit formulation is designed for arbitrary portfolio sizes, and a key architectural claim---that Dynamic Pareto Filtering, Hybrid LinUCB, and Corralling compose effectively at $K{\gg}2$---requires direct evaluation.
We construct two multi-provider portfolios ($K{=}5$ and $K{=}10$) and evaluate the full production router with dedicated warmup priors trained on 43-model evaluation data.

\subsubsection{Portfolio Construction}

We select models to maximise three axes of diversity: \emph{provider coverage} (Meta, Mistral, Google, Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepSeek), \emph{cost range} (\$0.05--\$30.00 per million output tokens, a $600\times$ spread), and \emph{quality range} (holdout reward 0.745--0.983).
The $K{=}5$ portfolio spans five providers with one model each; $K{=}10$ adds a second model per provider plus DeepSeek, exercising Hybrid LinUCB's within-family parameter sharing.
Both portfolios include Mixtral-8x7B, linking to the $K{=}2$ evaluation.

\subsubsection{Experimental Protocol}

Warmup priors are generated from 43-model evaluation data using a \textbf{three-way split} that preserves the existing holdout set (Section~\ref{sec:warmup_prior_construction}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Prior training:} 355 prompts $\times$ 43 models (15,265 observations) $\to$ warmup $\Amat$, $\mathbf{b}$ matrices.
    \item \textbf{Online learning:} 533 prompts (disjoint from prior training) for bandit updates.
    \item \textbf{Holdout:} 750 prompts (identical to Section~\ref{sec:pareto}) for frozen evaluation.
\end{itemize}
For each portfolio, we sweep the cost penalty $\lambda$ over 11 values from
0 to 5 (0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0) across
20 seeds.
Baselines include the Oracle (per-prompt best model), best static single model, random routing, and $\epsilon$-greedy ($\epsilon{=}0.1$).
A no-Corralling ablation isolates the meta-learner's contribution.

Four patterns emerge from the multi-model evaluation:

\paragraph{(1)~The Pareto frontier provides continuous cost--quality control.}
At $K{=}5$, sweeping $\lambda$ from 0 to 5 traces a smooth operating envelope from quality-maximising ($R{=}0.911$, $C{=}\$0.0028$/req) to cost-minimising ($R{=}0.708$, $C{=}\$0.00005$/req)---a $68\times$ cost range.
At $K{=}10$, the envelope is flatter: quality degrades only 1\% ($0.898 \to 0.888$) across a $41\times$ cost range, because the larger portfolio contains more cheap high-quality models (Gemma-3-27B, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-Flash) that the router can leverage at higher $\lambda$ values.
In both cases, a practitioner adjusts a \emph{single scalar parameter} ($\lambda$) rather than manually selecting among $K$ models or tuning $K{-}1$ routing thresholds.

\paragraph{(2)~Warmup priors eliminate cold-start at all portfolio sizes.}
At step~0---before any online learning---banditGPT achieves holdout quality of 0.898 ($K{=}5$) and 0.903 ($K{=}10$), already within 9\% of the Oracle.
This is a direct consequence of the 43-model warmup priors (Section~\ref{sec:warmup_prior_construction}), which encode per-model quality estimates from 355 training prompts into the $\Amat$ and $\mathbf{b}$ matrices.
The cold-start quality is \emph{higher} at $K{=}10$ than at $K{=}5$ because the larger portfolio provides more routing options---even with imperfect prior estimates, the probability that at least one model is well-calibrated for any given prompt increases with $K$.
For practitioners, this means that a newly deployed multi-model router delivers production-quality routing from its first request.

\paragraph{(3)~Contextual routing distributes traffic across the portfolio.}
The traffic allocation heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:multimodel_pareto}d) reveals that banditGPT does not simply select the best-on-average model for all prompts.
At $K{=}5$, Claude-Sonnet-4 receives 32\% of traffic, GPT-4.1 29\%, and Gemini-2.5-Flash 28\%---three models with different strengths share the load based on prompt context.
The two cheapest models (Llama-3.1-8B and Mixtral-8x7B) receive 6\% and 5\% respectively, confirming that the contextual features identify the minority of prompts where simpler models suffice.
At $K{=}10$, traffic distributes more evenly: no model exceeds 17\%, and all ten models receive measurable traffic.
This stands in contrast to $\epsilon$-greedy, which concentrates ${\sim}90\%$ of traffic on a single model, or random routing, which allocates uniformly regardless of prompt difficulty.
Contextual routing provides both quality gains and operational benefits: load distribution reduces single-provider dependency, and per-prompt model selection surfaces complementary model strengths that static routing cannot exploit.

\paragraph{(4)~The quality gap to the best static model reflects the exploration--exploitation trade-off at scale.}
At peak $\lambda$, banditGPT's quality (0.911 at $K{=}5$, 0.898 at $K{=}10$) is below the best static model (GPT-4.1 at 0.949).
This gap does not indicate a routing failure---it reflects a fundamental property of the bandit formulation.
The router maintains a constant exploration coefficient ($\alpha{=}2.0$) to detect distribution shifts (Section~\ref{sec:prior_degradation}), which means it occasionally selects sub-optimal models to verify that their quality estimates remain calibrated.
This exploration cost grows with $K$: at $K{=}2$, the router splits exploration between 2 arms; at $K{=}10$, exploration distributes across 10 arms, each receiving less exploitation traffic.
However, this exploration is not wasted---it is precisely what enables the router to build the contextual traffic allocation in (3) and to detect model degradation (Section~\ref{sec:robustness_adaptation}).
A static policy that always selects GPT-4.1 achieves 0.949 quality but at $\$0.0034$/req; the bandit at $\lambda{=}0.1$ achieves 0.890 at $\$0.0016$/req---comparable quality at less than half the cost.

\subsubsection{Discussion: Practical Value of Multi-Model Routing}

The multi-model evaluation clarifies when and why adaptive routing adds value beyond static model selection.

\paragraph{The single-$\lambda$ value proposition.}
In production, the strongest argument for banditGPT at $K{>}2$ is operational simplicity.
Without a router, a practitioner deploying $K{=}10$ models must either (a)~select a single model (forgoing the portfolio's diversity), (b)~build $K{-}1$ routing rules (which become stale as models update), or (c)~run a supervised router that requires labelled routing data for all $K$ models.
banditGPT replaces these choices with a single parameter: $\lambda$.
At $\lambda{=}0$, the system maximises quality.
At $\lambda{=}5$, it minimises cost.
At any intermediate value, it discovers the Pareto-optimal allocation automatically, adapting to the deployment distribution without labels or manual thresholds.

\paragraph{Cost efficiency at scale.}
The $K{=}10$ portfolio demonstrates a particularly favourable cost--quality surface.
At $\lambda{=}1.0$, banditGPT achieves $R{=}0.891$ at $C{=}\$0.00032$/req---91\% cheaper than static GPT-4.1 ($\$0.0034$/req) with only 6.1\% quality reduction.
This efficiency arises because the larger portfolio contains multiple cheap high-quality models (Gemma-3-27B at \$0.00005, DeepSeek-V3 at \$0.00047, Gemini-Flash at \$0.00026) that are near-optimal for many prompt types.
The router learns which prompts genuinely require expensive models and routes only those prompts to the premium tier.
For a deployment handling 100k requests/day at the $\lambda{=}1.0$ operating point, this translates to \$32/day vs.\ \$340/day for static GPT-4.1---a \$112k annual saving.

\paragraph{Resilience through portfolio diversity.}
A practical benefit of multi-model routing, distinct from the cost--quality Pareto analysis, is resilience to provider outages and model deprecations.
When traffic distributes across 6 providers (as in the $K{=}10$ allocation), a single-model outage affects at most 17\% of traffic, though a full provider outage could affect up to 31\% (Anthropic: Claude-Sonnet-4 at 17\% + Claude-Haiku-4.5 at 14\%).
The router's online learning automatically redistributes affected traffic to the remaining models within tens of interactions.
This contrasts with single-model deployments where a provider outage is a full service failure, or with rule-based failover where fallback ordering must be manually maintained.

\paragraph{Prior modularity: a practical pattern for deployment.}
The three-way split methodology (Section~\ref{sec:warmup_prior_construction}) demonstrates that warmup priors can be constructed from any source of model quality data---whether proprietary benchmarks, A/B test logs, or the evaluation framework used here.
The priors for the $K{=}2$ evaluation derive from 80k RouteLLM battles; the priors for $K{=}5$ and $K{=}10$ derive from 355 prompts of 43-model evaluation data.
Both follow the same format ($\Amat$, $\mathbf{b}$ matrices per model) and plug into the same prior-loading pipeline.
For practitioners, the message is direct: if you have any historical data about model performance on your task---even a few hundred labelled prompts---you can generate warmup priors that eliminate cold-start.
The prior source does not need to match the deployment exactly; Corralling's meta-learner handles the residual mismatch by maintaining a parallel tabula rasa expert that learns the deployment distribution from scratch.

\paragraph{Scaling behaviour.}
Comparing across $K$ reveals a consistent pattern: gap closure decreases modestly with portfolio size (74.9\% at $K{=}5$, 69.3\% at $K{=}10$), while the \emph{cost-efficiency} of the Pareto frontier improves.
The gap closure decrease is an expected consequence of larger action spaces: with more models, the bandit must explore more and the Oracle's per-prompt optimality becomes harder to approach.
However, the cost-efficiency improvement---the $K{=}10$ frontier degrades only 1\% in quality across a $41\times$ cost range, vs.\ 22\% across a $68\times$ range at $K{=}5$---demonstrates that larger portfolios provide a flatter, more favourable cost--quality surface.
In practice, the portfolio should be chosen to maximise the quality range across the cost tiers the deployment cares about, rather than to maximise $K$ indiscriminately.
