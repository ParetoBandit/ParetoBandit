\section{Theoretical Motivation (brief)}
\label{appendix:spectral_separation}

To provide theoretical context for our router's design, we discuss the relationship between the contextual heterogeneity in Figure~\ref{fig:contextual_heterogeneity} and the expected convergence behavior of the meta-algorithm.

In contextual bandit settings, error rates and regret depend on how well contexts separate arms with different expected rewards. Even when a single principal component explains a small fraction of embedding variance, it can still capture routing-relevant signal if it aligns with preference differences.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Slopes and Routing Margin}

The likelihood-ratio test in Section~\ref{sec:motivation} establishes that per-model contextual slopes $\gamma_m$ are heterogeneous ($\chi^2 = 771.3$, $\text{df}{=}54$, $p < 10^{-15}$). For any pair of models $(m_i, m_j)$, the expected reward difference at context $\xvec$ is $(\gamma_{m_i} - \gamma_{m_j}) \widetilde{\text{PC1}}(\xvec) + (\alpha_{m_i} - \alpha_{m_j})$. Since slopes differ by up to ${\sim}8\times$ (GPT-4-Turbo: $\gamma = -0.254$ vs.\ GPT-4.1: $\gamma = -0.032$), the optimal model changes across the PC1 axis. The decision boundaries between model pairs are well-separated for most of the feature space, allowing the router to achieve stable model selection.

\subsection{Expected Routing Regret}

Since PCA features correlate with model preference on held-out data---all ten models show significant contextual sensitivity (Figure~\ref{fig:contextual_heterogeneity}B)---the feature space provides meaningful signal for linear contextual bandits such as LinUCB to exploit. The multi-directional nature of the signal (different PCs predict different models) further supports the use of a full 32-component feature vector rather than PC1 alone. We treat this appendix as intuition rather than a formal guarantee, and rely on empirical validation in the main paper and supplementary material.
