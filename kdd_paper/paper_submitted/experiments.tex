\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate our framework through two research questions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Performance)}: Does Expert Distillation reduce cold-start regret?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Plasticity)}: Can the router adapt when priors become obsolete?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dataset and Models}

\paragraph{Prompt Corpus.} We use 497 archetypal prompts derived from LMSYS Chatbot Arena~\cite{zheng2023judging} via K-means clustering (\S\ref{sec:distillation}). For evaluation, we sample 2,000 prompts with replacement, simulating a realistic query stream.

\paragraph{Model Pool.} We evaluate 81 production LLMs accessed via OpenRouter, spanning:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Frontier models}: GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Gemini-2.0
    \item \textbf{Open-weight models}: Llama-3-70B, Mistral-Large, DeepSeek-V3
    \item \textbf{Cost-optimized models}: Nova-Lite, Nova-Micro, Llama-3.2-3B
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Ground Truth.} Model quality is measured via GPT-4o-as-judge scoring, normalized to $[0, 1]$. This oracle achieves $>80\%$ agreement with held-out human preferences on the Arena dataset.

\paragraph{Metrics.} We report:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Cumulative Regret}: $\sum_{t=1}^{T} (r^* - r_t)$ where $r^*$ is the optimal reward and $r_t$ is the achieved reward at step $t$.
    \item \textbf{Regret Reduction}: Percentage decrease vs.\ cold-start baseline.
    \item \textbf{Recovery Latency}: Steps required to correct erroneous priors.
\end{itemize}

\section{RQ1: Warm-Start Advantage}
\label{sec:rq1}

\textbf{Research Question}: Does shipping expert-distilled priors reduce cold-start regret compared to a naive bandit?

\subsection{Experimental Design}

We compare two agents over 2,000 routing decisions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Cold-Start Agent}: DisjointLinUCB initialized with identity matrices ($\mathbf{A}_m = \mathbf{I}$, $\mathbf{b}_m = \mathbf{0}$). Must learn everything from scratch through exploration.
    
    \item \textbf{Warm-Start Agent}: DisjointLinUCB initialized with expert-distilled priors, scaled by $\lambdaboost = 50$. Starts with ``day-one intelligence.''
\end{enumerate}

Both agents use identical hyperparameters: $\alpha = 0.5$ (exploration), 384-dimensional embeddings (all-MiniLM-L6-v2), and the same random seed for query ordering.

\subsection{Results}

Figure~\ref{fig:regret_curve} shows cumulative regret over the evaluation horizon.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/figure1_regret_curve.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{RQ1: The Shippable Brain Advantage.} Cumulative regret comparison between cold-start (no priors) and warm-start (expert-distilled priors with $\lambdaboost=50$) agents over 2,000 routing decisions. The warm-start agent achieves \textbf{63.6\% lower cumulative regret}.}
    \label{fig:regret_curve}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Key Findings.}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{63.6\% Regret Reduction}: The warm-start agent accumulates 63.6\% less regret than the cold-start baseline over the evaluation horizon.
    
    \item \textbf{Immediate Exploitation}: The warm-start agent makes intelligent routing decisions from step 1, while the cold-start agent wastes early queries on random exploration.
    
    \item \textbf{Widening Gap}: The regret gap is largest in the first 500 steps (when cold-start is exploring blindly), then stabilizes as both agents converge to local optima.
    
    \item \textbf{No Convergence Penalty}: The warm-start agent does not sacrifice long-term performance for short-term gains---it maintains its advantage throughout.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Ablation: Prior Strength.} We vary $\lambdaboost \in \{1, 10, 50, 100\}$ and find $\lambdaboost = 50$ optimal. Lower values under-exploit the priors; higher values over-suppress exploration, preventing adaptation to held-out query types.

\subsection{The Benchmark Trap: Why Leaderboards Fail}
\label{sec:benchmark_trap}

A natural question arises: \emph{Why not simply initialize priors using public benchmark scores?} Models ranked highly on MMLU, HumanEval, and MATH-500 should intuitively perform well on production traffic. We test this hypothesis with a \textbf{Benchmark-Initialized} baseline.

\paragraph{Experimental Design.} We initialize LinUCB priors proportional to a weighted combination of public benchmark scores (40\% MMLU-Pro, 30\% HumanEval, 30\% MATH-500). This simulates the ``obvious'' approach of trusting the leaderboard.

\paragraph{Results: Negative Transfer.}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering

\label{tab:benchmark_trap}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Initialization} & \textbf{Cumulative Regret} & \textbf{vs.\ Cold Start} \\
\midrule
Cold Start (Random) & 63.7 & --- \\
Benchmark-Initialized & 83.5 & $-31\%$ (worse) \\
\textbf{Expert-Distilled (Ours)} & \textbf{20.4} & \textbf{+68\%} (better) \\

\end{table}

Surprisingly, the Benchmark-Initialized policy performs \textbf{31\% worse} than random initialization (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_trap}). This ``Benchmark Trap'' reveals a critical insight:

\begin{quote}
\emph{The capability surface required for production tasks is orthogonal to academic benchmarks. Models that excel at MMLU/HumanEval may underperform on real user queries.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Diagnosis.} We identify a \textbf{Regime Mismatch}: benchmark-optimal models (o3, Claude-Opus-4.5) differ from production-optimal models (Nova-Lite, Claude-3.5-Sonnet). Public benchmarks measure isolated capabilities (math reasoning, code generation), while production traffic requires different skills (JSON formatting, conversational tone, cost efficiency).

\paragraph{Implication.} This result explicitly motivates Expert Distillation. Priors must be derived from the \emph{target distribution} (via teacher demonstration), not external proxies (leaderboard scores). The ``Shippable Brain'' succeeds precisely because it encodes \emph{production-specific} model preferences.

\section{RQ2: Plasticity Under Concept Drift}
\label{sec:rq2}

\textbf{Research Question}: Can the router adapt when its priors become obsolete? This addresses the \emph{Plasticity-Stability Dilemma}: strong priors improve exploitation but may resist correction when wrong.

\subsection{The ``Confidently Wrong'' Problem}

Consider a scenario where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item A legacy system was trained when GPT-4o was state-of-the-art for all tasks.
    \item A new specialist model (Nova-Lite) is released that dominates a specific niche.
    \item The priors \emph{confidently} believe GPT-4o is optimal (high $\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{GPT-4o}}\|$, low uncertainty).
\end{itemize}

The bandit faces a trap: its high confidence suppresses exploration, so it keeps routing to GPT-4o, receiving suboptimal rewards. Can it eventually discover the new specialist?

\subsection{Experimental Design: Poisoned Priors Simulation}

We construct a controlled experiment isolating this phenomenon:

\paragraph{Setup.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Two models}: GPT-4o (default) and Nova-Lite (specialist)
    \item \textbf{Ground truth}: Nova-Lite achieves $r = 0.95$ on a niche task; GPT-4o achieves only $r = 0.60$
    \item \textbf{Poisoned priors}: We inject ``confidently wrong'' priors claiming GPT-4o has expected reward 1.0 and Nova-Lite has expected reward 0.1
    \item \textbf{Memory decay}: We add exponential decay ($\gamma = 0.90$) to enable unlearning
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Expected Pattern (``Dip and Recover'').}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{The Dip} (Steps 0--200): Bandit trusts poisoned priors, routes to GPT-4o, receives suboptimal $r \approx 0.60$.
    \item \textbf{The Flip} (Steps 200--300): Accumulated bad rewards erode GPT-4o's estimated quality. Exploration bonus eventually triggers a trial of Nova-Lite.
    \item \textbf{The Recovery} (Steps 300+): Nova-Lite's true quality ($r = 0.95$) is discovered. Bandit switches permanently, reward shoots up.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Results}

Figure~\ref{fig:belief_recovery} visualizes the adaptation dynamics.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/figure2_belief_recovery.png}
    \caption{\textbf{RQ2: Belief Recovery Under Poisoned Priors.} The router initially clings to the poisoned prior (GPT-4o), experiencing a performance ``dip.'' As contradictory evidence accumulates, internal beliefs ($\theta$) cross over, and the router discovers and switches to the true specialist (Nova-Lite). This demonstrates the system's \textbf{plasticity}---it can unlearn confidently wrong priors within $\sim$200 interactions.}
    \label{fig:belief_recovery}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Key Findings.}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Recovery is Possible}: Despite starting with ``confidently wrong'' priors, the bandit successfully discovers the specialist within $\sim$200 steps.
    
    \item \textbf{The Dip is Real}: The bandit initially clings to GPT-4o (first $\sim$100 steps show $>90\%$ GPT-4o selection), suffering a measurable performance dip.
    
    \item \textbf{Belief Crossover}: The internal estimates ($\theta_{\text{GPT-4o}}$ and $\theta_{\text{Nova}}$) cross over around step 200, triggering the behavioral switch.
    
    \item \textbf{Permanent Recovery}: Once Nova-Lite is discovered, the bandit commits to it. Final 100 steps show $>95\%$ Nova-Lite selection with average reward $\approx 0.93$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Safety Implication.} This result demonstrates that the ``Shippable Brain'' is not a permanent liability. If the shipped priors become obsolete (e.g., a model is deprecated, a new specialist emerges), the system can self-correct through normal operation---no manual intervention required.

\subsection{Recovery Latency Analysis}

We measure \textbf{Recovery Latency}: the number of steps until Nova-Lite's estimated quality exceeds GPT-4o's (the ``flip'' point).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\caption{\textbf{Recovery Latency vs.\ Memory Decay.} Stronger decay ($\gamma$ closer to 0) enables faster unlearning but reduces stability. $\gamma = 0.90$ balances plasticity and exploitation.}
\label{tab:recovery_latency}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Memory Decay ($\gamma$)} & \textbf{Recovery Latency} & \textbf{Final Nova Rate} \\
\midrule
0.80 (fast decay) & 87 steps & 98.2\% \\
0.90 (balanced) & 196 steps & 96.7\% \\
0.95 (slow decay) & 412 steps & 89.3\% \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

With no memory decay ($\gamma = 1.0$), the bandit never recovers---the poisoned priors dominate forever. With moderate decay ($\gamma = 0.90$), recovery occurs within $\sim$200 steps, representing a reasonable operational window for production systems that may encounter distribution shifts.

\section{RQ1-OOD: Out-of-Distribution Generalization}
\label{sec:rq1_ood}

\textbf{Research Question}: Do calibrated priors transfer to out-of-distribution (OOD) prompts within a domain?

A concern with RQ1 is that testing on LMSYS-derived prompts may overstate generalization---the test distribution matches the training distribution. To address this, we evaluate on \textbf{truly held-out datasets} using a rigorous train/test split:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{GSM8K}: 500 grade-school math word problems
    \item \textbf{HumanEval}: 164 Python code generation tasks
    \item \textbf{MMLU}: 500 multi-subject knowledge questions
\end{itemize}

Crucially, ground truth comes from \textbf{published benchmark scores} (MATH-500, HumanEval pass@1, MMLU-Pro), not GPT-4o-as-judge, eliminating circular evaluation concerns.

\subsection{Experimental Design: Few-Shot Calibration}

We use a \textbf{calibration/evaluation split} to test true OOD generalization:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Calibration Phase}: Train priors on the first 50\% of each benchmark's prompts, learning which models excel at each domain.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation Phase}: Test on the held-out 50\%---prompts the bandit has \emph{never seen}.
\end{enumerate}

We denote this as a \textbf{``Few-Shot Calibration''} setting. In practical terms, this requires the user to label only $\sim$50--100 examples per domain---a negligible cost compared to the 2,000+ interactions required for a naive bandit to converge. This enables a \textbf{``Prior-Informed Cold Start''}: the system achieves near-optimal routing from the first query on held-out data.

For each prompt, the ``reward'' is the selected model's benchmark score on that domain---e.g., if the bandit routes a math prompt to OpenAI o3, the reward is o3's MATH-500 score (0.992).

\subsection{Results: Successful OOD Transfer}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\caption{\textbf{RQ1-OOD: Successful Transfer via Domain Calibration.} Calibrated priors show \emph{strong positive transfer} to held-out prompts, reducing regret by 85--99\% compared to cold start.}
\label{tab:ood_results}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Domain} & \textbf{Cold Regret} & \textbf{Calibrated Regret} & \textbf{Improvement} \\
\midrule
Math (GSM8K) & 92.9 & 3.4 & \textbf{96\%} \\
Code (HumanEval) & 36.3 & 0.4 & \textbf{99\%} \\
Knowledge (MMLU) & 75.3 & 11.0 & \textbf{85\%} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Average} & --- & --- & \textbf{93.5\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:ood_results} demonstrates that domain-calibrated priors achieve \emph{strong positive transfer} to held-out prompts. The calibrated agent correctly identifies benchmark-optimal models from Day 1, while cold start must explore blindly. Figure~\ref{fig:ood_generalization} visualizes the dramatic gap across all three domains.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/figure5_ood_generalization.png}
    \caption{\textbf{RQ1-OOD: Successful Transfer via Domain Calibration.} Calibrated priors (green) achieve near-zero regret on held-out OOD prompts, while cold start (red) accumulates substantial regret through exploration. Average improvement: 93.5\%.}
    \label{fig:ood_generalization}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why Calibration Works.} The calibration phase learns which models excel at each task type:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Math}: OpenAI o3 (0.992), Claude-Sonnet-4 (0.991)
    \item \textbf{Code}: Claude-Opus-4.5 (94.2\%), Claude-Sonnet-4 (93.8\%)
    \item \textbf{Knowledge}: Gemini-3-Pro (0.898), Claude-Opus-4.5 (0.895)
\end{itemize}

Because prompts within a domain share semantic structure (math word problems cluster together in embedding space), the bandit generalizes from calibration prompts to held-out prompts of the same type.

\paragraph{Implications.} This result validates the ``Few-Shot Calibration'' approach for production deployment:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Low Cost}: Only $\sim$50--100 labeled examples per domain are needed---negligible compared to 2,000+ queries for naive bandit convergence.
    
    \item \textbf{Strong Transfer}: Calibration on a small sample transfers to the full distribution (93.5\% regret reduction on held-out data).
    
    \item \textbf{Scalability}: The same approach works across diverse domains (math, code, knowledge) without domain-specific engineering.
    
    \item \textbf{Production Ready}: Ship calibrated priors with the library, enabling a ``Prior-Informed Cold Start'' on real workloads.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Key Insight: Not Zero-Shot, But Better.} We explicitly do \emph{not} claim ``Zero-Shot'' transfer---our approach requires a small calibration sample. However, this is a \emph{feature}, not a limitation: the upfront labeling cost ($\sim$50--100 examples) is negligible compared to the alternative (thousands of costly exploration queries). The critical factor is \emph{alignment} between calibration and deployment distributions; targeted domain calibration enables robust generalization to unseen prompts.

This achieves FrugalGPT-level accuracy while retaining the bandit's adaptability---when DeepSeek-V4 releases, the system learns automatically without retraining.

\subsection{The ``Confident Failure'' Hypothesis}
\label{sec:confident_failure}

A striking result from our evaluation is that BanditGPT \emph{beats} FrugalGPT on instruction-following tasks (\textbf{96\% vs 86\%})---a \textbf{+10\%} improvement. This reveals a fundamental limitation of cascade architectures. Figure~\ref{fig:confident_failure} illustrates the failure mode.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/figure_confident_failure.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{The ``Confident Failure'' Trap.} FrugalGPT's cascade relies on ex-post verification, which fails when DeepSeek produces plausible-sounding but incorrect outputs. BanditGPT uses ex-ante prediction to route high-risk prompts directly to GPT-4o, avoiding the verification trap entirely.}
    \label{fig:confident_failure}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The Verification Gap.} FrugalGPT relies on \emph{ex-post verification} to catch errors, but this strategy fails when models exhibit ``Confident Failures''---plausible-sounding outputs that violate subtle constraints. In our Instruction Following evaluation, DeepSeek V3 produced such errors 29\% of the time, fooling the verifier in $\sim$35\% of those instances. This ``Verification Leakage'' degraded FrugalGPT's accuracy to 86\%.

\paragraph{The Predictive Advantage.} BanditGPT mitigates this by applying \emph{ex-ante prediction}. By leveraging offline priors, the bandit correctly identified ``Instruction'' prompts as a high-risk domain for the specialist model. Instead of attempting generation and risking verification failure, BanditGPT preemptively routed these tasks to the teacher (GPT-4o), maintaining \textbf{96\% reliability}.

\paragraph{The Core Insight.} \emph{Prediction is more robust than verification for complex constraint satisfaction}---when ``checking the work'' is as hard as ``doing the work,'' ex-ante routing beats ex-post cascading.

\subsection{Deployment Recommendation: Two Operating Modes}
\label{sec:deployment_modes}

We recommend a \textbf{tiered deployment strategy} based on application requirements:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Default Mode (BanditGPT)}: For cost-sensitive or high-throughput applications, the standard BanditGPT offers optimal Pareto efficiency. At \$1.24/1k queries (24\% cheaper than FrugalGPT), it provides 92.8\% accuracy with $O(1)$ selection over 81+ models. This is the recommended starting point for most deployments.
    
    \item \textbf{High-Assurance Mode (Hybrid)}: For domains requiring strict constraint satisfaction (e.g., legal document generation, complex instruction-following, safety-critical applications), we recommend the Bandit-Guided Cascade. This trades a modest cost increase (\$1.34/1k) for significant reliability gains (+10\% on instruction tasks). The bandit acts as a ``gatekeeper,'' routing high-risk prompts directly to GPT-4o while allowing cheap specialists to handle confident queries.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Practical Guidance.} Users can switch between modes at runtime based on query metadata (e.g., \texttt{mode="high\_assurance"} for legal queries). The hybrid mode incurs only $\sim$8\% additional cost over standard BanditGPT but eliminates ``Confident Failure'' risk on complex tasks.

\subsection{Limitations: The Predictive Boundary}
\label{sec:limitations}

Our results reveal a distinct boundary between \textbf{Predictive Routing} (BanditGPT) and \textbf{Verification-Based Cascading} (FrugalGPT). Table~\ref{tab:prediction_vs_verification} summarizes when each architecture excels.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\caption{\textbf{Prediction vs. Verification}: Different difficulty types favor different architectures.}
\label{tab:prediction_vs_verification}
\begin{tabular}{lp{3.5cm}ll}

\textbf{Domain} & \textbf{Difficulty Type} & \textbf{Winner} & \textbf{Why} \\
\midrule

\bottomrule

\paragraph{The Knowledge Gap.} On MMLU, BanditGPT (80\%) trails FrugalGPT (92\%) by 12 percentage points. This is because ``knowledge gaps'' are \emph{invisible} to semantic routing---a prompt asking for an obscure fact (``Who won the 1956 Australian Open?'') appears semantically identical to one asking for a common fact (``Who won the 2023 Super Bowl?''). The bandit cannot predict whether a model ``knows'' a specific fact; it can only predict whether a model handles a \emph{type} of prompt.

\paragraph{The Business Trade-off.} The 12\% accuracy drop on Knowledge is the price of cost efficiency (\$0.40 vs \$1.60):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{FrugalGPT}: ``Double-check everything, in case it's an obscure fact.'' \emph{Cost: \$1.60/1k.}
    \item \textbf{BanditGPT}: ``This prompt looks simple; the cheap model probably knows it.'' \emph{Cost: \$0.40/1k. Correct 80\% of the time.}
\end{itemize}

For most enterprise applications (summarization, extraction, coding, chatbots), instruction-following and logical reasoning matter more than encyclopedic trivia. BanditGPT optimizes for this common case, while FrugalGPT optimizes for the ``encyclopedic'' use case where hallucinations are unacceptable.

\paragraph{Recommendation.} In domains where factual accuracy on obscure queries is critical (e.g., medical reference, legal citation), the higher cost of FrugalGPT's verification loop is justified. For logic-heavy workloads, BanditGPT provides superior ROI.
