\section{Hyperparameter Ablation Studies}
\label{app:ablations}

The main results (\S\ref{sec:metalearning_cost}) use the following
hyperparameter defaults: constant exploration ($\alpha{=}2.0$), mixing
parameter ($\gamma{=}0.05$), and learning rate
($\eta{=}\sqrt{\ln K/T}{\approx}0.030$).
This appendix validates these choices through systematic ablation and
provides guidance for practitioners requiring non-standard
configurations.

% ============================================================================
% D.1: Exploration Strategy (Alpha) Ablation
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Exploration Strategy Ablation}
\label{sec:alpha_ablation}

The LinUCB exploration parameter $\alpha$ controls the width of
confidence bounds, governing the exploration-exploitation trade-off.
Standard practice employs adaptive decay schedules
(e.g., $\alpha_t = \alpha_0 \cdot \max(\alpha_{\min}, 1/\sqrt{t+1})$)
to transition from exploration to exploitation~\cite{auer2002ucb}.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Experimental Setup
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
We evaluate four configurations on the 750-prompt holdout set
(PSI=0.225) across $N{=}20$ independent random seeds.
All configurations use Corralling with $\eta{=}0.030$,
$\gamma{=}0.05$, and loss decay $1.0$ (no decay).
Decay schedules use
$\alpha_t = \alpha_0 \cdot \max(0.1,\; 1/\sqrt{t+1})$.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{%
    \textbf{Exploration Strategy Ablation.}
    Decay improves Corralling performance on a static benchmark by
    allowing experts to commit to learned preferences sooner.
}
\label{tab:alpha_ablation}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Configuration} & \textbf{Warmup Expert} & \textbf{Tabula Rasa Expert} & \textbf{Cumulative Regret} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Homogeneous Decay} & decay: $2.0 \to 0.1$ & decay: $2.0 \to 0.1$ & \textbf{40.6 $\pm$ 4.4} \\
Mixed & $\alpha=2.0$ & decay: $2.0 \to 0.1$ & 41.9 $\pm$ 4.4 \\
\midrule
Reversed Mixed & decay: $2.0 \to 0.1$ & $\alpha=2.0$ & 46.8 $\pm$ 4.6 \\
Homogeneous Constant & $\alpha=2.0$ & $\alpha=2.0$ & 48.2 $\pm$ 5.1 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Results and Analysis
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Results.}
Decay-based exploration achieves the lowest regret on this static
benchmark, with homogeneous decay (40.6) outperforming constant
exploration (48.2) by 16\%:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Homogeneous Decay:} 40.6 $\pm$ 4.4
          (best; both experts commit to learned preferences)
    \item \textbf{Mixed:} 41.9 $\pm$ 4.4
          ($+$3\%; tabula rasa decay alone captures most of the benefit)
    \item \textbf{Reversed Mixed:} 46.8 $\pm$ 4.6
          ($+$15\%; warmup decay alone provides limited improvement)
    \item \textbf{Homogeneous Constant:} 48.2 $\pm$ 5.1
          (worst; continued exploration wastes regret budget)
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:alpha_ablation} shows detailed per-seed results.
Notably, decay also reduces variance (4.4 vs 5.1), indicating that
committed experts provide more predictable outcomes.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Mechanism and Interpretation
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Mechanism: Exploitation Benefits in Static Environments.}
On a fixed holdout set, the optimal decision boundary does not change
over time.  Decaying $\alpha$ allows experts to narrow their confidence
bounds and commit to learned preferences once sufficient evidence
accumulates.  This is beneficial because:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Warmup expert with decay:} Commits to semantic priors
          once online evidence confirms them, reducing unnecessary
          exploration of contexts where priors are correct.
    \item \textbf{Tabula rasa with decay:} Commits to learned
          preferences sooner, providing clearer signal to the
          meta-learner about its quality.
    \item \textbf{Meta-learner:} Benefits from experts that express
          confident, stable preferences---confident experts produce
          lower-variance loss estimates.
\end{enumerate}

The asymmetry in the Mixed vs Reversed configurations reveals that
tabula rasa decay ($-$6.3 regret) is more impactful than warmup decay
($-$1.4 regret).  This makes sense: the warmup expert already has
strong priors that constrain its behavior, so $\alpha$-decay provides
marginal additional commitment.  The tabula rasa expert, starting from
zero, benefits substantially from transitioning to exploitation after
its initial learning phase.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Important Caveat
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Important Caveat: Static vs Dynamic Environments.}
This finding is \emph{specific to static benchmarks} where the
data-generating process does not change.  In non-stationary environments
(\S\ref{sec:catastrophic}), premature exploitation prevents detection
of model degradation or distribution shift.  Constant $\alpha$ maintains
``vigilance''---the continued ability to detect changes---at the cost
of higher regret in stable conditions.

\textbf{Configuration guideline:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Static/validated deployment} (known-good priors,
          stable distribution): Use decay ($\alpha: 2.0 \to 0.1$) for
          both experts to maximise performance.
    \item \textbf{Unknown/shifting deployment} (unvalidated priors,
          potential drift): Use constant $\alpha{=}2.0$ to maintain
          detection capability.  Accept the 16\% regret premium as the
          cost of environmental awareness.
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric compromise:} Decay tabula rasa only
          (41.9 regret) captures most decay benefit while preserving
          warmup vigilance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mixing Parameter Ablation}
\label{app:gamma_ablation}

The mixing parameter $\gamma$ controls the minimum expert selection
probability: $P_t(\text{expert}) \geq \gamma/K$.
This prevents ``expert death,'' where an expert's weight drops to zero
and cannot recover from subsequent distribution shifts.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Experimental Setup
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
We evaluate $\gamma \in \{0.00, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20\}$ on the
750-prompt holdout set across $N{=}20$ independent seeds.
All configurations use constant exploration ($\alpha{=}2.0$) and the
theoretically optimal learning rate ($\eta{=}0.030$).
$\gamma{=}0.00$ represents pure exponential weighting (Hedge/EXP3)
without mixing.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Results
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Results: Gamma Has Minimal Impact.}
With a correctly calibrated learning rate, $\gamma$ has negligible
effect on performance (Table~\ref{tab:gamma_ablation}):

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{%
    \textbf{Mixing Parameter Ablation.}
    With $\eta{=}0.030$, all $\gamma$ values achieve similar regret.
    The total range (0.7 regret) is well within one standard deviation.
}
\label{tab:gamma_ablation}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
$\gamma$ & \textbf{Cumulative Regret} & \textbf{vs $\gamma{=}0.00$} \\
\midrule
0.00 & 47.9 $\pm$ 4.8 & --- \\
\textbf{0.05 (default)} & \textbf{48.2 $\pm$ 5.1} & $+$0.6\% \\
0.10 & 48.5 $\pm$ 4.9 & $+$1.3\% \\
0.20 & 48.6 $\pm$ 4.9 & $+$1.5\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The entire range spans only 0.7 cumulative regret (47.9 to 48.6)
across all $\gamma$ values.  Variance is similarly stable (4.8--5.1).
There is no statistically significant difference between any pair of
configurations.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Why gamma doesn't matter here
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Why Gamma Has Minimal Impact.}
With the theoretically optimal $\eta{\approx}0.030$, weight updates are
small and gradual.  Expert weights evolve from 50/50 to approximately
71/29 over 750 rounds---they never approach the extreme values where the
$\gamma$-mixing floor would activate.  Specifically:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item At $\gamma{=}0.05$ with $K{=}2$, the mixing floor guarantees
          $P(\text{expert}) \geq 0.025$.  Since the minimum expert
          probability remains well above this threshold throughout
          training, the floor is never binding.
    \item At $\gamma{=}0.00$, the absence of a floor has no consequence
          because $\eta$ is too small to drive weights to zero within
          750 rounds.
\end{itemize}

This contrasts with an earlier configuration using $\eta{=}1.0$, where
$\gamma$ had a larger effect because the aggressive learning rate could
drive expert weights to near-zero within a few rounds, making the mixing
floor essential for stability.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Why we still recommend gamma=0.05
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Why We Still Recommend $\gamma{=}0.05$.}
Despite the minimal impact observed here, we recommend $\gamma{=}0.05$
as the default for three reasons:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Zero cost:} The performance difference ($<$1\%) is
          negligible, so there is no reason \emph{not} to include it.
    \item \textbf{Longer horizons:} Over thousands of queries (typical
          production deployments), weights may reach more extreme values
          where the floor provides genuine protection.
    \item \textbf{Robustness to misconfiguration:} If a practitioner
          uses a sub-optimal $\eta$ (too large), $\gamma$ provides a
          safety net against expert death.  The parameter costs nothing
          when it is not needed and provides insurance when it is.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Configuration guideline:}
Use $\gamma{=}0.05$ for all deployments.  No tuning is required.
Deviate only with many experts ($K{>}5$, consider $\gamma{=}0.01$ to
reduce forced exploration overhead) or highly non-stationary
environments ($\gamma{=}0.10$ for faster recovery).
