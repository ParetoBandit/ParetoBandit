\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}

\subsubsection{Latent Semantic Space Construction}

To translate raw linguistic data into a manageable feature space for the BanditGPT context vector, we utilize a dimensionality reduction pipeline focused on \textbf{Latent Semantic Specialization}.

\paragraph{Dimensionality Reduction Strategy.}
While raw sentence embeddings (e.g., from the \texttt{sentence-transformers} library) provide high-fidelity representations, their inherent noise and high dimensionality (e.g., 768 or 1536 dimensions) impede the convergence speed of a contextual bandit. 
We employ Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to project these embeddings into a 32-dimensional latent space. 
This specific dimensionality was selected because it captures the ``intrinsic dimensionality'' of task difficulty---filtering out stylistic linguistic variance while preserving the semantic features critical for identifying the ``Hard'' perimeter seen in Figure~\ref{fig:semantic_specialization}.
The 32-component model captures 35.14\% of the total variance, balancing representation capacity with computational tractability.

\paragraph{Defining Semantic Neighborhoods.}
Our 32-dimensional space is grounded by the \textbf{Bimodal Reward Structure} identified in the RouteLLM dataset. 
Rather than regressing through a continuous probability map, we isolate three distinct semantic zones based on the measured reward gap ($\Delta = R_{\text{Strong}} - R_{\text{Weak}}$):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Easy/Sufficient Core} ($|\Delta| \leq 0.3$): 17,712 prompts (22.1\%) where the weak model's performance is indistinguishable from the strong model.
    
    \item \textbf{The Ambiguous Valley} ($0.3 < \Delta \leq 0.6$): A 0.0\% density region where the offline dataset provides no decisive signal. This ``Valley'' serves as a structural justification for online exploration; when the router encounters a prompt in this latent neighborhood, it must rely on real-time feedback rather than legacy priors.
    
    \item \textbf{The Hard/Required Perimeter} ($\Delta > 0.6$): 54,845 prompts (68.6\%) where the strong model is statistically superior.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Addressing the ``RouteLLM Anchor''}

A significant challenge in constructing these priors is the 68.6\% ``Hard'' skew found in the original human-voted preference data. 
As visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:semantic_specialization}, this creates a massive ``Hard'' shell that dominates the semantic landscape.

In a standard classification framework (e.g., RouteLLM's BERT-based router), this skew acts as a ``Historical Anchor,'' pre-filling 70\% of the latent space with a bias toward expensive model usage. 
Our framework addresses this through \textbf{Bayesian Recalibration}, using the 32-dimensional PCA neighborhoods not as absolute truths, but as soft priors that can be rapidly de-weighted via Gamma-scaling ($\gamma$) during the online calibration phase.
This allows BanditGPT to inherit the linguistic structure (``what coding prompts look like'') while discarding the frequency bias (``how often GPT-4-Turbo is needed''), enabling efficient domain adaptation with minimal calibration data.

\subsection{Semantic Task Specialization in Latent Space}

Figure~\ref{fig:semantic_specialization} reveals the fundamental structure of task difficulty across 80,000 RouteLLM prompts.
By projecting prompt embeddings into a 2D PCA space and visualizing the regional hard/easy density ratio as a heatmap, we uncover three critical insights that validate our approach.
The heatmap shows the ratio $(D_{\text{hard}} - D_{\text{easy}}) / (D_{\text{hard}} + D_{\text{easy}})$ where $D$ represents KDE density, with red indicating hard-dominated regions, blue showing easy-dominated regions, and white representing mixed areas.

\subsubsection{Latent Semantic Specialization}

The high-resolution visual evidence in Figure~\ref{fig:semantic_specialization} provides the ``Ground Truth'' for our latent representation: task difficulty is not a random noise variable but a structured semantic property of the prompt space.
The heatmap confirms the \textbf{Latent Semantic Transfer (LST)} hypothesis: difficulty is a latent property encoded within a prompt's semantic neighborhood.
We use a 32-component PCA model that captures 35.14\% of the total variance in the 384-dimensional sentence embeddings, with the first 2 principal components (used for visualization) capturing 5.39\% of the variance.

\paragraph{The Geography of Difficulty: Core-Shell Architecture.}
Our analysis reveals what we term a \textbf{Core-Shell Architecture} in latent space.
The visualization shows a high-density ``Hard'' perimeter (Red) that encapsulates a more varied ``Easy/Mixed'' core (Blue/White).
This geography visually proves that high-difficulty tasks are often semantically distinct---clustering as specialized domains, complex logical structures, or technical edge cases---while general conversational tasks cluster toward the center.
The red regions (hard-dominated, representing 68.6\% of prompts with $\text{Gap} > 0.6$) and blue regions (easy-dominated, 22.1\% with $|\text{Gap}| \le 0.3$) form distinct pockets, demonstrating spatial clustering that enables a linear model (LinUCB) to learn efficient decision boundaries.

\paragraph{Implications for Cold-Start Capability.}
Because ``Hard'' tasks cluster together in semantic space, the router can identify a hard task in the evaluation set \emph{even if it has never seen that specific prompt before}, simply because it lands in a ``red'' semantic neighborhood learned during the 80,000-sample warmup.
This is the foundation of our cold-start generalization.

\subsubsection{The Ambiguous Frontier}

The white/mixed regions in the heatmap represent what we term the \textbf{Ambiguous Frontier}---areas where hard and easy prompts coexist in similar densities, meaning a prompt's semantic location alone does not provide a definitive routing signal.
The substantial extent of these mixed regions (contour line at 0.0 ratio) is not noise; it is a fundamental property of the task distribution that justifies our model choice.

In these ambiguous zones, a \emph{static router} (e.g., threshold-based or trained classifier) would consistently misclassify prompts because the linguistic features alone are insufficient.
The Contextual Bandit excels precisely in this regime: it uses \emph{real-time reward feedback} to navigate ambiguous cases, adapting its policy as it observes which model actually performs better for prompts in these mixed-density regions.

\paragraph{Why Bandits Outperform Static Classifiers.}
Static routers assume a fixed decision boundary in feature space.
However, the heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:semantic_specialization}) shows that no clean boundary exists---the interspersed red, blue, and white regions demonstrate that easy and hard tasks coexist throughout semantic space.
BanditGPT resolves this by treating routing as a \emph{sequential decision problem}, where the decision boundary is learned online through contextual reward feedback in these mixed regions.

\subsubsection{Support for Decisive Bimodality}

The reward gap distribution ($\Delta = R_{\text{GPT-4-Turbo}} - R_{\text{Mixtral}}$) exhibits a sharp bimodal structure characterized by an absolute vacuum in the moderate range. 
Our analysis of 80,000 samples confirms that exactly 0.00\% of prompts fall within the moderate difficulty category ($0.3 < \Delta \le 0.6$). 
Instead, tasks are strictly partitioned into three discrete modes: (1) \textbf{Hard tasks} (68.6\%, $\Delta = 1.0$) where GPT-4-Turbo wins decisively, (2) \textbf{Easy tasks} (22.1\%, $\Delta = 0.0$) where models are equivalent, and (3) \textbf{Inverse tasks} (9.3\%, $\Delta = -1.0$) where Mixtral outperforms the stronger model.

\paragraph{The Failure of Continuous Regressors.}
This decisive bimodality is the strongest argument for why a contextual bandit outperforms standard regressors (like RouteLLM's Matrix Factorization).
Because the problem is naturally discrete and not continuous, a regressor attempting to interpolate across a non-existent ``Moderate Valley'' incurs significant bias and slower convergence.
BanditGPT exploits this discrete structure by treating routing as a binary-modal selection problem, allowing for near-instantaneous policy shifts once the modes are identified.

\paragraph{Mode-Centric Buffer Thresholds.}
We justify the use of $0.3$ and $0.6$ as thresholds specifically because they sit within this ``no-man's land.'' 
By using these values as buffer thresholds, we isolate the discrete reward modes from potential floating-point noise or marginal tie-breaks, ensuring that the semantic clusters visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:semantic_specialization} are statistically robust and representative of decisive model advantages. 
This structural alignment between the warmup priors and the evaluation domain is what allows the Bayesian Recalibration (via covariance inflation, $\gamma$-scaling) to converge so efficiently during the 150-sample calibration phase.
The router does not need to learn the entire difficulty spectrum from scratch; it only needs to \emph{recalibrate} the relative frequency of hard vs.\ easy tasks in the target domain.

\paragraph{Efficient Domain Adaptation.}
Because the warmup priors already encode the semantic neighborhoods of hard tasks, calibration reduces to adjusting the \emph{prior confidence} (via $\gamma$) rather than retraining the feature representation.
This is why we achieve 74\% of the optimal efficiency gain using only 0.19\% of the warmup data (1,121 vs.\ 80,000 samples).

\subsubsection{Technical Challenge: The ``RouteLLM Anchor''}

The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:semantic_specialization} reveals a significant class imbalance in the warmup data: 68.6\% (54,845) of prompts are classified as ``Hard,'' while only 22.1\% (17,712) are ``Easy.'' 
This skew creates the \textbf{RouteLLM Anchor}---a legacy constraint where our only available large-scale training data is heavily skewed by human preference bias.
In the Chatbot Arena battles, users often over-vote for the flagship model (GPT-4-Turbo) even on tasks where a smaller model is sufficient, pre-filling our prior with the belief that 70\% of semantic space is high-difficulty.

\paragraph{Breaking the Anchor: The Bandit's Resilience.}
The primary technical challenge becomes: \emph{How do we deploy an efficient router when our only available training data is heavily skewed by human preference bias?} 
This is the ``hero moment'' for our adaptive algorithm. 
Rather than trusting these frequencies as absolute, BanditGPT treats the warmup data merely as a \textbf{Prior Structure} for semantic neighborhoods. 
The Bayesian Recalibration phase (via $\gamma$-scaling) allows the router to quickly pivot from this 68.6\% ``Hard'' belief to a cost-optimized policy that favors the weak model for the specific task distribution of the target domain.
By adjusting the prior confidence, we successfully break the RouteLLM Anchor and achieve domain-specific efficiency without discarding the linguistic intuition learned from the 80,000 samples.

\subsubsection{Key Takeaway: Cold-Start Generalization vs. Prior Bias}

Figure~\ref{fig:semantic_specialization} provides \textbf{empirical evidence} that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Difficulty is latent, not random.} Hard tasks cluster in distinct semantic neighborhoods, enabling cold-start generalization.
    \item \textbf{Static features are insufficient.} The Ambiguous Frontier necessitates online learning with contextual feedback.
    \item \textbf{Bimodal structure enables efficient calibration.} The warmup priors capture the essential difficulty distribution, requiring only small-scale recalibration for domain adaptation.
\end{enumerate}

This structural understanding is the foundation of BanditGPT's sample efficiency and cold-start performance.
The router does not memorize individual prompts; it learns the \emph{semantic geometry} of task difficulty, allowing it to generalize to unseen prompts within the same latent regions.
