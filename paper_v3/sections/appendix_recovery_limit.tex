\section{Recovery Limit under Quality Degradation}
\label{appendix:recovery_limit}

Experiment~03 (\S\ref{sec:eval_catastrophic}) demonstrates recovery from an ${\sim}18$\% quality degradation within the standard 608-prompt Phase~3 horizon.  Here we characterise the \emph{recovery envelope}: the maximum degradation severity from which the system can recover to near-Phase~1 performance, and how extending the recovery horizon shifts that boundary.

\paragraph{Setup.}
We sweep Mistral-Large's degraded reward from $0.05$ to $0.85$ (4--94\% degradation relative to its normal reward of ${\sim}0.92$).  Degradation is modelled as a mean-shift: per-prompt rewards are shifted so the degraded arm's mean reward equals the target level while retaining prompt-dependent variation (clipped to $[0,1]$).  This is more realistic than constant-reward replacement because real regressions affect prompts heterogeneously.  Cost remains unchanged during Phase~2 so only the reward signal reveals the regression.  All runs use the moderate budget ($\$6.62{\times}10^{-4}$/prompt), 20~seeds, and the same hyperparameters as the main experiment.  We define \emph{full recovery} as a Phase~3/Phase~1 reward ratio ${\geq}97$\%.

For the extended-horizon evaluation, Phase~3 uses all non-Phase-2 holdout prompts ($2{\times}$ the standard horizon) without recycling, ensuring the i.i.d.\ prompt arrival assumption is preserved and extended-horizon results reflect genuine recovery on fresh prompts.

\paragraph{Recovery mechanisms.}
Recovery operates through two complementary mechanisms.  \emph{Geometric forgetting} ($\gamma{<}1$) exponentially down-weights corrupted Phase-2 observations so that fresh Phase-3 data dominates the mean estimate; this is the primary driver of recovery.  \emph{Staleness-driven exploration} inflates the UCB variance for non-selected arms, potentially triggering re-exploration.  However, under budget-constrained routing the cost penalty can exceed the exploration bonus, limiting this mechanism's independent contribution.  Both mechanisms act jointly, but the forgetting half-life (${\sim}230$ steps at $\gamma{=}0.997$) sets the characteristic timescale.

\paragraph{Recovery envelope (Figure~\ref{fig:recovery_limit}a).}
At the standard 608-prompt horizon, full recovery is achieved for degradations up to ${\sim}18$\% (failure reward ${\geq}0.75$, P3/P1${\,=\,}97.5$\%).  Beyond this threshold, recovery plateaus around $90$\%: once the mean-estimate corruption exceeds what geometric forgetting can erase within 608~prompts, additional severity has diminishing marginal impact.

Extending Phase~3 to 1{,}216 unique prompts ($2{\times}$ the standard horizon, no prompt recycling) uniformly lifts the entire envelope.  Full recovery now extends to ${\sim}24$\% degradation (failure reward ${\geq}0.70$, P3/P1${\,=\,}98.3$\%), and the severe-degradation floor rises from ${\sim}90$\% to ${\sim}93$\%.  For the most extreme cases (${\geq}90$\% degradation), the extended horizon still leaves a gap, suggesting that yet longer horizons or explicit operator intervention would be needed.  At mild degradation levels (${\leq}10$\%), the P3/P1 ratio can exceed $100$\% (observed: $100.8$\% at 7\% degradation): Phase~2 trains the policy away from the degraded arm, and for some prompts the alternative routing discovered during degradation is genuinely better.

\paragraph{Convergence dynamics (Figure~\ref{fig:recovery_limit}b).}
The extended-horizon time-series (50-prompt rolling window) reveal that recovery is monotonic but rate-dependent on severity.  Milder degradations converge to the Phase~1 baseline well before the horizon limit, while more severe degradations require the full extended horizon and may still fall short.  In all cases the confidence bands narrow over time, indicating stable convergence rather than high-variance oscillation.

\paragraph{Limitations and deployment implications.}
This study evaluates only the moderate budget level.  Under a tighter budget the cost penalty $\lambda$ is higher, which may slow recovery; under a looser budget, recovery may be faster.  Additionally, only single-arm, quality-only degradation is tested; simultaneous multi-arm or cost-shift regressions may behave differently.

For typical silent regressions (${\leq}20$\%), ParetoBandit's default configuration recovers automatically within the standard observation window.  For severe regressions, operators can either extend the recovery horizon or intervene with an explicit model demotion---the staleness-inflation mechanism ensures the demoted arm will be re-explored once conditions improve.
