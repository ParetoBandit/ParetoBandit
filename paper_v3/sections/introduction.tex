\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Production LLM portfolios now span a $530\times$ cost range (e.g., \$0.00003/req for Llama-3.1-8B vs.\ \$0.015 for Gemini-2.5-Pro), and the best model for a given request varies with the prompt~\cite{chen2024routerdc, feng2025graphrouter}. A growing body of work has proposed routers that learn which model to dispatch~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt, ong2024routellm, panda2025pilot}. The question is no longer \emph{whether} to route, but how to do so under the operational realities of deployment: dollar budgets, non-stationary pricing, and evolving model portfolios.

Most existing routers learn a fixed policy from offline data and freeze it at serving time. Cascading approaches query models sequentially from cheapest to most expensive~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt}; supervised classifiers learn from preference data~\cite{ong2024routellm}, contrastive representations~\cite{chen2024routerdc}, or graph-structured interactions~\cite{feng2025graphrouter}. These methods perform well when deployment matches training, but the environment does not hold still. Providers silently update APIs in ways that shift quality~\cite{chen2023chatgptdrift, ma2024prompt}, pricing changes abruptly (OpenAI cut GPT-4~Turbo input prices by 67\%~\cite{openai2025turbopricing}; Google cut Gemini~1.5~Flash pricing by 78\% overnight~\cite{google2024flashpricing}), and new models launch regularly. Even cost-aware formulations share this limitation: OmniRouter~\cite{ma2025omnirouter} solves an offline linear program over a fixed prompt set, and PROTEUS~\cite{bhatti2026proteus} trains a Lagrangian RL policy offline, explicitly identifying post-deployment cost drift as an open problem. A frozen router cannot exploit a price drop, detect a degraded model, or incorporate a new endpoint without an offline retraining cycle.

A parallel line of work frames routing as a bandit problem, where the system observes the outcome only for the model it dispatches~\cite{panda2025pilot, wang2025barp, li2025llmbandit}. Continual-learning frameworks~\cite{wang2025mixllm} further accommodate evolving model pools. Yet empirical comparisons under shared prompts and model pools show that margins over single-model baselines remain small or inconsistent, including for vendor-offered routers~\cite{li2026llmrouterbench}. Despite this progress, two deployment gaps remain open.

\paragraph{Gap 1: No autonomous budget control.}
Several routers accept budget or SLA targets~\cite{panda2025pilot, ma2025omnirouter, bhatti2026proteus}, but none enforces a per-request dollar target in closed loop over an open-ended stream. Knapsack formulations accept a total dollar budget but require knowing the number of requests in advance, making them unsuitable for open-ended production streams~\cite{panda2025pilot}. OmniRouter~\cite{ma2025omnirouter} solves a constrained optimisation over a fixed prompt set via Lagrangian dual decomposition and cannot adapt to live traffic. PROTEUS~\cite{bhatti2026proteus} trains a Lagrangian RL policy against SLA constraints offline but freezes it at serving time, so it cannot react to post-deployment price or quality shifts. Although these formulations do accept dollar or SLA targets, they require either a known request horizon, an offline prompt set, or offline training, and none re-adjusts spending in real time when the pricing surface changes.

\paragraph{Gap 2: No principled non-stationarity handling.}
Although non-stationary bandit algorithms with geometric discounting or sliding windows are well studied in the theory literature~\cite{garivier2011switching, russac2019weighted}, no existing LLM router incorporates such mechanisms. The bandit-based routers cited above all use infinite memory ($\gamma{=}1.0$), which is optimal when the environment is stationary but causes the router to react far too slowly when a model degrades or a price drops. More broadly, no LLM routing paper we are aware of evaluates how a router responds when model quality degrades after deployment, nor when API prices change mid-stream. In our catastrophic-failure experiment, a bandit that learns from traffic but lacks closed-loop budget control detects the degraded model yet overshoots the per-request cost target by up to $5.42\times$ because it cannot re-balance spending after the cost landscape shifts (Section~\ref{sec:eval_nonstationary}). Meanwhile, standard cross-validation selects exactly $\gamma{=}1.0$ because it optimises stationary metrics, producing a configuration that fails under drift.

\vspace{0.5em}

These gaps carry direct financial consequences. A router without closed-loop cost control either wastes quality headroom (under-spend) or triggers SLA-violating cost overruns (over-spend). A router that cannot detect a price drop leaves freed budget idle rather than reinvesting it in quality.

Beyond these two gaps, production portfolios are not static: new models launch, old ones are deprecated, and operators need to adjust the roster without downtime. A bandit formulation naturally accommodates portfolio changes because its exploration bonus discovers a newcomer's quality-cost niche from live traffic alone. We demonstrate this capability experimentally but note that it is a practical benefit of the design rather than a limitation unique to prior work.

We address these challenges with \textbf{ParetoBandit}, an open-source adaptive routing system for budget-constrained, non-stationary LLM serving. ParetoBandit is built on cost-aware contextual bandits and introduces three system-level contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Autonomous budget control} (Section~\ref{sec:budget_pacer}). An online primal-dual budget pacer accepts a dollar-denominated per-request cost target directly and enforces it in closed loop over an open-ended stream with no known horizon. The pacer detects price drops via an EMA-smoothed cost signal, automatically reallocates freed budget to higher-quality models, and recovers compliance when prices are restored. Realised spend never exceeds the target: utilization reaches $0.98$--$1.00\times$ only when the budget actively constrains quality, and drops well below when the quality-optimal policy is already cheaper, avoiding unnecessary frontier calls.

  \item \textbf{Non-stationarity resilience} (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive_learning}). Geometric forgetting gives the bandit a bounded memory window: recent observations carry more weight than old ones, so the router tracks quality and cost shifts while retaining the knowledge encoded in its offline warmup priors. The exploration and forgetting rates are selected via a Pareto knee-point procedure (Appendix~\ref{sec:hparam_sweep}) anchored by a single practitioner-specified parameter: the adaptation horizon $T_{\text{adapt}}$, the number of queries needed to override the prior after a distributional shift. This avoids the trap of standard cross-validation, which invariably selects no forgetting. Under a mid-cycle price drop, the system delivers a $+0.073$ quality lift at the tightest budget. Under catastrophic model failure, it redistributes traffic and maintains budget compliance across all tested conditions.

  \item \textbf{Runtime portfolio management} (Section~\ref{sec:production_infra}). As a practical benefit of the bandit formulation, a hot-swap model registry supports adding and removing models at runtime via a single API call. The bandit's exploration bonus discovers a cold-started model's quality-cost niche from live traffic within approximately 200 steps, while the budget pacer maintains compliance throughout the portfolio expansion. Persistent context stores and atomic checkpointing support delayed feedback, crash recovery, and the operational realities of multi-process serving architectures.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why $K{\geq}3$ matters.}
Real enterprise portfolios are not binary strong-vs-weak pairs. Organizations maintain three or more model tiers, and the number of viable endpoints per provider continues to grow. The value of a larger portfolio is that more models at distinct price points give the router more degrees of freedom to find quality within a budget~\cite{li2026llmrouterbench}. A $K{=}2$ router can only choose ``cheap'' or ``expensive''; with $K{\geq}3$ it can discover that a mid-cost model matches frontier quality on easy prompts at a fraction of the cost, stretching the budget to afford frontier calls where they matter most. The same logic applies to non-stationarity: when a model degrades, a richer portfolio increases the chance that an alternative maintains quality at a comparable cost.

We evaluate ParetoBandit on 1,785 prompts drawn from eight public benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, HellaSwag, ARC-Challenge, and others; see Section~\ref{sec:eval_setup}) spanning a canonical three-tier portfolio ($K{=}3$, with a $K{=}4$ onboarding extension), scored by DeepSeek-R1 under a continuous three-factor rubric. R1 was selected over a three-judge panel because it produces the sharpest routing signal; Appendix~\ref{sec:judge_robustness} validates that all three judges preserve the expected model ordering and that R1's routing decisions capture ${\geq}97\%$ of any alternative judge's oracle reward. The system design is presented in Section~\ref{sec:system_design} and evaluated against both deployment gaps, along with the model-onboarding capability, in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.
