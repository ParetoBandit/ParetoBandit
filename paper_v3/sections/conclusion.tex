\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented ParetoBandit, an adaptive LLM routing system that integrates cost-aware contextual bandits with online budget pacing and geometric forgetting. Across four experiments on a three-model portfolio, the system maintains per-request cost compliance within $0.95$--$1.01{\times}$ of the target budget while filling the quality--cost gaps between fixed single-model baselines. Under cost drift, the budget pacer autonomously exploits a price drop and recovers compliance when pricing is restored. Under silent quality degradation, ParetoBandit detects the regression, redistributes traffic, and holds budget compliance throughout---while baselines that learn but lack closed-loop cost control overshoot by up to $6.9{\times}$. Cold-start onboarding of a fourth model proceeds without reconfiguration, with the budget pacer maintaining compliance through the portfolio transition. As organizations increasingly operate multi-provider LLM portfolios subject to volatile pricing and unannounced quality shifts, closed-loop cost control moves from a convenience to a systems-level necessity; these results suggest that bandit-driven routing with integrated budget pacing can fill that gap without manual intervention.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
Five limitations merit discussion, each pointing toward a concrete extension of the current framework.

\emph{(i)~Offline evaluation.}
Our evaluation relies on a fixed offline reward matrix with non-stationarity injected via phase-boundary permutations; live deployment would introduce additional stochastic feedback delays and distribution shift.
The cached context-vector architecture (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) already supports asynchronous reward ingestion, making shadow routing or interleaved A/B evaluation a natural next step that requires engineering effort but no algorithmic changes.

\emph{(ii)~Human-in-the-loop rewards.}
Although the architecture supports delayed human feedback, all experiments use deterministic LLM-judge scores; we have not evaluated the system under the noise, subjectivity, and sparsity characteristic of human-in-the-loop rewards.
Sparse labeling interacts with geometric forgetting: the effective sample size per arm may shrink faster than new labels replenish it, potentially destabilizing reward estimates.
Two mitigations are straightforward within the current design: adapting $\gamma$ to the label arrival rate (slowing forgetting when feedback is sparse) and imputing pseudo-labels from the current $\hat\theta_a$ at reduced weight to prevent the sufficient statistics from decaying during label gaps.

\emph{(iii)~Rate constraint vs.\ aggregate budget.}
Because $B$ is a \emph{per-request} rate constraint, total expenditure scales as $B \times N$, so a traffic spike can exceed an aggregate dollar budget even while per-request compliance holds.
A token-bucket layer that dynamically tightens $B$ as a cumulative spend ceiling approaches would cap total expenditure without modifying the bandit; in practice, setting $B$ so that each request is individually profitable makes traffic surges welcome rather than hazardous.

\emph{(iv)~Onboarding exploration cost.}
Unlike matrix-factorisation routers that characterise new models offline, ParetoBandit's online onboarding exposes a bounded exploration cost to production traffic.
Transfer priors---initialising a new arm's sufficient statistics from a structurally similar existing arm, scaled by a discount factor---and shadow evaluation (routing traffic to the new model in parallel without serving its responses) could reduce this burn-in cost substantially while preserving the online learning guarantees.

\emph{(v)~Latency and serving-capacity awareness.}
The current formulation optimises quality subject to a cost constraint but does not model downstream inference latency or endpoint congestion.
MixLLM~\cite{wang2025mixllm} addresses this gap by penalising arms whose estimated waiting time approaches a tolerance threshold, demonstrating that latency-aware routing avoids the congestion bottlenecks that arise when traffic concentrates on a single high-quality model.
However, MixLLM's latency penalty relies on a simulated queue model with fixed hardware assumptions, its covariance accumulation lacks a forgetting mechanism, and its cost--quality trade-off is governed by a static willingness-to-pay parameter rather than closed-loop budget enforcement.
Moreover, MixLLM evaluates on $K{=}11$ Routerbench models---the same pool where PILOT's traffic collapses to one or two active arms per task under exact-match rewards (Appendix~\ref{appendix:reward_discrimination}); while MixLLM's continuous quality predictor should mitigate this collapse, no per-model traffic distribution is reported to confirm genuine multi-model discrimination across all eleven arms.
ParetoBandit's Bandits-with-Knapsacks formulation naturally accommodates latency as a second rate constraint: introducing a dual variable $\mu_t$ with its own EMA-smoothed update---identical in form to the cost pacer (Eqs.~\ref{eq:cost_ema}--\ref{eq:dual_update})---would add a single adaptive penalty term $\mu_t \tilde{L}_a$ to the arm score (Eq.~\ref{eq:budget_ucb}), where $\tilde{L}_a$ is the observed or predicted latency for arm $a$.
This extension requires no changes to the LinUCB update, the forgetting mechanism, or the warmup priors, and would provide closed-loop latency pacing that adapts to real-time conditions rather than relying on a fixed queue simulation.

Validating the latency extension empirically and evaluating ParetoBandit under realistic human-feedback conditions are the most immediate next steps.
