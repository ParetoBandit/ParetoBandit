\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented ParetoBandit, an adaptive LLM routing system that integrates cost-aware contextual bandits with online budget pacing and geometric forgetting. Across four experiments on a three-model portfolio, the system maintains per-request cost compliance within $0.95$--$1.01{\times}$ of the target budget while filling the quality--cost gaps between fixed single-model baselines. Under cost drift, the budget pacer autonomously exploits a price drop and recovers compliance when pricing is restored. Under silent quality degradation, ParetoBandit detects the regression, redistributes traffic, and holds budget compliance throughout---while baselines that learn but lack closed-loop cost control overshoot by up to $6.9{\times}$. Cold-start onboarding of a fourth model proceeds without reconfiguration, with the budget pacer maintaining compliance through the portfolio transition. As organizations increasingly operate multi-provider LLM portfolios subject to volatile pricing and unannounced quality shifts, closed-loop cost control moves from a convenience to a systems-level necessity; these results suggest that bandit-driven routing with integrated budget pacing can fill that gap without manual intervention.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
Three limitations merit discussion. First, our evaluation relies on a fixed offline reward matrix with non-stationarity injected via phase-boundary permutations; live deployment would introduce additional latency variance and stochastic feedback delays. Second, although the architecture supports delayed human feedback via cached context vectors (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}), all experiments use deterministic LLM-judge scores; we have not evaluated the system under the noise, subjectivity, and sparsity characteristic of human-in-the-loop rewards. In particular, sparse labeling---where only a fraction of requests receive feedback---interacts with geometric forgetting: the effective sample size per arm may shrink faster than new labels replenish it, potentially destabilizing reward estimates. Third, because $B$ is a \emph{per-request} rate constraint, total expenditure scales as $B \times N$, so a traffic spike can exceed an aggregate dollar budget even while per-request compliance holds. Capping total spend requires an external rate limiter; in practice, setting $B$ so that each request is individually profitable makes traffic surges welcome rather than hazardous. Looking ahead, validating ParetoBandit under realistic human-feedback conditions and extending the framework to multi-objective constraints beyond cost, such as latency percentiles or prompt-category fairness, are natural next steps.
