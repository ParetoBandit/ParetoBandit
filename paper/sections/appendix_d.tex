\subsection{Catastrophic Failure Detection}
\label{app:catastrophic_failure}

Figure~\ref{fig:decommission} visualizes the internal dynamics of our Corralling Algorithm handling catastrophic model degradation.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/figure6_expert_decommission.png}
\caption{\textbf{Expert Decommissioning Under Synthetic Catastrophic Failure.} (Top) Evolution of expert weights during a synthetic scenario where model quality drops from $\mu=0.80$ to $\mu=0.15$. The system initially explores the Warmup Expert (Orange) but detects increasing loss. At $t \approx 50$, cumulative evidence triggers sharp exponential decay, shifting traffic to the Tabula Rasa Expert by $t=200$. (Bottom) Cumulative loss comparison confirms the Tabula Rasa expert (Green) is strictly superior post-failure.}
\label{fig:decommission}
\label{fig:expert_decommission}
\label{fig:catastrophic}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
We simulate a three-phase scenario with deterministic failure injection:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 ($t=0$--$100$):} Both models healthy ($\mu=0.80$, Cohen's $d\approx 0$)
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 ($t=100$--$300$):} GPT-4 catastrophically fails ($\mu=0.15$, Cohen's $d\approx 5.0$)
    \item \textbf{Phase 3 ($t=300$--$500$):} GPT-4 recovers ($\mu=0.80$)
\end{itemize}

Corralling settings: $\eta=0.3$ (fast failure detection), $\gamma=0.05$ (exploration floor).

\paragraph{Results (synthetic scenario only).}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Detection speed:} 3 steps (immediate failover after failure begins)
    \item \textbf{Success rate:} 100\% across all configurations tested in this synthetic scenario
    \item \textbf{False positives:} 0\% during Phase 1 (no spurious failovers)
    \item \textbf{Conservative recovery:} Maintains decommissioning even after recovery (safety-first)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope and Limitations:} This experiment validates the \emph{mechanism} of adaptive failover under idealized conditions: large, abrupt quality collapse (Cohen's $d \approx 5.0$) with deterministic, instantaneous phase transitions. The ``100\% detection rate'' and ``3-step failover'' claims apply only to this specific synthetic scenario and should not be generalized to production settings, where model failures are typically:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Gradual:} Quality may degrade slowly over days/weeks rather than collapsing instantly
    \item \textbf{Ambiguous:} Degradation may be intermittent or context-dependent ($d < 0.5$), making detection substantially harder
    \item \textbf{Partial:} A model may degrade on some task types while maintaining performance on others
\end{itemize}
Evaluating detection performance on real-world model failures---which would require either historical failure data or carefully designed gradual-degradation simulations---remains important future work.

\subsection{Gamma Mixing Parameter Ablation: Multi-Dimensional Validation}
\label{app:gamma_ablation}

The mixing parameter $\gamma$ provides a floor on expert selection probability, preventing "expert death" where an expert's weight drops to zero and cannot recover. We validate $\gamma=0.05$ as optimal across four dimensions using N=750 holdout prompts with 5 seeds per configuration (25 runs total, 18,750 model selections).

\subsubsection{The Four-Dimensional Validation Framework}

Figure~\ref{fig:gamma_ablation} tells a unified story about why $\gamma=0.05$ is not arbitrary but the empirically validated optimum.

\paragraph{(A) Performance Dimension: Minimal Cost for Enhanced Reliability.}
Panel (A) shows cumulative regret for $\gamma \in \{0.00, 0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20\}$. The configuration $\gamma=0.05$ achieves near-optimal regret (60.6 $\pm$ 1.4) comparable to pure exponential weighting ($\gamma=0.00$: 59.0 $\pm$ 5.0), but with \textbf{3$\times$ lower variance}. This demonstrates minimal performance cost for substantially enhanced reliability. Higher values degrade performance: $\gamma=0.10$ yields 69.2 $\pm$ 12.4 (+15\% regret), and $\gamma=0.20$ yields 77.0 $\pm$ 15.0 (+28\% regret), as the system wastes exploration on inferior experts.

\paragraph{(B) Safety Dimension: Stochastic Expert Death as Evidence.}
Panel (B) reveals why low $\gamma$ is problematic. The \emph{large error bars} at $\gamma=0.00$ (spanning 5 orders of magnitude: $10^{-7}$ to $10^{-2}$) provide \textbf{evidence} of stochastic expert death. Different random seeds cause different experts to be suppressed unpredictably---in some runs, Warmup drops to $10^{-7}$; in others, Tabula Rasa drops to $10^{-8}$; occasionally both remain near $10^{-2}$. This variance proves expert death is real and non-deterministic. In contrast, $\gamma=0.05$ reduces variance by 80\%, maintaining consistent minimum weights near the theoretical floor ($\gamma/K = 0.025$ for $K=2$ experts).

\paragraph{(C) Decisiveness Dimension: Lower Minimum Indicates Adaptation.}
Panel (C) shows temporal evolution of minimum expert weight. Counterintuitively, $\gamma=0.05$ achieves the \emph{lowest} minimum weights ($\sim 10^{-4}$), which indicates \emph{strong adaptation}, not failure. The meta-learner confidently allocates 80--90\% weight to the empirically higher-reward expert (based on observed losses), minimizing allocation to the lower-reward expert while staying safely above the death threshold ($10^{-8}$, red dashed line). Meanwhile, $\gamma=0.001$ maintains high minimum weights ($\sim 0.1$) due to \emph{indecision}---both experts remain near parity despite performance differences, wasting 30--40\% of queries on suboptimal routing. The $\gamma=0.2$ configuration is forced to maintain $\geq 10\%$ weight per expert, preventing decisive commitment.

\paragraph{(D) Predictability Dimension: Outcome Consistency.}
Panel (D) quantifies variance in final expert weights across seeds. $\gamma=0.05$ achieves 45\% lower variance than $\gamma=0.00$ (0.06 vs. 0.11), ensuring consistent deployment behavior. Extremely low variance at $\gamma=0.10$ (0.04) reflects forced exploration rather than adaptation, resulting in poor performance (panel A).

\subsubsection{The Goldilocks Optimum}

The four panels converge on $\gamma=0.05$ as the optimal balance:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Dimension} & $\gamma=0.00$ & $\gamma=0.05$ & \textbf{Improvement} \\
\midrule
Regret (A) & 59.0 $\pm$ 5.0 & 60.6 $\pm$ 1.4 & $-72\%$ variance \\
Min Weight (B) & 0.05 $\pm$ 0.08 & 0.06 $\pm$ 0.02 & $-75\%$ variance \\
Adaptation (C) & Indecisive & Decisive ($10^{-4}$) & Strong commit \\
Stability (D) & 0.11 & 0.06 & $-45\%$ variance \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Multi-dimensional validation of $\gamma=0.05$. Net result: same performance as $\gamma=0.00$ but dramatically more reliable.}
\label{tab:gamma_validation}
\end{table}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Interpretation}

With Corralling, expert selection probabilities are:
\begin{equation}
P_t(\text{expert}_i) = (1 - \gamma) \cdot \text{softmax}(-\eta \cdot L_{t,i}) + \frac{\gamma}{K}
\end{equation}
where $L_{t,i}$ is cumulative loss and $K$ is the number of experts. This guarantees $P_t(\text{expert}_i) \geq \gamma/K$ for all $t$ and all $i$, providing a safety floor. At $\gamma=0.05$ with $K=2$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Minimum probability: 2.5\% per expert
    \item Expected regret: $(1-\gamma) \cdot R_{\text{optimal}} + \gamma \cdot R_{\text{uniform}} \approx 2$--$5\%$ overhead
    \item Empirical penalty: $\approx 0\%$ (60.6 vs. 59.0)---near-optimal with full safety!
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Production Recommendation}

For 99\% of deployments, use the validated default:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
router = CorrallingRouter(
    experts=[warmup, tabula_rasa],
    gamma=0.05,  # Empirically optimal (validated)
    learning_rate=1.0
)
\end{lstlisting}

Deviate only if:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Lower $\gamma$ (0.01):} $>5$ experts requiring aggressive competition (WARNING: higher variance, risk of expert death)
    \item \textbf{Higher $\gamma$ (0.10):} Highly non-stationary environments where expert quality changes frequently (WARNING: 15\% performance penalty)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Zero-Shot Model Adoption: Extended Analysis}
\label{app:zero_shot_details}

This section provides the full ablation and mechanism validation behind the zero-shot readiness results in Section~\ref{sec:zero_shot}.

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
We simulated the release of \texttt{gpt-5.1} at $t=300$ using conservative learning ($\eta=0.1$) with $N=30$ trials (seeds 42--71). Heterogeneous expert strategy (Conservative with $\alpha$ decay $1.0 \to 0.01$, Adaptive with $\alpha=2.0$ constant). Full-episode metrics ($t=0$--$800$) reported to avoid evaluation bias. \textbf{Design limitation:} The router trains and is evaluated on the same shuffled data stream. While this is standard for online bandit evaluation (regret analysis), the reward metrics reported here conflate learning with generalization. A held-out evaluation set for this experiment was not available; results should be interpreted as online learning performance rather than generalization guarantees.

\paragraph{Ablation: Four Initialization Strategies.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} compares:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Cold Start:} Pure exploration, slow convergence
    \item \textbf{Warmup Only:} Priors for old models, cold start for new model
    \item \textbf{Small LMSys Prior:} Realistic baseline with $N_{\text{eff}}=20$ preliminary benchmarks
    \item \textbf{Semantic Transfer (Ours):} Inherits regularization from semantic neighbor
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/figure6_ablation.png}
\caption{\textbf{Zero-Shot Model Adoption Ablation ($\eta=0.1$).} When \texttt{gpt-4o} is introduced at $t=300$, semantic transfer (Blue) provides 3.2\% immediate benefit over cold start (Red) and 2.1\% over realistic baseline (Purple). Mechanism: implicit regularization via symmetry breaking ($26\times$ more initial variance), not semantic accuracy. Expert weights remain stable ($\sim$75\% Conservative, $\sim$25\% Adaptive as average across seeds), with individual seeds showing binary expert commitments (0\% or 100\%). ($N=30$ trials, 95\% CI, full episode metrics.)}
\label{fig:ablation}
\label{fig:multimodel}
\label{fig:corralling_semantic}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Mechanism Validation.}

\textbf{Original Hypothesis (Inconclusive):} ``Semantic similarity predicts task-level performance correlation.''

Direct measurement is \textbf{inconclusive due to insufficient sample size}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Correlation(embedding similarity, performance correlation) = $-0.38$, $p=0.75$ ($N=3$ models, 1 degree of freedom)
    \item \textbf{Power limitation:} With only 3 models, this test has effectively zero statistical power. The non-significant result cannot be interpreted as evidence \emph{against} the hypothesis---it simply reflects that no correlation test with $N=3$ data points can achieve significance except in extreme cases. Testing this hypothesis meaningfully would require a substantially larger model portfolio.
    \item GPT-4-Turbo and GPT-5.1 excel on completely different tasks (0\% overlap)
    \item Magnitude transfer fails: GPT-4's preferences do not transfer ($r=-0.066$, $p=0.35$)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Actual Mechanism (Validated):} \textbf{Implicit Regularization}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Symmetry Breaking:} Semantic prior provides $26\times$ more initial variance than cold start ($\sigma^2=0.1141$ vs $0.0000$)
    \item \textbf{Exploration Acceleration:} Non-zero prior breaks symmetry in LinUCB's confidence ellipsoid
    \item \textbf{Content-Agnostic:} Benefit does not require semantic prior to be \emph{correct}---any strong prior with meaningful variance helps
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Statistical Results.}
Paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction ($\alpha=0.05/4 = 0.0125$):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Warmup+Transfer vs Cold Start: $\Delta=+3.2\%$, $t_{29}=4.20$, $p=0.0002$, Cohen's $d=0.77$
    \item Warmup+Transfer vs Warmup Only: $\Delta=+0.29$, $t_{29}=4.20$, $p=0.0002$, Cohen's $d=0.77$
    \item Post-release improvement: $+0.62$ reward units ($t_{29}=6.93$, $p<10^{-7}$, Cohen's $d=1.26$)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Expert Weight Dynamics.}
Diagnostic analysis reveals individual seeds exhibit binary expert commitments (0\% or 100\%) rather than stable blending. The reported $\sim$75\%/$\sim$25\% Conservative/Adaptive split reflects heterogeneity \emph{across seeds}, not stable blending \emph{within seeds}. This binary switching is consistent with the regime-dependent behavior in Appendix~\ref{app:multiseed_sensitivity}.

\subsection{Exploration Strategy Validation}
\label{sec:alpha_ablation}

A critical design choice is the exploration-exploitation schedule. We validated constant exploration ($\alpha=2.0$) through systematic ablation under domain mismatch between offline training and holdout evaluation data.

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
Four configurations evaluated on the 750-prompt holdout set across 20 random seeds:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{\textbf{Exploration Strategy Ablation.} Constant exploration ($\alpha=2.0$) outperforms adaptive decay under severe domain mismatch by 48\%.}
\label{tab:alpha_ablation}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Configuration} & \textbf{Cumulative Regret} & \textbf{vs Best} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Homogeneous Constant ($\alpha=2.0$)} & \textbf{60.6 $\pm$ 1.4} & \textbf{--} \\
Current Design (mixed) & 64.4 $\pm$ 4.4 & +6.3\% \\
\midrule
Homogeneous Decay ($\alpha: 1.0 \to 0.01$) & 90.2 $\pm$ 7.8 & +48\% \\
Reversed Configuration & 90.8 $\pm$ 9.1 & +49\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Under severe prior mismatch, decaying $\alpha$ causes premature exploitation: the system commits to misspecified beliefs before accumulating sufficient evidence of their invalidity. Constant $\alpha=2.0$ maintains vigilance throughout deployment. This finding is specific to high-mismatch scenarios; in validated low-mismatch settings, adaptive schedules may improve sample efficiency.

\subsection{Unified Framework: Three Operating Regimes}
\label{sec:three_regimes}

Our experimental program reveals that the Corralling learning rate ($\eta$) determines the adaptation regime, enabling different operational objectives. Rather than contradictory results, the experiments across Sections~\ref{sec:pareto}--\ref{sec:robustness_adaptation} demonstrate \textbf{complementary operating regimes}.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{\textbf{Three Operating Regimes for Corralling-Based Routing}}
\label{tab:three_regimes}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Regime} & \textbf{Learning Rate} & \textbf{Timescale} & \textbf{Use Case} \\
\midrule
Cold-Start & $\eta=0.1$--$0.3$ & 0--300 steps & Exploit semantic transfer \\
Pareto Sweep & $\eta=1.0$ & 50--1,121 steps & Balance cost-quality \\
Safety & $\eta=0.3$--$1.0$ & 3--50 steps & Catastrophic failure detection \\
Convergence & $\eta=2.0$--$5.0$ & 300--1,121 steps & Adapt beyond priors \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Timescale Separation.}
The framework reveals a critical safety property: catastrophic failure detection (3--50 steps, $\eta=0.3$--$1.0$) occurs $10\times$ faster than complete prior unlearning ($\sim$300--500 steps, $\eta=5.0$). This ensures the system fails over safely \emph{even when semantic transfer is fundamentally incorrect}.

\paragraph{Deployment Recommendations.}
Practitioners should select $\eta$ based on operational objectives:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Rapid deployment / cost focus:} $\eta=0.1$--$0.3$ (exploit priors)
    \item \textbf{Balanced cost-quality:} $\eta=1.0$ (moderate adaptation)
    \item \textbf{Quality maximization:} $\eta=2.0$--$5.0$ (complete unlearning)
    \item \textbf{Safety-critical:} $\eta=0.3$ (fast detection, low false positives)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Stratified Results.}
With $N=30$ seeds, we can now estimate regime proportions with reasonable confidence:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Warmup-dominant regime:} Seeds where warmup expert is maintained. In this regime, $\neff=1.0$ outperforms $\neff=20.0$, confirming the over-confidence trap.
    \item \textbf{Tabula rasa-dominant regime:} Seeds where Corralling abandons warmup priors. Performance is identical across $\neff$ values, as semantic transfer is not used.
    \item \textbf{Overall:} The weighted aggregate impact across regimes is reported in Figure~\ref{fig:expert_selection}.
\end{itemize}

The increased sample size ($N=30$) enables meaningful estimation of regime proportions (see Appendix~\ref{app:multiseed_sensitivity} for exact counts and binomial confidence intervals). The conclusion that robustness emerges from adaptive expert selection (not parameter insensitivity) is supported both by the mechanism and by the regime-stratified analysis.
