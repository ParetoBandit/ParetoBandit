\section{Empirical Motivation: The Semantic Structure of Routing}

Before presenting our routing algorithm, we first establish that LLM routing is a structured problem amenable to learning, rather than a random optimization task.

\subsection{Bimodal Task Distribution and the Performance Paradox}

We projected 1,871 prompts from the LMSYS dev and holdout sets onto the first two principal components of a 32-component PCA model trained on sentence embeddings. Despite the 2D projection capturing only 5.39\% of the total variance, a clear bimodal semantic structure emerges (Figure~\ref{fig:pca}). The distribution reveals two distinct clusters separated by a decision boundary at $\text{PC1} \approx 0.3$:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low PC1 ``Nuance Zone'' (82.4\%)}: Represents routine conversational queries, simple creative tasks, and information requests. Crucially, the flagship model (GPT-4-Turbo) retains an advantage here (Mean Gap $+0.13$), likely due to better zero-shot coherence and world knowledge.
    
    \item \textbf{High PC1 ``Complexity Trap'' (17.6\%)}: Isolates complex coding challenges, technical documentation, and specialized reasoning. Paradoxically, the cheaper model (Mixtral-8x7B) \emph{outperforms} the flagship here (Mean Gap $-0.68$), likely due to reduced safety refusals and specialized priors.
\end{itemize}

This spatial separation reveals a \textbf{Performance Paradox} that invalidates conventional routing wisdom: a static ``hardness-based'' strategy that sends complex tasks to the expensive model would actively degrade quality on 17.6\% of traffic while wasting budget.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{figures/figure1_pca.png}
\caption{Bimodal semantic structure reveals the Performance Paradox. PCA projection of 1,871 LMSYS prompts shows two clusters: Low PC1 (82.4\%) where GPT-4-Turbo wins (Gap $+0.13$), and High PC1 (17.6\%) where Mixtral paradoxically outperforms (Gap $-0.68$). This inverts traditional routing assumptions: the ``complex'' tasks benefit from the cheaper model. Decision boundary at PC1 $\approx$ 0.3.}

\subsection{Scale Validation: The ``Economic Catastrophe''}

To ensure these findings were not an artifact of the smaller holdout set, we extended our analysis to the complete LMSYS Chat-1M dataset, comprising 594,199 unique prompts---a $317\times$ increase in scale.

Remarkably, the semantic structure exhibits \emph{spectral invariance}. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:spectral}, the decision boundary remains stable at $\text{PC1}=0.3$. However, the distribution shifts significantly: the production traffic is even more skewed toward the Low PC1 cluster (94.1\%) than the holdout set (82.4\%)---the zone where GPT-4-Turbo retains an advantage.

\textbf{Implication}: This reveals that our initial holdout evaluation represents a conservative stress test. In production (Chat-1M), the Performance Paradox creates two opposing economic pressures: (1) The High PC1 ``Complexity Trap'' shrinks to 5.9\%, but routing these tasks to GPT-4-Turbo creates a ``Quality Hole'' where users pay more for worse results. (2) The Low PC1 cluster grows to 94.1\%, requiring careful identification of the sub-manifold where Mixtral is ``good enough'' to avoid sacrificing quality. A naive ``minimize cost'' approach would fail on the latter; a naive ``hardness classifier'' would fail on the former. This necessitates adaptive, context-aware routing.

\subsection{Distribution Shift: The ``Negative Transfer'' Trap}

A fundamental challenge in routing is \emph{covariate shift}: the phenomenon where the distribution of prompts in production ($Q$) differs from the offline training data ($P$). If significant shift exists, static priors (like those used by RouteLLM) will be miscalibrated, leading to \emph{Negative Transfer}.

To quantify this, we calculated the Population Stability Index (PSI) between our Warmup Priors (Source) and the RouteLLM deployment data (Target). As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:shift}, we observe a significant domain mismatch:
\begin{equation}
\text{PSI} = \sum (\%P_i - \%Q_i) \ln(\%P_i / \%Q_i) = \mathbf{0.275}
\end{equation}

This exceeds the critical industry threshold of $0.25$, indicating substantial distribution shift.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{figures/figure2_distribution_shift.png}
\caption{Feature Distribution Shift. (Top) The deployment distribution (Red) is significantly left-shifted compared to the training data (Blue), with PSI=0.275. (Bottom) This shift is driven by a higher prevalence of Low PC1 tasks in production than anticipated.}
\label{fig:shift}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The Consequence} (Table~\ref{tab:prior_mismatch}): This shift is not harmless; it actively degrades performance.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GPT-4-Turbo}: Prior expectation $0.94$ $\to$ Observed $0.84$ (Overestimated utility).
    \item \textbf{Mixtral}: Prior expectation $0.45$ $\to$ Observed $0.81$ (Underestimated utility by $+80\%$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Conclusion}: The offline priors are structurally biased against the cheaper model. A static router trained on $P$ would erroneously route traffic to GPT-4-Turbo based on outdated ``hardness'' beliefs. This necessitates the Adaptive Corralling mechanism (Section~3) to dynamically down-weight these misspecified priors in real-time.
