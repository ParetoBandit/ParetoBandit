\subsection{Ablation Study: Pure Semantic Transfer (Corralling Disabled)}

To isolate the pure effect of the effective prior strength parameter ($\neff$) on semantic transfer performance, we conducted an ablation study with Corralling disabled (\texttt{use\_corralling=False}). This forces the system to always use the warmup expert (CostAwareLinUCBRouter with semantic transfer), removing the adaptive expert selection mechanism.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/figureS1_ablation_no_corralling.png}
    \caption{%
        \textbf{Supplementary Figure S1: Pure Semantic Transfer Without Corralling.}
        When Corralling is disabled, forcing semantic transfer for all routing decisions, we observe a clear monotonic degradation in performance as $\neff$ increases. 
        \textbf{Left panel}: Time series showing post-release performance (t>300) for five n-effective values across three seeds. 
        \textbf{Right panel}: Statistical summary showing mean ± std. 
        %
        \textbf{Key finding}: Performance spans 6.2\% from best ($\neff=1.0$: 4.508) to worst ($\neff=20.0$: 4.245), confirming the ``over-confidence trap'' hypothesis. Strong priors ($\neff \geq 10$) actually underperform the cold start baseline (4.416), demonstrating that forced semantic transfer can be harmful when prior strength is too high. The exploration bonus term $\alpha \sqrt{\xvec^T \mat{A}^{-1} \xvec} \propto 1/\sqrt{\neff}$ shrinks with increasing $\neff$, causing the system to stubbornly prefer cheaper incumbents even when the expensive new model is superior.
        %
        This ablation isolates the \textit{mechanism} of the over-confidence trap, demonstrating that $\neff$ choice \textit{does} matter when semantic transfer is forced. The absence of this effect in the production system (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity_analysis}, Table~\ref{tab:multiseed_corralling}) validates Corralling's adaptive behavior: detecting when priors fail and switching to cold-start exploration.
    }
    \label{fig:ablation_no_corralling}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Experimental Protocol.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Configuration}: \texttt{use\_corralling=False} (forced semantic transfer)
    \item \textbf{Seeds}: 42--44 (same as multi-seed validation)
    \item \textbf{n-effective values}: $\{1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0, 20.0\}$
    \item \textbf{Warmup models}: Mixtral-8x7B, GPT-4-Turbo (initialized with 40k battles)
    \item \textbf{New model}: GPT-5.1 released at $t=300$ with semantic transfer from GPT-4-Turbo
    \item \textbf{Evaluation}: Post-release performance (t=300--1000, N=700 decisions)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The monotonic degradation from $\neff=1.0$ to $\neff=20.0$ provides clear evidence that:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item The over-confidence trap is \textit{real} and \textit{measurable} (6.2\% effect size)
    \item Strong priors can be \textit{worse than no priors} when transfer is forced
    \item Weak priors ($\neff=1.0$) preserve exploration flexibility, enabling better discovery of expensive model utility
    \item This effect is \textit{masked in production} (Corralling ON) by adaptive expert selection
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Multi-Seed Validation (Production Configuration)}

Figure~\ref{fig:multiseed_validation} shows the full multi-seed results with confidence intervals for the production system configuration (Corralling enabled).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/figureS2_multiseed_validation.png}
    \caption{%
        \textbf{Supplementary Figure S2: Multi-Seed Validation with Corralling.}
        Performance across three seeds for five n-effective values in the production system (Corralling enabled). 
        \textbf{Left panel}: Individual seed trajectories showing post-release performance (t>300). 
        \textbf{Right panel}: Statistical summary with 95\% confidence intervals.
        %
        \textbf{Key finding}: No significant differences across n-effective values (all $p>0.40$, paired t-tests). The narrow performance band (mean $\pm$ CI all overlap) contrasts sharply with the 6.2\% span observed without Corralling (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation_no_corralling}). 
        %
        This apparent ``insensitivity'' reflects Corralling's adaptive behavior: seed 42 maintains warmup expert (where $\neff$ matters), while seeds 43--44 abandon it (where $\neff$ is irrelevant). The aggregate result masks the underlying regime-dependent effects revealed by stratified analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:expert_selection}).
    }
    \label{fig:multiseed_validation}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Statistical Testing.}
We performed paired t-tests comparing each n-effective value against the partial cold start baseline:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\neff=1.0$ vs baseline: $t=1.21$, $p=0.434$ (ns)
    \item $\neff=5.0$ vs baseline: $t=1.19$, $p=0.437$ (ns)
    \item $\neff=20.0$ vs baseline: $t=1.24$, $p=0.423$ (ns)
\end{itemize}

All comparisons show $p>0.40$, indicating no statistically significant differences at conventional thresholds ($\alpha=0.05$).

\paragraph{Effective Sample Size.}
The nominal sample size is N=700 post-release decisions per seed (total 2,100 decisions). However, sequential decision-making introduces autocorrelation. Using Ljung-Box test, we estimate the effective sample size as $\sim$210 per seed (autocorrelation adjustment factor $\approx 3.3$). This provides sufficient power to detect medium-to-large effects ($>$3\%) but may miss small effects ($<$2\%).

\paragraph{Why Not Significant?}
The non-significance is \textit{not} because $\neff$ doesn't matter. Rather:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item In warmup-dominant seeds (33\%), $\neff$ has +4.6\% effect
    \item In tabula rasa-dominant seeds (67\%), $\neff$ has 0\% effect
    \item Averaging across regimes: $0.33 \times 4.6\% \approx 1.5\%$ (below detection threshold)
\end{enumerate}

This demonstrates that \textbf{regime-stratified analysis is essential}. Aggregate statistics mask the true regime-dependent behavior.

\subsection{Individual Seed Trajectories}

\paragraph{Seed 42 (Warmup-Dominant Regime).}
Expert weights: Warmup expert maintained at 100\% throughout post-release evaluation (t>300). This indicates Corralling detected that warmup priors matched the data distribution during the warmup phase (t<300).

Performance by n-effective:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\neff=1.0$: 4.477 (best)
    \item $\neff=5.0$: 4.358
    \item $\neff=20.0$: 4.280 (worst)
    \item \textbf{Effect size}: +4.6\% (1.0 vs 20.0)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Seeds 43--44 (Tabula Rasa-Dominant Regime).}
Expert weights: Tabula rasa expert at 100\% throughout post-release evaluation. Corralling abandoned semantic transfer during warmup phase, indicating prior-data mismatch.

Performance by n-effective:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item $\neff=1.0$: 4.241
    \item $\neff=5.0$: 4.202
    \item $\neff=20.0$: 4.247
    \item \textbf{Effect size}: -0.1\% (essentially zero)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The binary expert selection (0\% or 100\%, never intermediate) demonstrates that Corralling's regime switching is \textit{decisive}, not gradual. The system commits to a strategy based on early performance signals and maintains that commitment throughout evaluation. This is distinct from traditional variance (stochastic fluctuation around a mean) and represents a form of \textit{discrete regime identification}.

\subsection{Cross-Experiment Consistency}

During our revision, we investigated whether the regime-switching behavior observed in this sensitivity analysis was consistent with other experiments. We re-analyzed Figure 7 data (zero-shot readiness experiment) to check for similar patterns.

\paragraph{Finding.}
Figure 7 also exhibits binary regime switching (5/5 seeds tested show expert weights at 0\% or 100\%, not intermediate blending). The previously reported ``~75\% warmup weight'' was an \textit{average across seeds with different regimes}, masking the underlying discrete switching (Simpson's Paradox).

\paragraph{Implication.}
The regime-switching behavior is not specific to the n-effective sensitivity experiment but appears to be a general characteristic of Corralling's adaptive expert selection under these experimental conditions. This validates that the mechanism is robust and generalizable.

\subsection{Limitations and Generalizability}

\paragraph{Regime Frequency.}
The observed 33\%/67\% warmup/tabula rasa split reflects the specific characteristics of our test distribution (LMSYS Chatbot Arena battles with GPT-5.1 vs GPT-4-Turbo vs Mixtral-8x7B). Different model portfolios or task distributions may exhibit different regime frequencies.

The adaptive behavior generalizes: Corralling will monitor expert performance and shift probability mass toward better-performing strategies. What varies across deployments is the \textit{frequency} of each regime, not the \textit{mechanism} of regime switching.

\paragraph{Production Recommendations.}
For practitioners:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Monitoring}: Track expert selection frequencies in deployment ($\sim$30-40\% warmup expected based on our data)
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameter Tuning}: Limited value for $\neff$ optimization (expected impact $\sim$1.5\% overall)
    \item \textbf{Focus Instead On}: Prior quality (warmup data match), learning rate ($\eta$) selection, and Corralling configuration
\end{itemize}
