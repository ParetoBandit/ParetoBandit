\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate ParetoBandit on a canonical portfolio of three LLMs, spanning a large cost differential, and using realistic prompt data to simulate both stationary and non-stationary production environments.  Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarises the data splits and model portfolio.

\subsection{Datasets and Ground Truth}

We collect 11,983 prompts from the LMSYS Chatbot Arena~\cite{zheng2023judging}, reflecting real-world, open-ended user interactions spanning coding, reasoning, and conversational tasks.  Each prompt is routed to all three models in the portfolio and evaluated by an automated LLM-as-a-judge.

\paragraph{Quality evaluation.}
We employ DeepSeek-R1~\cite{deepseekai2025deepseekr1} as the evaluator via a structured chain-of-thought (CoT) judge prompt.  The prompt first elicits a concise reasoning analysis of the response, then requests scores on three continuous dimensions ($[0,1]$): \emph{Reasoning Quality}, \emph{Instruction Following}, and \emph{Communication Quality}.  The composite scalar reward is the weighted average $r_t = 0.4 \cdot s_{\text{reason}} + 0.3 \cdot s_{\text{instr}} + 0.3 \cdot s_{\text{comm}} \in [0, 1]$.  This continuous rubric provides the granular quality distinctions necessary for multi-arm routing; in the LLM routing setting, binary win/loss outcomes collapse into ties on a large fraction of prompts because adjacent-tier models produce comparable quality on most queries, rendering binary signals insufficient for distinguishing among $K > 2$ models (Section~\ref{sec:discussion} provides a detailed analysis and supporting evidence from the literature).  The full judge prompt is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{sec:judge_robustness}.

For online learning, the bandit's convergence rate scales directly with the reward gap between the optimal and suboptimal arms~\cite{lattimore2020bandit}; we therefore select the judge that maximises routing margins.  DeepSeek-R1 produces the largest inter-model gaps and the highest fraction of actionable prompts among the judges we evaluated, while its oracle routing decisions capture $\geq 97\%$ of two independent supplementary judges' oracle reward (Appendix~\ref{sec:judge_robustness}).  Recent multi-judge aggregation methods~\cite{verga2024poll,zhao2025care} target confounder-induced bias rather than the signal compression inherent in averaging judges with different scale ranges; applied to our panel, averaging reduces routing margins by ${\sim}30\%$ (Appendix, Figure~\ref{fig:panel_signal}), making the single maximally discriminative judge the principled choice for bandit learning.

\paragraph{Data splits.}
The 11,983 prompts are partitioned into three disjoint sets via stratified sampling (by oracle difficulty, seed$=$42, 70/15/15 ratio).  No prompt appears in more than one split:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Train} ($n{=}8{,}374$): Used \emph{offline} to build warmup priors that initialise the bandit's sufficient statistics ($A_a, b_a$), addressing the cold-start problem.  Never seen during online evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Val} ($n{=}1{,}785$): Used for online learning and hyperparameter selection (Section~\ref{sec:exp4_hparam_sweep}) under stationary conditions.
  \item \textbf{Test} ($n{=}1{,}824$): Evaluation split, held out from both warmup-prior fitting and hyperparameter selection.  The bandit policy continues to receive feedback and adapt during the test trajectory, consistent with the standard cumulative-regret protocol for online learning~\cite{lattimore2020bandit}; ``held-out'' refers to data isolation, not policy freezing.  Non-stationary scenarios (reward drift, cost drift) are constructed by manipulating reward or cost columns at a mid-trajectory phase boundary.  All reported results use this split, averaged over multiple random seeds.  The val burn-in is a production-realistic protocol that adds no statistically significant advantage to test-set performance: warmup priors alone (0\% burn-in) achieve equivalent regret ($74.0$ vs.\ $74.4$, $p{=}0.64$, 20~seeds), and this finding holds under budget-paced conditions (tight: $p{=}0.40$; moderate: $p{=}0.74$); the burn-in's purpose is calibration under deployment conditions, not artificial performance inflation (Appendix~\ref{appendix:val_burnin_ablation}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Context representation.}
Contextual routing rests on the inductive bias that \emph{semantically similar prompts benefit from the same model}: a coding question, a creative-writing task, and a factual look-up each exercise different model capabilities, so a representation that captures these semantic axes gives the bandit a signal for differential routing.
Dense sentence embeddings are the consensus feature choice across the LLM routing literature---adopted by supervised systems (RouteLLM~\cite{ong2024routellm}, RouterDC~\cite{chen2024routerdc}, GraphRouter~\cite{feng2025graphrouter}), bandit-based routers (PILOT~\cite{panda2025pilot}, MixLLM~\cite{wang2025mixllm}), and RL approaches (PROTEUS~\cite{bhatti2026proteus})---because they compress a variable-length prompt into a fixed-dimensional vector that preserves the task-level distinctions relevant to model selection.

Each prompt is encoded by the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} SentenceTransformer~\cite{reimers2019sentence} (384-D), L2-normalized, and projected to a 25-dimensional context vector via PCA, followed by a bias term ($d{=}26$).
Dimensionality reduction is essential because LinUCB maintains a $d \times d$ design matrix $A_a$ per arm, making per-step updates $O(d^2)$; projecting from $384$ to $25$ dimensions reduces this cost by ${\sim}235\times$.
We choose PCA over nonlinear alternatives (e.g., autoencoders) because LinUCB assumes a linear reward model $\mathbb{E}[r \mid x] = \theta^\top x$; PCA is the variance-maximizing linear projection, preserving the most signal under the same linearity assumption the bandit already makes.
Although 25 components retain only ${\sim}29\%$ of total embedding variance, the routing-relevant signal---which dimensions of prompt semantics predict \emph{inter-model} reward gaps---concentrates in the leading principal components; a PCA-dimension ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:pca_ablation}) confirms the Pareto AUC surface is flat across $d \in [6, 25]$, indicating that the remaining $71\%$ of variance encodes semantic distinctions orthogonal to routing quality.
After projection, each PCA coordinate is divided by the square root of its explained variance (\emph{whitening}), yielding approximately zero-mean, unit-variance features.
This whitening step is critical for LinUCB: the isotropic prior $A_0 = \lambda I$ implicitly assumes equal feature scale across dimensions; without whitening, leading principal components dominate the UCB exploration bonus and distort arm selection.
Intuitively, PCA strips away the embedding dimensions that carry noise rather than routing-relevant signal, while whitening normalises the scale of every surviving feature so that no single component dominates the bandit's uncertainty estimates.
Together, the two steps ensure that LinUCB's exploration--exploitation balance is governed by the tunable hyperparameters ($\alpha$, $\gamma$, $n_\text{eff}$) rather than by artefacts of the raw feature geometry.
Crucially, the PCA projection matrix is fitted \emph{once}, \emph{offline}, on ${\sim}46{,}000$ LMSYS Arena prompts that are \textbf{strictly disjoint} from all three experimental splits (train/val/test).
During all experiments the PCA transform and whitening scales are frozen and applied as inference-only projections; no component of the feature pipeline is re-fitted on the evaluation data.

\subsection{Environment Construction}

The $K{=}3$ portfolio spans three distinct cost tiers (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}, right).  The $500\times$ price differential between Llama-3.1-8B and Gemini-2.5-Pro stresses the router's ability to balance quality and cost efficiently.

\paragraph{Non-stationary evaluation protocol.}
A key contribution of this work is the systematic evaluation of LLM routers under non-stationary conditions.
Existing bandit-based routing methods---PILOT~\cite{panda2025pilot}, BaRP~\cite{wang2025barp}, LLM Bandit~\cite{li2025llmbandit}---evaluate exclusively under stationary assumptions: model quality and pricing are fixed throughout the entire experimental trajectory.
Yet the production LLM landscape is inherently non-stationary: providers update model weights, adjust RLHF procedures, and alter pricing on timescales of weeks~\cite{chen2023chatgptdrift}, and silent quality degradation after provider-side updates has been empirically documented~\cite{chen2025silent}.
To our knowledge, no prior LLM routing work has experimentally evaluated router behaviour under reward or cost non-stationarity.
We adopt the standard three-phase stress-test protocol from the non-stationary multi-armed bandit literature~\cite{garivier2011switching}---normal operation, abrupt perturbation, recovery---which provides clean phase boundaries for measuring detection latency, adaptation speed, and steady-state compliance.

We construct two distinct non-stationary test environments to evaluate the router's robustness:
1. \textbf{Cost Drift (Price Drop)}: Simulates an API price cut. The test trajectory ($n{=}1{,}824$) is divided into three 608-prompt phases. Phase~1 uses normal pricing; Phase~2 drops Gemini-2.5-Pro's price to \$0.0001 per request; Phase~3 restores the original pricing. Phase~3 reuses Phase~1 prompts, providing a controlled within-subject design.
2. \textbf{Catastrophic Model Failure}: Simulates a production API outage where Mistral-Large begins returning garbage responses. The test trajectory ($n{=}1{,}824$) is similarly divided into three phases of 608~prompts each. Phase~1 uses normal conditions; Phase~2 degrades Mistral-Large's reward to $0.05$ and cost to \$0 (the provider does not charge for failed requests); Phase~3 restores Mistral to normal operation (reusing Phase~1 prompts for controlled comparison). A separate reward-drift scenario (Llama--Mistral reward swap) is presented in Appendix~\ref{appendix:reward_swap}.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare ParetoBandit against three baselines representing increasing levels of routing sophistication:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Fixed Policy (Offline):} Warmup priors deployed
    without online learning ($\gamma{=}1.0$, updates disabled)
    and a static cost penalty $\lambda_s$.  The industry standard:
    deploy an offline-trained router with a hand-tuned cost weight.
  \item \textbf{Naive Bandit ($\gamma{=}1.0$):} LinUCB with warmup
    priors and online learning, but infinite memory (no forgetting)
    and a static cost penalty.  The approach taken by existing
    bandit-based LLM
    routers~\cite{panda2025pilot,wang2025barp,li2025llmbandit}.
  \item \textbf{SW-UCB ($W{=}200$):} Sliding-Window
    LinUCB~\cite{garivier2011switching} without warmup priors
    ($\alpha{=}0.01$).  Retains only the last $W$ observations
    with equal weighting.  Window size $W{=}200$ matches
    ParetoBandit's asymptotic effective sample size
    $1/(1{-}\gamma){=}200$---the standard equivalence between
    exponential and rectangular windows---ensuring equal estimation
    precision while isolating the forgetting shape and initialisation
    as the experimental variables.  Used in the appendix
    reward-swap experiment (Appendix~\ref{appendix:reward_swap}).
  \item \textbf{ParetoBandit ($\gamma{=}0.995$):} Our proposed system,
    initialized with warmup priors, utilizing geometric forgetting,
    and governed by the Primal-Dual \texttt{BudgetPacer} targeting
    a specific dollar budget.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
All prior-initialised conditions (Fixed, Naive, ParetoBandit) share
$\alpha{=}0.1$ and $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$.
The \emph{prior strength} $n_\mathit{eff}$~\cite{kveton2023warmstart} is a robustness knob that governs how quickly online evidence can override the offline warmup priors.
This tunability is essential because priors are trained on historical data that may already be partially stale by deployment time: in the LLM ecosystem, providers refresh model weights, adjust RLHF procedures, and shift pricing on timescales of weeks~\cite{chen2023chatgptdrift,thompson2026timeline}, so an offline prior's quality estimates carry inherent uncertainty about their continued accuracy.
$n_\mathit{eff}$ sets the effective number of pseudo-observations the prior contributes, directly controlling how long offline estimates dominate over incoming online evidence.
A high $n_\mathit{eff}$ keeps the router close to its prior policy for many steps---stable early routing, but slow to correct if the prior is wrong; a low $n_\mathit{eff}$ lets the router begin learning from live traffic almost immediately, acting as a safety valve against prior--reality mismatch at the cost of noisier early decisions (Appendix~\ref{appendix:prior_mismatch} quantifies this interaction empirically).
With $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$ and a validation split of $1{,}785$ prompts, the prior is influential during early deployment but transient by design: geometric forgetting ($\gamma{=}0.995$) gradually replaces offline estimates with online evidence over ${\sim}200$ effective-memory steps.  After sufficient online learning, the router's steady-state quality is determined by recent observations regardless of initialisation---a burn-in ablation (Appendix~\ref{appendix:val_burnin_ablation}) confirms that warmup priors alone (0\% burn-in) achieve the same test regret as the full protocol ($74.0$ vs.\ $74.4$, $p{=}0.64$), while a cold start without priors incurs $+10.0\%$ higher regret ($p < 10^{-5}$).  The priors' value is thus a \emph{deployment latency} gain: informed routing from request~\#1.
SW-UCB uses $\alpha{=}0.01$ (appropriate for cold-start without
priors, where no warmup information is available to stabilise early routing).
Experiment~2 additionally includes a \textbf{Recalibrated Bandit}
that re-tunes its static penalty at the Phase~2 boundary, serving
as the strongest static-penalty baseline for cost-drift evaluation.

\subsection{Metrics}

We evaluate the systems using three primary metrics and four supplementary routing metrics standard in the LLM routing literature~\cite{ong2024routellm,ding2024hybrid}:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Cumulative Regret:} Measured against a cost-adjusted oracle that selects the model maximizing $r_{a,t} - \lambda_s \tilde{c}_a$ at each step $t$ (where $\lambda_s$ is a shared static penalty). Lower regret indicates better routing decisions relative to the optimal choice.
  \item \textbf{Budget Compliance:} The ratio of actual realized cost to the target budget $B$. A perfect pacer achieves a ratio $\leq 1.0\times$, maximizing quality without overspending. Ratios $>1.0\times$ indicate budget violations.
  \item \textbf{Pareto AUC:} The area under the quality--cost Pareto frontier, measuring the router's efficiency across all possible cost trade-offs. Higher AUC indicates better quality at lower costs.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
To facilitate comparison with the LLM routing literature, we additionally report four metrics evaluated on the Pareto frontier produced by each method (Table~\ref{tab:routing_metrics}):
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{cost@$Q$\%\,qual} ($\downarrow$): the cost on the Pareto frontier at $Q$\% of per-prompt oracle quality (the reward obtained by always selecting the highest-quality model for each prompt).  Measures how cheaply a method reaches a given quality threshold.
  \item \textbf{qual@$C$\%\,cost} ($\uparrow$): the quality achieved at $C$\% of per-prompt oracle cost.  Measures quality at a fixed cost fraction.
  \item \textbf{Save@$Q$\%} ($\uparrow$): fractional cost reduction at $Q$\% of oracle quality relative to the oracle cost, i.e.\ $1 - \text{cost@}Q / \text{cost}_{\text{oracle}}$.
  \item \textbf{AUCPC} ($\uparrow$): the normalised Area Under the Cost--Performance Curve~\cite{ding2024hybrid}, integrated over the model-cost range $[\text{cheapest model cost},\, \text{most expensive model cost}]$ and scaled to $[0,1]$ using cheapest-model quality and oracle quality as anchors.
\end{enumerate}
