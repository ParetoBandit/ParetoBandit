\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate BanditGPT on a canonical portfolio of three LLMs, spanning a large cost differential, and using realistic prompt data to simulate both stationary and non-stationary production environments.  Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarises the data splits and model portfolio.

\subsection{Datasets and Ground Truth}

We collect 11,983 prompts from the LMSYS Chatbot Arena~\cite{zheng2023judging}, reflecting real-world, open-ended user interactions spanning coding, reasoning, and conversational tasks.  Each prompt is routed to all three models in the portfolio and evaluated by an automated LLM-as-a-judge.

\paragraph{Quality evaluation.}
We employ DeepSeek-R1~\cite{deepseekai2025deepseekr1} as the evaluator via a structured chain-of-thought (CoT) judge prompt.  The prompt first elicits a concise reasoning analysis of the response, then requests scores on three continuous dimensions ($[0,1]$): \emph{Reasoning Quality}, \emph{Instruction Following}, and \emph{Communication Quality}.  The composite scalar reward is the weighted average $r_t = 0.4 \cdot s_{\text{reason}} + 0.3 \cdot s_{\text{instr}} + 0.3 \cdot s_{\text{comm}} \in [0, 1]$.  This continuous rubric provides the granular quality distinctions necessary for multi-arm routing; binary win/loss outcomes collapse into ties on a large fraction of prompts and are insufficient for $K > 2$ portfolios.  The full judge prompt is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{sec:judge_robustness}.

For online learning, the bandit's convergence rate scales directly with the reward gap between the optimal and suboptimal arms~\cite{lattimore2020bandit}; we therefore select the judge that maximises routing margins.  DeepSeek-R1 produces the largest inter-model gaps and the highest fraction of actionable prompts among the judges we evaluated, while its oracle routing decisions capture $\geq 97\%$ of two independent supplementary judges' oracle reward (Appendix~\ref{sec:judge_robustness}).  Recent multi-judge aggregation methods~\cite{verga2024poll,zhao2025care} target confounder-induced bias rather than the signal compression inherent in averaging judges with different scale ranges; applied to our panel, averaging reduces routing margins by ${\sim}30\%$ (Appendix, Figure~\ref{fig:panel_signal}), making the single maximally discriminative judge the principled choice for bandit learning.

\paragraph{Data splits.}
The 11,983 prompts are partitioned into three disjoint sets via stratified sampling (by oracle difficulty, seed$=$42, 70/15/15 ratio).  No prompt appears in more than one split:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Train} ($n{=}8{,}374$): Used \emph{offline} to build warmup priors that initialise the bandit's sufficient statistics ($A_a, b_a$), addressing the cold-start problem.  Never seen during online evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Val} ($n{=}1{,}785$): Used for online learning and hyperparameter selection (Section~\ref{sec:exp4_hparam_sweep}) under stationary conditions.
  \item \textbf{Test} ($n{=}1{,}824$): Held-out evaluation split.  Non-stationary scenarios (reward drift, cost drift) are constructed by manipulating reward or cost columns at a mid-trajectory phase boundary.  All reported results use this split, averaged over multiple random seeds.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Context representation.}
Each prompt is encoded by the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} SentenceTransformer (384-D) and projected to a 25-dimensional context vector via PCA, followed by a bias term ($d{=}26$).
Crucially, the PCA projection matrix is fitted \emph{once}, \emph{offline}, on ${\sim}46{,}000$ LMSYS Arena prompts that are \textbf{strictly disjoint} from all three experimental splits (train/val/test).
During all experiments the PCA transform is frozen and applied as an inference-only projection; no component of the feature pipeline is re-fitted on the evaluation data.
A PCA-dimension ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:pca_ablation}) confirms the Pareto AUC surface is flat across $d \in [6, 25]$.

\subsection{Environment Construction}

The $K{=}3$ portfolio spans three distinct cost tiers (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}, right).  The $500\times$ price differential between Llama-3.1-8B and Gemini-2.5-Pro stresses the router's ability to balance quality and cost efficiently.

We construct two distinct non-stationary test environments to evaluate the router's robustness:
1. \textbf{Reward Drift (Model Quality Shift)}: Simulates a silent API update where model quality changes mid-deployment. The test trajectory ($n=1,785$) is split into two phases. Phase 1 (steps 1--893) uses normal rewards where Mistral-Large is the utility-optimal model. In Phase 2 (steps 894--1,785), Llama-8B and Mistral-Large exchange their reward distributions, simulating a scenario where Mistral degrades to the worst option while Llama becomes the best. Gemini-Pro serves as an unchanged anchor.
2. \textbf{Cost Drift (Price Drop)}: Simulates an API price cut. The test trajectory ($n=1,824$) is similarly split. Phase 1 (steps 1--912) uses normal pricing. In Phase 2 (steps 913--1,824), Gemini-2.5-Pro's price drops to \$0.0001 per request, drastically altering the optimal cost-quality trade-off.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare BanditGPT against three baselines representing increasing levels of routing sophistication:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Fixed Policy (Offline):} Warmup priors deployed
    without online learning ($\gamma{=}1.0$, updates disabled)
    and a static cost penalty $\lambda_s$.  The industry standard:
    deploy an offline-trained router with a hand-tuned cost weight.
  \item \textbf{Naive Bandit ($\gamma{=}1.0$):} LinUCB with warmup
    priors and online learning, but infinite memory (no forgetting)
    and a static cost penalty.  The approach taken by existing
    bandit-based LLM
    routers~\cite{panda2025pilot,wang2025barp,li2025llmbandit}.
  \item \textbf{SW-UCB ($W{=}200$):} Sliding-Window
    LinUCB~\cite{garivier2011switching} without warmup priors
    ($\alpha{=}0.01$).  Retains only the last $W$ observations
    with equal weighting.  Window size is matched to BanditGPT's
    effective memory (${\sim}200$ steps) for a controlled
    comparison of the forgetting mechanism against an established
    non-stationary bandit algorithm.  Used in the reward-drift
    experiment (Section~\ref{sec:exp2_reward_shift}).
  \item \textbf{BanditGPT ($\gamma{=}0.995$):} Our proposed system,
    initialized with warmup priors, utilizing geometric forgetting,
    and governed by the Primal-Dual \texttt{BudgetPacer} targeting
    a specific dollar budget.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
All prior-initialised conditions (Fixed, Naive, BanditGPT) share
$\alpha{=}0.1$ and $n_\mathit{eff}{=}1{,}000$.
SW-UCB uses $\alpha{=}0.01$ (appropriate for cold-start without
priors).
Experiment~3 additionally includes a \textbf{Recalibrated Bandit}
that re-tunes its static penalty at the Phase~2 boundary, serving
as the strongest static-penalty baseline for cost-drift evaluation.

\subsection{Metrics}

We evaluate the systems using three primary metrics:
1. \textbf{Cumulative Regret:} Measured against a cost-adjusted oracle that selects the model maximizing $r_{a,t} - \lambda_s \tilde{c}_a$ at each step $t$ (where $\lambda_s$ is a shared static penalty). Lower regret indicates better routing decisions relative to the optimal choice.
2. \textbf{Budget Compliance:} The ratio of actual realized cost to the target budget $B$. A perfect pacer achieves a ratio $\leq 1.0\times$, maximizing quality without overspending. Ratios $>1.0\times$ indicate budget violations.
3. \textbf{Pareto AUC:} The area under the quality-cost Pareto frontier, measuring the router's efficiency across all possible cost trade-offs. Higher AUC indicates better quality at lower costs.
