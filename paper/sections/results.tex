We evaluate banditGPT on three critical dimensions: economic efficiency (Pareto optimality), algorithmic safety (robustness to alignment traps), and operational agility (adaptation to new models).

\subsubsection{The ``Over-Provisioning Penalty'' of Static Routing}

A critical finding is the non-monotonic behavior of the RouteLLM benchmark. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}, RouteLLM's performance collapses when the threshold is lowered to admit more GPT-4 traffic ($\tau < 0.10$).

\textbf{The Phenomenon}: Users paying \$0.013 (Maximum Cost) receive a reward of 0.812---worse than the \$0.0003 Mistral baseline (0.823). We term this the ``Over-Provisioning Penalty'': paying a $43\times$ cost premium to \emph{decrease} quality by 1.3\%.

\textbf{The Cause}: Static routers interpolate based on general ``hardness.'' They fail to distinguish the Alignment Tax Cluster (High PC1)---where GPT-4's verbosity degrades performance---from the Natural Language Cluster (Low PC1) where GPT-4 is genuinely superior.

\textbf{The Solution}: banditGPT (Blue Line) learns this inversion online. By using GPT-4 only when statistically justified, it achieves a peak reward of 0.909, enabling a +10.4\% quality gain while still reducing costs by 27\% compared to the naive GPT-4 baseline.

\subsection{Algorithmic Safety: Adaptive Prior Decommissioning}

While Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} demonstrates average efficiency, production systems require worst-case safety. A critical vulnerability is \emph{Negative Transfer}: if the offline priors are misspecified (e.g., trained on ``Hard'' data but deployed on ``Alignment Tax'' traffic), the router may stubbornly over-provision resources.

Figure~\ref{fig:decommission} visualizes the internal dynamics of our Corralling Algorithm handling this exact scenario.

\begin{figure}[t]

However, in the Alignment Tax Zone (High PC1), GPT-4 actually performs worse than Mixtral ($\Delta = -0.68$). A static prior would disastrously route these tasks to the expensive model.

\subsection{Operational Agility: Zero-Shot Readiness}

To simulate the rapid release cycle of LLMs (e.g., a ``Mid-Flight Model Drop''), we introduced a new model into the registry at $t=300$. Figure~\ref{fig:zeroshot} compares the standard ``Cold Start'' approach against our Latent Semantic Transfer protocol.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/figure7_zero_shot.png}
\caption{Zero-Shot Readiness. When a new model is released at $t=300$, the Cold Start baseline (Red) suffers a catastrophic regression ($3.3 \to 1.7$ reward), requiring 500 steps to recover. In contrast, Latent Semantic Transfer (Green) maintains performance ($\sim 4.5$) by inheriting the ``intuition'' of a semantic neighbor.}
\label{fig:zeroshot}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The Cold Start Dip}: The baseline LinUCB router (Red Line) suffers a catastrophic regression, with average reward plummeting from $3.3 \to 1.7$ immediately after the new model is added. This represents the ``cost of exploration,'' as the router essentially fumbles in the dark to learn the new model's utility.

\textbf{The Transfer Advantage}: The banditGPT router (Green Line) exhibits Zero-Shot Readiness, maintaining a high stable reward ($\sim 4.5$) with no discernible dip. By inheriting the preference vector $\thetavec$ of its semantic neighbor (GPT-4-Turbo) while resetting its confidence $\Amat$, the router effectively ``hits the ground running''.
