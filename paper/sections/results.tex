We evaluate banditGPT on three critical dimensions: economic efficiency (Pareto optimality), algorithmic safety (robustness to alignment traps), and operational agility (adaptation to new models).

\subsubsection{The ``Over-Provisioning Penalty'' of Static Routing}

A critical finding is the non-monotonic behavior of the RouteLLM benchmark. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}, RouteLLM's performance collapses when the threshold is lowered to admit more GPT-4 traffic ($\tau < 0.10$).

\textbf{The Phenomenon}: Users paying \$0.013 (Maximum Cost) receive a reward of 0.812---worse than the \$0.0003 Mistral baseline (0.823). We term this the ``Over-Provisioning Penalty'': paying a $43\times$ cost premium to \emph{decrease} quality by 1.3\%.

\textbf{The Cause}: Static routers interpolate based on general ``hardness.'' They fail to distinguish the Alignment Tax Cluster (High PC1)---where GPT-4's verbosity degrades performance---from the Natural Language Cluster (Low PC1) where GPT-4 is genuinely superior.

\textbf{The Solution}: banditGPT (Blue Line) learns this inversion online. By using GPT-4 only when statistically justified, it achieves a peak reward of 0.909, enabling a +10.4\% quality gain while still reducing costs by 27\% compared to the naive GPT-4 baseline.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Readiness: Accelerating Adoption}
\label{sec:zero_shot}

A critical challenge in production routing is the ``Cold Start Dip'' associated with deploying new models. Traditional bandits ($\mu=0, \sigma=\infty$) require an exploration period that degrades user experience before discovering the new model's quality.

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
We simulated the release of \texttt{gpt-5.1} at $t=300$. To ensure methodological rigor, we filtered performance metrics using a \textbf{covariate-based complexity filter} (selecting prompts with logic/code keywords) rather than outcome-based filtering, avoiding circular reasoning.

\paragraph{The ``Dominance'' Challenge.}
Analysis of the holdout set revealed that \texttt{gpt-5.1} is trivially dominant on complex tasks (99.0\% win rate), rendering traditional task-correlation metrics undefined ($r \approx 0$). In such a ``flat'' reward landscape, the challenge is not learning \textit{nuanced} preferences, but achieving \textbf{Adoption Velocity}---how quickly can the system identify and switch to the new dominant model?

\paragraph{Results.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} compares three admission strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cold Start:} The router explores randomly. Reward converges slowly as the bandit accumulates evidence of \texttt{gpt-5.1}'s superiority.
    \item \textbf{Warmup Only:} The router has priors for old models but treats \texttt{gpt-5.1} as unknown. It suffers the same exploration penalty as Cold Start.
    \item \textbf{Latent Semantic Initialization (Ours):} By initializing \texttt{gpt-5.1} with the preference vector ($\thetavec$) of its semantic neighbor (\texttt{gpt-4-turbo}), we establish a \textbf{``Prior of Competence.''}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/figure6_ablation_final.png}
\caption{\textbf{Zero-Shot Readiness: Accelerating Adoption of Dominant Models.} When \texttt{gpt-5.1} is released at $t=300$, Cold Start (Red) and Warmup Only (Orange) require extensive exploration to discover the new model's superiority. In contrast, Latent Semantic Initialization (Green) achieves instant convergence by transferring a ``Prior of Competence'' from the semantic neighbor (\texttt{gpt-4-turbo}). Even in a flat reward landscape where task correlation is undefined ($r \approx 0$), the transferred magnitude enables immediate adoption, eliminating exploration lag. (N=30 trials, 95\% CI, Complex subset via covariate filter)}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}, our method bypasses the exploration phase entirely. Even though the transferred $\thetavec$ does not capture perfect task correlation (due to the target's dominance), it successfully transfers the \textit{magnitude} of belief---identifying the new model as a ``Flagship-tier'' candidate immediately. This results in near-instant convergence to the optimal policy, eliminating the adoption lag seen in baselines.

\subsubsection{Robustness: Prior Strength Calibration}

A potential concern with semantic transfer is brittleness to the \textit{effective prior strength} $\neff$, which governs confidence in the transferred preference vector. To validate robustness, we simulate releasing \texttt{gpt-4-turbo} at $t=300$ and sweep $\neff \in \{1, 2, 5, 10, 20\}$ on 1,000 real LMSYS battles.

The narrow robustness band (shaded, $\neff \in [2,20]$) maintains $\geq$+12.4\%, confirming the method is not brittle.

\label{fig:sensitivity}

\paragraph{The Over-Confidence Trap.}
Surprisingly, \textbf{weak priors outperform strong priors}: $\neff=1.0$ achieves +17.6\% over baseline, while $\neff=20.0$ achieves only +12.4\%. The degradation occurs because high $\neff$ inflates $\mat{A}_{\text{new}} = \neff \cdot \mat{A}_{\text{neighbor}}$, shrinking LinUCB's exploration bonus. For expensive new models, cost penalties dominate, trapping the system in local optima despite accurate semantic signals.

\paragraph{Design Insight.}
Semantic transfer succeeds by transferring \textit{direction} ($\thetavec_{\text{neighbor}}$), not rigid confidence. Setting $\neff=1.0$ preserves exploration flexibility while capturing similarity, achieving optimal adoption velocity with no manual tuning required. The narrow robustness band (5.2pp worst-case degradation) confirms the system is production-ready across diverse model releases.

\subsection{Exploration Strategy Validation}
\label{sec:alpha_ablation}

A critical design choice in our Corralling architecture is the exploration-exploitation schedule. To ensure optimal performance under domain uncertainty, we systematically validated our constant exploration design ($\alpha=2.0$) for both experts through comprehensive ablation studies under the severe domain mismatch in our deployment scenario (PSI=0.275, 68.6\%→13.7\% hard prompt shift).

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
We evaluated four configurations on the 750-prompt holdout set across 5 random seeds:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Homogeneous Constant}: Both experts with $\alpha=2.0$ (constant)
    \item \textbf{Current Design}: Warmup $\alpha: 1.0 \to 0.01$ (decay), Tabula Rasa $\alpha=2.0$ (constant)
    \item \textbf{Homogeneous Decay}: Both experts with $\alpha: 1.0 \to 0.01$ (adaptive decay)
    \item \textbf{Reversed}: Warmup $\alpha=2.0$, Tabula Rasa $\alpha: 1.0 \to 0.01$
\end{enumerate}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{\textbf{Exploration Strategy Ablation Study.} Constant exploration ($\alpha=2.0$) outperforms adaptive decay under severe domain mismatch. Premature exploitation (decaying $\alpha$) causes the system to commit to misspecified priors, degrading performance by 48\%.}
\label{tab:alpha_ablation}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Configuration} & \textbf{Cumulative Regret} & \textbf{vs Best} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Homogeneous Constant ($\alpha=2.0$)} & \textbf{60.6 ± 1.4} & \textbf{--} \\
Current Design (mixed) & 64.4 ± 4.4 & +6.3\% \\
\midrule
Homogeneous Decay ($\alpha: 1.0 \to 0.01$) & 90.2 ± 7.8 & +48\% \\
Reversed Configuration & 90.8 ± 9.1 & +49\% \\

\paragraph{Validated Design: Constant Exploration Optimizes Performance.}
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:alpha_ablation}, homogeneous constant exploration achieves the lowest regret (60.6 ± 1.4), outperforming our original mixed design by 6.3\%. More dramatically, both configurations using adaptive decay degrade performance by approximately 48\% (90.2 vs 60.6 regret). Under severe prior mismatch, decaying $\alpha$ causes premature exploitation: the system commits to misspecified beliefs before accumulating sufficient evidence of their invalidity.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Standard bandit algorithms employ adaptive decay schedules (e.g., $\alpha_t = \alpha_0/\sqrt{t}$) to transition from exploration to exploitation. However, when warmup priors are substantially misaligned with deployment, this premature convergence is catastrophic. Constant $\alpha=2.0$ maintains vigilance throughout the deployment, preventing the system from "locking in" to harmful beliefs. This finding is specific to high-mismatch scenarios; in validated low-mismatch settings, adaptive schedules may improve sample efficiency.

\subsection{Practical Deployment Recommendations}
\label{sec:practical}

Our experimental validation reveals actionable guidelines for practitioners deploying adaptive LLM routing under domain uncertainty.

\paragraph{Strategy Selection Based on Prior Quality.}
While Corralling provides robust performance (59.2 regret in our experiments), practitioners with validated prior quality can optimize their strategy selection. Our convergence analysis demonstrates that pure Tabula Rasa achieves 49.5 ± 2.8 regret when priors are \emph{known} to be severely misspecified---16\% better than Corralling's hedging overhead. Conversely, Corralling provides 18.5\% safety improvement when prior quality is \emph{uncertain} (59.2 vs 74.7 for harmful warmup-only).

\textbf{Recommendation}: Validate prior quality on N=100--200 held-out deployment samples. If warmup predictions achieve $>$80\% accuracy, warmup-only is optimal; if $<$50\%, pure Tabula Rasa outperforms hedging; otherwise, Corralling provides robust safety.

\paragraph{Constant Exploration Under Uncertainty.}
Configuration guideline: Use $\alpha=2.0$ (constant) for both experts when deploying under domain uncertainty. Our ablation (§\ref{sec:alpha_ablation}) validates 48\% improvement over adaptive decay. This prevents premature commitment to misspecified beliefs while maintaining sufficient exploration to detect distribution shifts.

\paragraph{Real-Time Monitoring for Rapid Iteration.}
Our temporal analysis reveals expert weight adaptation occurs in approximately 16 ± 14 requests---significantly faster than anticipated. This enables real-time deployment monitoring: warmup weight $<$0.2 indicates harmful priors (consider switching to Tabula Rasa); $>$0.8 indicates accurate priors (consider simplifying to warmup-only). The high variance in weight evolution (0.382 ± 0.471) underscores the importance of monitoring individual deployments rather than relying on population-level predictions.

\subsection{Limitations and Applicability}
\label{sec:limitations}

While our experimental validation demonstrates effectiveness under severe domain mismatch, several important limitations warrant discussion.

\paragraph{Prior Quality Dependency.}
Our constant exploration finding ($\alpha=2.0$) is validated under severe domain mismatch (PSI=0.275, 68.6\%→13.7\% hard prompt shift). Under such conditions, maintaining vigilance prevents catastrophic commitment to misspecified priors. However, this finding may not generalize to scenarios with accurate priors and low mismatch. When warmup data closely matches deployment, adaptive exploration schedules may improve sample efficiency by reducing unnecessary exploration once accurate preferences are learned. Future work should investigate mismatch-aware $\alpha$ schedules that adjust exploration dynamically based on detected prior quality.

\paragraph{Strategy Selection Trade-offs.}
Our experiments reveal that Corralling's 20\% overhead relative to optimal single-expert strategies (49.5 vs 59.2 regret) is justified only under genuine uncertainty. When practitioners can validate prior quality on held-out deployment data, strategy selection optimizes performance: pure Tabula Rasa for known-bad priors (16\% improvement), warmup-only for validated-good priors. The value of Corralling lies in its safety guarantee (18.5\% vs harmful warmup) when prior quality cannot be pre-validated---most valuable in risk-averse deployments where catastrophic performance is unacceptable.

\paragraph{Variance and Reproducibility.}
Multi-seed validation reveals substantial variance in expert weight evolution: final warmup weight is 0.382 ± 0.471 (standard deviation exceeds mean). This seed-dependency indicates outcomes are influenced by stochastic request ordering. Practitioners cannot rely on deterministic trajectories (e.g., "weight will be 0.2 after 200 requests") and must instead monitor deployment-specific weight evolution with appropriate confidence intervals.
