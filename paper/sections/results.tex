\subsubsection{The ``Over-Provisioning Penalty'' of Static Routing}

A critical finding is the non-monotonic behavior of the RouteLLM benchmark. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}, RouteLLM's performance collapses when the threshold is lowered to admit more GPT-4 traffic ($\tau < 0.10$).

\textbf{The Phenomenon}: Users paying \$0.013 (Maximum Cost) receive a reward of 0.812---worse than the \$0.0003 Mistral baseline (0.823). We term this the ``Over-Provisioning Penalty'': paying a $43\times$ cost premium to \emph{decrease} quality by 1.3\%.

\textbf{The Cause}: Static routers interpolate based on general ``hardness.'' They fail to distinguish the Alignment Tax Cluster (High PC1)---where GPT-4's verbosity degrades performance---from the Natural Language Cluster (Low PC1) where GPT-4 is genuinely superior.

\textbf{The Solution}: banditGPT (Blue Line) learns this inversion online. By using GPT-4 only when statistically justified, it achieves a peak reward of $0.912 \pm 0.006$ (mean $\pm$ 95\% CI, $n=5$ trials), enabling a +12.3\% quality gain over Mixtral while reducing costs by 27\% compared to the naive GPT-4 baseline. Error bars in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} confirm this advantage is statistically robust.

\subsubsection{Robustness to the ``Alignment Trap''}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:distribution_shift}, our deployment exhibits severe domain mismatch ($\text{PSI}=0.275$), where the Warmup Prior anticipates that complex tasks require GPT-4. However, in the Alignment Tax Zone (High PC1), GPT-4 actually performs worse than Mixtral ($\Delta = -0.68$). A static prior would disastrously route these tasks to the expensive model.

Table~\ref{tab:performance_gap} quantifies the impact of this mismatch and the robustness of our Corralling strategy. Multi-seed evaluation (N=10 seeds) with rigorous statistical testing reveals no significant difference between learning rates (p=0.63), but a fundamental stability-performance tradeoff.

\paragraph{Learning Rate Sensitivity and Risk Tolerance.}
Table~\ref{tab:performance_gap} compares two learning rates across 10 random seeds. Independent t-tests reveal no statistically significant difference in mean performance ($\eta=0.1$: 45.2$\pm$7.9 vs $\eta=1.0$: 48.1$\pm$16.8, t=-0.49, p=0.63, Cohen's d=-0.22). However, the distributions differ substantially:

Conservative learning ($\eta=0.1$) offers \textbf{stability}: mean=45.2, median=45.0, range=[33, 60], CV=17\%. Performance is consistent across all seeds with no catastrophic failures (max=60).

Aggressive learning ($\eta=1.0$) offers \textbf{better median performance}: median=41.0 (1.03$\times$ vs baseline), but with 2$\times$ higher variance (std=16.8, CV=35\%) and occasional catastrophic failures---2 of 10 seeds exceeded 75 regret, approaching the harmful warmup baseline (79 regret).

\textbf{Recommendation:} For production deployments requiring reliability and predictable performance, we recommend $\eta=0.1$. For research settings or risk-tolerant applications where median performance is prioritized and occasional failures are acceptable, $\eta=1.0$ may be preferable. Both learning rates achieve the core safety guarantee: 39-43\% improvement over harmful warmup priors.

\textbf{The Cost of Rigid Priors}: The ``Warmup Only'' baseline suffers catastrophic regret (79.0 total). Because it confidently exploits a misspecified prior (Alignment = 0.48), it aggressively routes ``Alignment Tax'' prompts to GPT-4, wasting budget.

\textbf{The Corralling Safety Net}: In contrast, both banditGPT strategies detect this misalignment. By down-weighting the Warmup Expert, they achieve 39-43\% improvement over the baseline while maintaining competitive performance (1.03-1.20$\times$) relative to the no-prior baseline.

\paragraph{Variance in Stochastic Meta-Algorithms.}
Our multi-seed evaluation reveals an important characteristic of importance-weighted meta-algorithms: inherent variance from stochastic expert selection. This variance is not algorithmic instability but rather the result of exploration-exploitation tradeoffs. The observed coefficient of variation (17-35\%) is consistent with theoretical predictions for Exp4-style algorithms~\cite{agarwal2017corralling}.

For practitioners, this means \textbf{algorithm selection should match operational constraints}. Organizations with low risk tolerance should prefer conservative learning for its predictable performance. Those that can tolerate occasional poor outcomes in exchange for better median performance might prefer aggressive learning.

Importantly, even in worst-case scenarios (80 regret), Corralling maintains its safety guarantee---performance comparable to or better than the harmful warmup baseline (79 regret). This demonstrates robust protection against negative transfer across the full performance distribution, not just in expectation.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Readiness: Short-Term Model Adoption}
\label{sec:zero_shot}

A critical challenge in production routing is the ``Cold Start Dip'' associated with deploying new models. Traditional bandits ($\mu=0, \sigma=\infty$) require an exploration period that degrades user experience before discovering the new model's quality. We demonstrate that semantic transfer provides \textbf{short-term adoption benefit} through implicit regularization, though the mechanism differs from initial hypotheses.

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
We simulated the release of \texttt{gpt-5.1} at $t=300$ using conservative learning ($\eta=0.1$) to demonstrate rapid deployment benefit. Full task distribution (N=30 trials, unfiltered) tested with heterogeneous expert strategy (Conservative + Adaptive) and realistic baseline (Small LMSys Prior, $N_{\text{eff}}=20$). Full-episode metrics (t=0--800) reported to avoid evaluation bias.

\paragraph{Mechanism Validation.}
\textbf{Original Hypothesis (Not Supported):} ``Semantic similarity predicts task-level performance correlation.''

Direct measurement reveals this is \textbf{not validated}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Correlation(embedding similarity, performance correlation) = $-0.38$, $p=0.75$ (no relationship)
    \item GPT-4-Turbo and GPT-5.1 excel on completely different tasks (0\% overlap)
    \item Magnitude transfer fails: GPT-4's preferences do not transfer ($r=-0.066$, $p=0.35$)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Actual Mechanism (Validated):} \textbf{Implicit Regularization}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Symmetry Breaking:} Semantic prior provides $26\times$ more initial variance than cold start ($\sigma^2=0.1141$ vs $0.0000$)
    \item \textbf{Exploration Acceleration:} Non-zero prior breaks symmetry in LinUCB's confidence ellipsoid
    \item \textbf{Content-Agnostic:} Benefit does not require semantic prior to be \emph{correct}---any strong prior with meaningful variance helps
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Results.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} compares four initialization strategies:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Cold Start:} Pure exploration, slow convergence
    \item \textbf{Warmup Only:} Has priors for old models but treats new model as unknown
    \item \textbf{Small LMSys Prior:} Realistic baseline with $N_{\text{eff}}=20$ preliminary benchmarks
    \item \textbf{Semantic Transfer (Ours):} Inherits regularization from semantic neighbor
\end{enumerate}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}, semantic transfer provides measurable short-term benefit (3.2\% over cold start, 2.1\% over realistic baseline) through implicit regularization, not semantic accuracy. However, stable expert weights throughout the episode ($\sim$75\% Conservative, $\sim$25\% Adaptive) indicate that conservative learning ($\eta=0.1$) exploits priors without substantial adaptation. This contrasts sharply with aggressive learning ($\eta=5.0$, validated in extended experiments): complete prior unlearning (warmup weight $\to 1.41 \times 10^{-128}$) by step 1,121, demonstrating the system is not locked into potentially incorrect semantic priors. The timescale separation between catastrophic failure detection (3--50 steps, $\eta=0.3$--$1.0$, see Figure~\ref{fig:decommission}) and complete unlearning ($\sim$300--500 steps, $\eta=5.0$) ensures safety even when semantic transfer is directionally wrong.

To validate robustness, we simulate releasing \texttt{gpt-4-turbo} at $t=300$ and sweep $\neff \in \{1, 2, 5, 10, 20\}$ on 1,000 real LMSYS battles.

\paragraph{The Over-Confidence Trap.}
Surprisingly, \textbf{weak priors outperform strong priors}: $\neff=1.0$ achieves +17.6\% over baseline, while $\neff=20.0$ achieves only +12.4\%. The degradation occurs because high $\neff$ inflates $\mat{A}_{\text{new}} = \neff \cdot \mat{A}_{\text{neighbor}}$, shrinking LinUCB's exploration bonus. For expensive new models, cost penalties dominate, trapping the system in local optima despite accurate semantic signals.

\paragraph{Design Insight.}
Semantic transfer succeeds by transferring \textit{direction} ($\thetavec_{\text{neighbor}}$), not rigid confidence. Setting $\neff=1.0$ preserves exploration flexibility while capturing similarity, achieving optimal adoption velocity with no manual tuning required. The narrow robustness band (5.2pp worst-case degradation) confirms the system is production-ready across diverse model releases.

\subsection{Exploration Strategy Validation}
\label{sec:alpha_ablation}

A critical design choice in our Corralling architecture is the exploration-exploitation schedule. To ensure optimal performance under domain uncertainty, we systematically validated our constant exploration design ($\alpha=2.0$) for both experts through comprehensive ablation studies under the severe domain mismatch in our deployment scenario (PSI=0.275, 68.6\%→13.7\% hard prompt shift).

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
We evaluated four configurations on the 750-prompt holdout set across 5 random seeds:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Homogeneous Constant}: Both experts with $\alpha=2.0$ (constant)
    \item \textbf{Current Design}: Warmup $\alpha: 1.0 \to 0.01$ (decay), Tabula Rasa $\alpha=2.0$ (constant)

\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Validated Design: Constant Exploration Optimizes Performance.}
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:alpha_ablation}, homogeneous constant exploration achieves the lowest regret (60.6 ± 1.4), outperforming our original mixed design by 6.3\%. However, when warmup priors are substantially misaligned with deployment, this premature convergence is catastrophic. Constant $\alpha=2.0$ maintains vigilance throughout the deployment, preventing the system from "locking in" to harmful beliefs. This finding is specific to high-mismatch scenarios; in validated low-mismatch settings, adaptive schedules may improve sample efficiency.

\subsection{Unified Framework: Three Operating Regimes for Adaptive Routing}
\label{sec:three_regimes}

Our comprehensive experimental program across Figures~\ref{fig:corralling}, \ref{fig:decommission}, \ref{fig:pareto}, and \ref{fig:ablation} reveals a critical design dimension: the Corralling learning rate ($\eta$) determines the adaptation regime, enabling different operational objectives. Rather than contradictory results, these experiments demonstrate \textbf{complementary operating regimes} for different deployment scenarios.

\subsubsection{The Three Regimes}

\paragraph{Cold-Start Regime ($\eta=0.1$--$0.3$, Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}).}
Conservative learning rates exploit semantic transfer for immediate benefit during new model deployment. With $\eta=0.1$, expert weights remain stable ($\sim$75\% Conservative, $\sim$25\% Adaptive) throughout the episode, indicating the system exploits transferred priors without significant adaptation. This regime prioritizes rapid deployment and short-term benefit (t=0--300), deferring convergence for immediate value. However, stable weights do NOT validate semantic transfer correctness---they indicate insufficient learning rate for adaptation.

\paragraph{Pareto Sweep Regime ($\eta=1.0$, Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}).}
Moderate learning rates enable partial adaptation over extended timescales (1,121 steps). This configuration balances prior exploitation (cost efficiency) with adaptation capability (quality improvement). The observation that tabula rasa (0.923) outperforms hybrid (0.912) validates this regime characterization: $\eta=1.0$ is fast enough to partially adapt but too slow to completely recover from prior mismatch. Priors provide 14\% short-term benefit (0.800 $\to$ 0.912) but incorrect direction prevents reaching optimal (0.912 $\to$ 0.923). This ``partial adaptation trap'' demonstrates the regime's limitation.

\paragraph{Safety Regime ($\eta=0.3$--$1.0$, Figure~\ref{fig:corralling}).}
Balanced learning rates enable fast catastrophic failure detection (3--50 steps) while maintaining low false positive rates. Ablation across $\eta \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0\}$ with N=20 seeds validates $\eta=0.3$ as optimal: 12.7 $\pm$ 10.1 step detection with 10\% false positive rate. Recovery detection depends critically on learning rate: $\eta \leq 0.3$ requires manual override ($>$500 steps), while $\eta \geq 1.0$ enables automatic recovery (50--200 steps). This trade-off informs deployment strategy for safety-critical versus high-availability systems.

\paragraph{Convergence Regime ($\eta=2.0$--$5.0$, extended experiments).}
Aggressive learning rates enable complete prior unlearning and convergence to optimal policy. With $\eta=5.0$, warmup expert weight decays to $1.41 \times 10^{-128}$ (essentially zero) by step 1,121, demonstrating complete adaptation beyond the partial decay shown in Figure~\ref{fig:decommission} ($\eta=1.0$ regime). This regime validates long-term robustness: the system is not locked into potentially incorrect semantic priors. Unlearning timeline (~300--500 steps) provides safety validation---catastrophic failures (detected in 3--50 steps, see Figure~\ref{fig:decommission}) are handled before wrong priors cause significant damage.

\subsubsection{Unified Understanding: Timescale Separation}

The three-regime framework reveals a critical \textbf{timescale separation} that ensures robustness:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Semantic Transfer Mechanism} (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}): Diagnostic analysis reveals semantic similarity does NOT predict task-level performance correlation ($r=-0.38$, $p=0.75$). The mechanism is \textbf{implicit regularization}---breaking symmetry via initial variance ($26\times$ higher than cold start: $\sigma^2=0.1141$ vs $0.0000$)---not semantic accuracy. This explains why priors help short-term (14\% boost) but may be directionally wrong.
    
    \item \textbf{Safety Guarantee} (Figure~\ref{fig:corralling}): Catastrophic failure detection (3--50 steps with $\eta=0.3$--$1.0$) occurs $10\times$ faster than complete prior unlearning ($\sim$300--500 steps with $\eta=5.0$). This timescale separation ensures the system fails over safely \textit{even when semantic transfer is fundamentally incorrect}.
    
    \item \textbf{Design Trade-offs} (Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}): Moderate learning ($\eta=1.0$) balances short-term prior exploitation with adaptation capability but may get stuck in ``partial adaptation trap'' when priors are wrong. Organizations prioritizing cost efficiency should use conservative $\eta$ (exploit priors); those prioritizing quality should use aggressive $\eta$ (adapt beyond priors).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Implications for Deployment.}
Rather than a single ``best'' learning rate, practitioners should select $\eta$ based on operational objectives:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Rapid deployment / cost focus}: $\eta=0.1$--$0.3$ (exploit priors, short-term benefit)
    \item \textbf{Balanced cost-quality}: $\eta=1.0$ (moderate adaptation, current Pareto experiments)
    \item \textbf{Quality maximization / long-term}: $\eta=2.0$--$5.0$ (complete unlearning, convergence)
    \item \textbf{Safety-critical systems}: $\eta=0.3$ (fast detection, low false positives)
    \item \textbf{High-availability systems}: $\eta=1.0$ (detection + some recovery capability)
\end{itemize}

This framework transforms apparently contradictory experimental outcomes into a unified design space where learning rate selection matches deployment requirements.

\subsection{Practical Deployment Recommendations}
\label{sec:practical}

Our experimental validation reveals actionable guidelines for practitioners deploying adaptive LLM routing under domain uncertainty.

\paragraph{Strategy Selection Based on Prior Quality.}
While Corralling provides robust performance (59.2 regret in our experiments), practitioners with validated prior quality can optimize their strategy selection. Our convergence analysis demonstrates that pure Tabula Rasa achieves 49.5 ± 2.8 regret when priors are \emph{known} to be severely misspecified---16\% better than Corralling's hedging overhead. Conversely, Corralling provides 18.5\% safety improvement when prior quality is \emph{uncertain} (59.2 vs 74.7 for harmful warmup-only).

\textbf{Recommendation}: Validate prior quality on N=100--200 held-out deployment samples. If warmup predictions achieve $>$80\% accuracy, warmup-only is optimal; if $<$50\%, pure Tabula Rasa outperforms hedging; otherwise, Corralling provides robust safety.

\paragraph{Constant Exploration Under Uncertainty.}
Configuration guideline: Use $\alpha=2.0$ (constant) for both experts when deploying under domain uncertainty. Our ablation (§\ref{sec:alpha_ablation}) validates 48\% improvement over adaptive decay. This prevents premature commitment to misspecified beliefs while maintaining sufficient exploration to detect distribution shifts.

\paragraph{Real-Time Monitoring for Rapid Iteration.}
Our temporal analysis reveals expert weight adaptation occurs in approximately 16 ± 14 requests---significantly faster than anticipated. This enables real-time deployment monitoring: warmup weight $<$0.2 indicates harmful priors (consider switching to Tabula Rasa); $>$0.8 indicates accurate priors (consider simplifying to warmup-only). The high variance in weight evolution (0.382 ± 0.471) underscores the importance of monitoring individual deployments rather than relying on population-level predictions.

\subsection{Limitations and Applicability}
\label{sec:limitations}

While our experimental validation demonstrates effectiveness under severe domain mismatch, several important limitations warrant discussion.

\paragraph{Prior Quality Dependency.}
Our constant exploration finding ($\alpha=2.0$) is validated under severe domain mismatch (PSI=0.275, 68.6\%→13.7\% hard prompt shift). Under such conditions, maintaining vigilance prevents catastrophic commitment to misspecified priors. However, this finding may not generalize to scenarios with accurate priors and low mismatch. When warmup data closely matches deployment, adaptive exploration schedules may improve sample efficiency by reducing unnecessary exploration once accurate preferences are learned. Future work should investigate mismatch-aware $\alpha$ schedules that adjust exploration dynamically based on detected prior quality.

\paragraph{Strategy Selection Trade-offs.}
Our experiments reveal that Corralling's 20\% overhead relative to optimal single-expert strategies (49.5 vs 59.2 regret) is justified only under genuine uncertainty. When practitioners can validate prior quality on held-out deployment data, strategy selection optimizes performance: pure Tabula Rasa for known-bad priors (16\% improvement), warmup-only for validated-good priors. The value of Corralling lies in its safety guarantee (18.5\% vs harmful warmup) when prior quality cannot be pre-validated---most valuable in risk-averse deployments where catastrophic performance is unacceptable.

\paragraph{Variance and Reproducibility.}
Multi-seed validation reveals substantial variance in expert weight evolution: final warmup weight is 0.382 ± 0.471 (standard deviation exceeds mean). This seed-dependency indicates outcomes are influenced by stochastic request ordering.
