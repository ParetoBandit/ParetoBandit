\section{Results \& Discussion}

\subsection{Forensic Discovery: The Alignment Tax}
\label{sec:semantic_structure}

Spatial analysis of the semantic manifold (Figure~\ref{fig:lmsys_holdout_structure}) reveals two clusters at PC1 = 0.3. Validation against ground-truth reward gaps exposes a performance inversion that enables the router's success.

\paragraph{The High PC1 ``Strictness Zone'' (17.6\%).}
Forensic analysis reveals this cluster is 85\% dominated by strict completion templates (``You are the text completion model... only send the completion... don't repeat...'')---explicit negative constraints. Here, the cheaper model (Mixtral-8x7B) achieves a mean gap of $-0.68$ (Mixtral $>$ GPT-4-Turbo). This reveals an \textbf{Alignment Tax}: RLHF-optimized models systematically violate negative instructions due to their training for verbosity and helpfulness. A static router that sends these constraint-heavy prompts to GPT-4-Turbo pays 40$\times$ more for worse format compliance.

\paragraph{The Low PC1 ``Nuance Zone'' (82.4\%).}
Conversely, the open-ended conversational cluster (Low PC1) is where GPT-4-Turbo's alignment provides value (Mean Gap $+0.13$). Routing this entire cluster to Mixtral would degrade quality---the router must surgically identify the sub-manifold where cost savings are possible without quality loss.

\paragraph{Implication: Counter-Intuitive Economics.}
The routing opportunity inverts conventional wisdom. We do not save money by routing ``easy'' tasks to cheap models---we save money by identifying ``strict'' tasks where the expensive model actively fails. This transforms the Alignment Tax from a quality problem into a cost-saving opportunity through \textbf{Forensic Agility}---discovering hidden failure modes in production data that human intuition would miss.

\subsubsection{The ``Over-Provisioning Penalty'' of Static Routing}

A critical finding is the non-monotonic behavior of the RouteLLM benchmark. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}, RouteLLM's performance collapses when the threshold is lowered to admit more GPT-4-Turbo traffic ($\tau < 0.10$).

\textbf{The Phenomenon}: Users paying \$0.013 (Maximum Cost) receive a reward of $0.812$---worse than the \$0.0003 Mistral baseline ($0.823$). We term this the ``Over-Provisioning Penalty'': paying a $43\times$ cost premium to \emph{decrease} quality by $1.3\%$.

\textbf{The Cause}: Static routers interpolate based on general ``hardness.'' They cannot distinguish the $6\%$ of prompts where GPT-4-Turbo is actually better from the $94\%$ where it is simply slower and more verbose.

\textbf{The Solution}: banditGPT (Blue Line) learns this inversion online. By using GPT-4-Turbo only when statistically justified, it achieves a peak reward of $0.909$, enabling a $+10.4\%$ quality gain while still reducing costs by $27\%$ compared to the naive GPT-4-Turbo baseline.

\subsection{The Cost of Autonomy}

In the ultra-low budget regime ($< \$0.004$), RouteLLM initially outperforms banditGPT. This reflects the ``Cost of Autonomy'': the online learner must pay an exploration penalty (regret) to discover the decision boundary, whereas the static router exploits pre-computed knowledge for free. However, this upfront investment pays dividends: once the budget permits ($> \$0.008$), banditGPT breaks through the static ceiling that confines RouteLLM.

\subsection{Algorithmic Safety: Adaptive Prior Decommissioning}

While Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} demonstrates average efficiency, production systems require worst-case safety. A critical vulnerability in prior-based routing is \emph{Negative Transfer}: if the offline priors are misspecified (e.g., trained on ``Hard'' data but deployed on ``Easy'' traffic), the router may stubbornly over-provision resources.

Figure~\ref{fig:decommission} visualizes the internal dynamics of our Corralling Algorithm handling this exact scenario.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/figure6_expert_decommission.png}
\caption{The ``Step Function'' of Decisive Decommissioning. (Top) Evolution of expert weights during the first 500 steps. The system initially explores the Warmup Expert (Orange), but detects a high loss due to domain mismatch. At $t \approx 50$, the cumulative evidence triggers a sharp exponential decay, effectively decommissioning the harmful prior by $t=200$. (Bottom) Cumulative loss comparison confirms the Tabula Rasa expert (Green) is strictly superior.}
\label{fig:decommission}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The ``Step Function'' Mechanism}: The sharp drop in Warmup weight at $t \approx 50$ is not an artifact but a designed feature of the Importance-Weighted Loss estimator. When the Warmup expert makes a mistake while its probability $p_t$ is low, the penalty $\hat{\ell} = \ell_t / p_t$ explodes. This creates a ``death spiral'' for the misspecified expert, ensuring that harmful priors are removed from the decision path within 100--200 steps rather than lingering for thousands.

\textbf{Outcome}: By decisively decommissioning the biased prior, the system pivots $85\%$ of traffic to Mixtral, achieving a $97.6\%$ cost reduction without manual intervention.

\subsection{Operational Agility: Zero-Shot Readiness}

In the fast-moving LLM landscape, models are released weekly. Standard bandit algorithms suffer a ``Cold Start'' penalty---a period of random exploration required to learn a new model's utility.

To evaluate agility, we simulated a ``Mid-Flight Upgrade'' event where a new model (``GPT-5.1'') is released at $t=300$. We compare a standard Cold Start initialization against our Latent Semantic Transfer.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/figure7_zero_shot.png}
\caption{Zero-Shot Readiness. When a new model is released at $t=300$, the Cold Start baseline (Red) suffers a catastrophic regression ($3.3 \to 1.7$ reward), requiring 500 steps to recover. In contrast, Latent Semantic Transfer (Green) maintains performance ($\sim 4.5$) by inheriting the ``intuition'' of a semantic neighbor.}
\label{fig:zeroshot}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The Cold Start Dip}: The baseline router (Red Line) treats the new model as a complete unknown. It forces users to endure $\sim$500 steps of degraded performance ($R < 3.0$) while it relearns the decision boundary from scratch.

\textbf{The Transfer Advantage}: By transferring the preference vector $\thetavec$ from a semantic neighbor (GPT-4-Turbo) while resetting confidence $\Amat$, banditGPT (Green Line) achieves Zero-Shot Readiness. It effectively says, ``I suspect this model behaves like GPT-4-Turbo, but I'm open to being proven wrong.'' This results in a $2.8\times$ performance advantage during the critical adoption window.

\subsection{Robustness: Sensitivity Analysis}

A common critique of transfer learning is hyperparameter sensitivity: Does the system fail if we trust the neighbor too much or too little?

We performed a sensitivity sweep on the Effective Prior Strength ($\neff$), ranging from $1.0$ (Weak Trust) to $20.0$ (Strong Trust).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/figure8_sensitivity.png}
\caption{Robustness to Prior Strength ($\neff$). We vary the strength of the transferred prior from $\neff=1$ to $\neff=20$. All transfer configurations (Blue/Teal lines) significantly outperform the Cold Start baseline (Red). The overlap of the trajectories confirms that performance is driven by the direction of the transferred mean ($\thetavec$), not the tightness of the confidence interval.}
\label{fig:sensitivity}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Invariance Result}: As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}, the performance trajectories for $\neff=1$ (Weak) and $\neff=20$ (Strong) are nearly indistinguishable.

\textbf{Implication}: This confirms mathematically that the benefit comes from the mean initialization ($\hat{\thetavec} \approx \thetavec_{\text{neighbor}}$). Even a weak suggestion is sufficient to guide the router away from the Cold Start failure mode. Consequently, $\neff$ does not require precise tuning; any value $\in [1, 20]$ yields near-optimal results.
