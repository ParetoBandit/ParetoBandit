\subsubsection{Comparison Across Cost Regimes}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}a reveals two distinct regimes, reflecting the complementary strengths of supervised pre-training and online adaptation:

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{RouteLLM} is pre-trained on 100k out-of-distribution (OOD) supervised preference pairs, giving it strong out-of-the-box performance, requiring no deployment data.
    \item \textbf{banditGPT} combines 80k OOD battle priors with 1,121 in-distribution online learning steps, adapting from its own routing outcomes, requiring no labels.
\end{itemize}

At \textbf{low-to-moderate budgets} (\$0.0003 to \$0.005 per request), RouteLLM's pre-trained classifier is competitive or superior. Its supervised training provides reliable routing without deployment overhead.

At \textbf{higher budgets} (\$0.005+), banditGPT surpasses RouteLLM. Online learning discovers the correct aggregate model ordering for this deployment distribution, achieving a peak quality of $0.914 \pm 0.003$ (95\% CI, $n{=}20$), representing a $+3.5\%$ improvement over RouteLLM's peak of 0.883. The Oracle upper bound (0.953) routes only 13\% of prompts to the expensive model with perfect per-prompt knowledge, representing the theoretical ceiling for this model pair.

\paragraph{Why RouteLLM wins at low budgets.} At tight budgets, both routers route the vast majority of traffic to the cheaper model and allocate only a handful of prompts to the expensive model. Quality depends on selecting the \emph{right} prompts for the expensive model. RouteLLM's MF classifier, pre-trained on 100k preference pairs, encodes fine-grained prompt-level discrimination that banditGPT's 1,121 online examples cannot match at this granularity. In contrast, at higher budgets where 30 to 70\% of prompts are routed, \emph{aggregate model calibration} dominates per-prompt selection, and banditGPT's online learning, which discovers that the cheaper model outperforms the expensive model on this distribution, provides the larger advantage.

This evaluation uses two models to match RouteLLM's topology and enable a controlled comparison. Multi-model results ($K \geq 3$) with dedicated warmup priors are provided in the supplementary material.

\subsubsection{The Value of Online Adaptation}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}b reveals why this matters in practice. banditGPT starts below RouteLLM (cold-start quality: $0.839 \pm 0.004$ with priors only) and crosses RouteLLM's peak between approximately \textbf{300 to 400 in-distribution prompts} (mean reward $0.893 \pm 0.014$ at step~400, vs.\ RouteLLM's 0.883). In this setting, the crossover illustrates that a modest amount of in-distribution experience can outweigh a large volume of out-of-distribution supervision.

The learning curve shows generally increasing improvement from cold-start ($0.839$) through convergence (${\sim}0.904$ by step~900), with minor fluctuations at intermediate checkpoints that remain within the 95\% confidence bands. Variance across trials contracts as training progresses (std from $0.040$ at step~100 to $0.009$ at step~900), indicating that the router converges to a stable, high-quality policy.

\subsubsection{Non-Monotonicity of Static Routing}
\label{sec:nonmonotone}

A striking secondary finding is the non-monotonic behavior of RouteLLM at high cost. Dense threshold coverage (26 points, including 16 in the $[0.0, 0.15]$ range) reveals that when the threshold is lowered to admit more traffic to the expensive model ($\tau < 0.10$), RouteLLM's quality \emph{degrades}: reward drops from the peak of 0.883 (at $\tau{\approx}0.12$, cost \$0.007) to 0.812 at $\tau{=}0$ (cost \$0.013). At maximum cost, RouteLLM pays ${\sim}44\times$ more than the static cheaper model for $1.3\%$ lower quality on this distribution, consistent with model preference heterogeneity.

This non-monotonicity is not a pathology of RouteLLM's MF model specifically; it is inherent to any pre-trained router deployed on a distribution where the assumed ``strong'' model is not uniformly superior. In specialized domains (legal, code review, structured data extraction), smaller fine-tuned models frequently outperform general-purpose frontier models, making this scenario practically common.

\subsubsection{Deployment guidance (brief)}
These results suggest a simple pattern for practice: supervised static routers can be strong at tight budgets when their training data matches deployment, while online adaptation can be valuable when (i) prompt distributions drift (Appendix~\ref{app:distribution_shift} quantifies this: PSI\,$=$\,0.763; any online bandit yields $+7.0$\,pp, and Corralling adds a significant $+1.3$\,pp on top; prior source has no measurable effect), (ii) model ordering is uncertain, or (iii) the portfolio grows beyond two models. A more detailed decision guide and monitoring protocol is included in the supplementary material; quantitative crossover points are deployment-dependent.

\subsubsection{Gap Closure Analysis}

On the frozen holdout set ($N{=}750$), banditGPT closes \textbf{70.0\%} of the optimality gap ($\frac{0.914 - 0.823}{0.953 - 0.823}$), compared to 46.2\% for RouteLLM at its best threshold. At step 0 (priors only, before online learning), banditGPT achieves $0.839 \pm 0.004$, above the static cheaper model but below RouteLLM's peak.
Operationally, banditGPT incurs inference cost for only the selected model per request; it requires no parallel evaluation of all candidates, unlike offline data collection for supervised routers, which must query every model on every prompt to obtain training labels.

Design ablations and hyperparameter sensitivity sweeps are reported in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Additional Evaluations and Limitations}
\label{sec:robustness_adaptation}
\label{sec:prior_degradation}
\label{sec:transfer_null}
\label{sec:limitations}

We report additional analyses in the appendix (supplementary material), including robustness under prior mismatch, synthetic catastrophic-failure stress tests, multi-model scaling, comparisons to alternative bandit baselines, and cumulative regret curves.
A leave-one-out ablation evaluates whether one-shot per-model semantic $\thetavec$-transfer (bootstrapping a new model's parameters from its nearest within-provider neighbor via tetrachoric correlation) provides benefit beyond the continuous family sharing of Hybrid LinUCB.
Across 20 seeds and 10 target models, the aggregate effect is not significant ($p{>}0.5$, Cohen's $d{<}0.05$), suggesting that the family-level $\betavec_F$ mechanism is an effective abstraction for cross-model knowledge transfer in this setting.
This is a positive architectural finding: the hybrid decomposition subsumes one-shot transfer, and practitioners need not invest in per-model bootstrapping pipelines.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our controlled RouteLLM comparison uses a single model pair and a single prompt distribution; deployments with different portfolios and traffic will shift the quantitative cost crossover points. Our full factorial distribution shift analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:distribution_shift}) demonstrates that online adaptation renders prior source statistically irrelevant ($p{=}0.18$) while Corralling provides a significant $+1.3$\,pp advantage ($p < 0.0001$) for this model pair; the magnitude of these effects will vary with portfolio size and distributional mismatch. Our robustness experiments are stress tests rather than guarantees, and our current treatment of non-stationarity primarily operates through meta-level mixing rather than explicit forgetting within each expert.
