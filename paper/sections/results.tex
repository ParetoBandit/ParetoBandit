\section{Results \& Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate banditGPT on three dimensions: economic efficiency (Pareto optimality), safety under prior mismatch, and operational agility (adaptation to failures and new models). Section~\ref{sec:pareto} presents the primary benchmark; Section~\ref{sec:robustness_adaptation} summarizes safety and adaptation capabilities, with full figures and extended analysis in Appendices~\ref{app:adaptation_details}--\ref{app:multiseed_sensitivity}.

\subsubsection{Non-Monotonicity of Static Routing}

A key finding is the non-monotonic behavior of the RouteLLM benchmark. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}, RouteLLM's performance degrades when the threshold is lowered to admit more GPT-4 traffic ($\tau < 0.10$).

At maximum cost (\$0.013), RouteLLM achieves a reward of only 0.812---worse than the \$0.0003 Mixtral baseline (0.823). This amounts to paying a $43\times$ cost premium for $1.3\%$ \emph{lower} quality, a direct consequence of the Alignment Tax (Figure~\ref{fig:pca}): static routers trained on general ``hardness'' heuristics over-provision expensive models to strict constraint tasks where RLHF-optimized models underperform cheaper alternatives (gap $= -0.682$), failing to distinguish this region from the natural language region where GPT-4 provides genuine value (gap $= +0.133$).

banditGPT learns this inversion online. By using GPT-4 only when statistically justified, it achieves a peak reward of $0.912 \pm 0.006$ (mean $\pm$ 95\% CI, $n=5$ trials) on warm-start evaluation with continued learning,\footnote{The Pareto frontier (Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}) reports 0.9088 peak quality, evaluated on a frozen held-out test set (N=750) without continued online learning. Both evaluations validate strong performance.} enabling a +12.3\% quality gain over Mixtral while reducing costs by 27\% compared to the naive GPT-4 baseline.

\paragraph{Note on ``adaptive vs.\ static'' framing.}
The comparison between banditGPT (adaptive) and a static single-model allocation (100\% GPT-4-Turbo) demonstrates that an adaptive system outperforms a non-adaptive one---which is expected by construction when the task space is heterogeneous. The more informative comparison is banditGPT vs.\ RouteLLM, which compares two adaptive strategies (online vs.\ supervised). Readers should note that the RouteLLM comparison involves different learning paradigms and data access (see Section~\ref{sec:experiments} for caveats).

\subsection{Robustness and Adaptation}
\label{sec:robustness_adaptation}

Production systems additionally require safety under prior mismatch, rapid failure detection, and zero-shot model adoption. We summarize the key findings here; full experimental details, figures, and statistical tests appear in Appendices~\ref{app:adaptation_details} and~\ref{app:multiseed_sensitivity}.

\subsubsection{Safety Under Prior Mismatch}
\label{sec:adaptation}

Table~\ref{tab:performance_gap} evaluates Corralling under severe domain mismatch (PSI=0.275) across N=10 seeds.

\input{../experiments_v1/02_table/table2_final}

When warmup priors are misspecified, the ``Warmup Only'' baseline suffers catastrophic regret (79.0). Corralling detects this misalignment and achieves 39--43\% improvement by down-weighting the harmful expert. An independent t-test comparing learning rates ($\eta=0.1$: 45.2$\pm$7.9 vs $\eta=1.0$: 48.1$\pm$16.8) yields $p=0.63$, Cohen's $d=-0.22$. However, with only $N=10$ seeds per condition, statistical power to detect this effect size is approximately 8--10\%---far below the 80\% convention. We therefore \emph{cannot conclude the conditions are equivalent}, only that our experiment is insufficiently powered to distinguish them. The practical recommendation for $\eta=0.1$ rests on its substantially lower variance (CV=17\% vs 35\%) and absence of catastrophic failures, not on a claim of equivalent mean performance.

\subsubsection{Catastrophic Failure Detection}

We evaluated Corralling's response to sudden model degradation in a controlled scenario where quality drops from $\mu=0.80$ to $\mu=0.15$ (Cohen's $d\approx 5.0$). Corralling detects the failure within 3--50 steps and shifts traffic to the healthy model, with 0\% false positives during stable phases. Conservative recovery ensures the system does not prematurely return traffic to a potentially unreliable model. Full dynamics are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:catastrophic_failure}.

\subsubsection{Zero-Shot Model Adoption}
\label{sec:zero_shot}

When a new model (\texttt{gpt-5.1}) is released at $t=300$, semantic transfer from its nearest neighbor (GPT-4-Turbo) provides measurable short-term benefit. Across $N=30$ trials with paired statistical testing:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Transfer vs cold start: $\Delta=+3.2\%$, $t_{29}=4.20$, $p=0.0002$, Cohen's $d=0.77$
    \item Post-release improvement: $+0.62$ reward ($t_{29}=6.93$, $p<10^{-7}$, Cohen's $d=1.26$)
\end{itemize}

The mechanism is \textbf{implicit regularization} (symmetry breaking via $26\times$ higher initial variance), not semantic accuracy transfer---embedding similarity does not predict task-level performance ($r=-0.38$, $p=0.75$, $N=3$ models). This content-agnostic mechanism means \emph{any} strong prior with meaningful variance accelerates adoption. Full ablation and mechanism analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:zero_shot_details}.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity_analysis}

The effective prior strength ($\neff$) exhibits regime-dependent effects. Without Corralling (forced transfer), performance degrades monotonically with increasing $\neff$ (6.2\% span from best to worst), confirming an ``over-confidence trap'' where strong priors suppress exploration of new models. With Corralling enabled, this effect vanishes ($p>0.40$, N=3 seeds): the meta-learner adaptively abandons transfer when priors mismatch the data (67\% of seeds) and exploits it when they match (33\%). System robustness thus emerges from adaptive expert selection, not parameter insensitivity. Regime-stratified analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:multiseed_sensitivity}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Prior Quality Dependency.}
Our constant exploration finding ($\alpha=2.0$) is validated under severe domain mismatch (PSI=0.275). In low-mismatch deployments with accurate priors, adaptive exploration schedules may improve sample efficiency. Future work should investigate mismatch-aware $\alpha$ schedules.

\paragraph{Strategy Selection Trade-offs.}
Corralling incurs $\sim$20\% overhead relative to optimal single-expert strategies (59.2 vs 49.5 regret), justified only under genuine prior uncertainty. When prior quality can be validated on held-out deployment data, direct strategy selection (tabula rasa for known-bad priors, warmup-only for validated-good priors) outperforms hedging.

\paragraph{Variance and Reproducibility.}
Multi-seed evaluation reveals substantial variance in expert weight evolution (final warmup weight: $0.382 \pm 0.471$, standard deviation exceeds mean). Outcomes are influenced by stochastic request ordering; practitioners should monitor deployment-specific dynamics rather than relying on population-level predictions.
