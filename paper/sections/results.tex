\subsection{Stationary Budget Pacing}
\label{sec:exp1_budget_pacing}

We first ask: \emph{can the router accept a dollar budget directly and automatically achieve it, without sacrificing routing quality compared to an offline-tuned static penalty?}

We evaluate the $K{=}3$ portfolio under stationary conditions.  Following the standard online-learning protocol, the policy continues to adapt during the test trajectory---metrics reflect cumulative performance over the stream, not a frozen snapshot.  The \textbf{Static baseline} uses a fixed cost-penalty $\lambda_s \in \{0, 0.05, \dots, 1.0\}$, producing a 7-point Pareto frontier. \textbf{ParetoBandit (BudgetPacer)} is evaluated at seven log-spaced targets, ranging from the cheapest model's empirical mean cost (\$0.000029/req) to the most expensive (\$0.0150/req).

\paragraph{Pareto frontier.} Both methods trace a quality--cost Pareto curve on the test set. At tight budgets (cost $< \$0.001$/req), the pacer achieves higher reward at lower cost: at a target of \$0.00008/req, the pacer reaches a mean reward of $0.813 \pm 0.0004$. The nearest static point ($\lambda_s{=}1.0$) achieves only $0.804 \pm 0.0003$ while spending $4.4\times$ more (\$0.00037/req). The pacer dominates because its adaptive $\lambda_t$ creates an implicit \emph{spend-when-valuable, save-when-cheap} policy: when easy prompts allow cheap routing, $\lambda_t$ decreases, freeing budget for harder prompts where expensive models provide greater quality uplift. The static penalty applies the same cost tax uniformly, forgoing this temporal budget allocation. In operational terms, this Pareto dominance means the pacer delivers $1.1\%$ higher reward than the best static penalty at $\bpStaticOneCostRatio\times$ lower cost---a gap that widens as budgets tighten, precisely where cost control matters most.

Notably, the two frontiers differ not only in quality at shared operating points but in \emph{coverage}: the static curve terminates at \$0.00037/req ($\lambda_s{=}1.0$, 93\% Llama), while the pacer extends to \$0.00003/req (99.9\% Llama)---a $12\times$ gap.  This is structural, not a sweep artefact: for any finite $\lambda_s$, the LinUCB exploration bonus on expensive models occasionally exceeds the fixed penalty, leaking traffic to costlier arms.  The adaptive $\lambda_t$ faces no such ceiling---it ratchets upward until the budget constraint binds, providing continuous Pareto coverage across the full cost spectrum without offline calibration.

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows that ParetoBandit equipped with the {\tt BudgetPacer} perfectly traces this optimal penalty Pareto frontier. At unconstrained budgets (cost $> \$0.005$), both methods asymptote toward a quality ceiling of ${\sim}0.922$---itself $95.7\%$ of the per-prompt oracle ($0.963$)---and the dual variable correctly decays to zero, so the pacer gracefully becomes a no-op.  The annotated oracle gaps frame the practitioner's tradeoff concretely: a ${\sim}4\times$ increase in oracle gap ($4.3\% \to 17.7\%$) buys a $300\times$ cost reduction; a more moderate $80\%$ cost saving (\$0.0095 $\to$ \$0.0018/req) widens the gap by only $1.4$\,pp.

\paragraph{Budget compliance.} The pacer's primary advantage is its ability to hit a target. For the six tightest targets, actual spend ranges from $0.97\times$ to $1.02\times$ of the budget---effectively perfect compliance. Figure~\ref{fig:model_mix} illustrates the resulting steady-state model allocation under a strict budget, dynamically balancing between Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-Large-2512 to maximize quality. The loosest target ($B{=}\$0.015$) shows under-utilisation ($0.64\times$) because the unconstrained router's natural spend (\$0.0094/req) is already below this target; the pacer constrains spend \emph{from above} but never forces the router to spend more than its quality-optimal policy requires.

\subsection{Adapting to Model Quality Shifts}
\label{sec:exp2_reward_shift}

We next evaluate robustness to non-stationarity (Reward Drift). At step $t{=}893$ (Phase~2), Llama-8B and Mistral-Large exchange their reward distributions (costs unchanged), simulating a scenario where Mistral degrades to the worst option while Llama becomes the best. All conditions use a shared static cost penalty ($\lambda_s{=}0.20$) to isolate the effect of the learning mechanism.

\paragraph{Why these baselines.}
We compare four conditions forming an \emph{ablation ladder}---each
adds exactly one capability---plus an external baseline from the
non-stationary bandit literature:
(1)~\textbf{Fixed Policy}: frozen priors, no online learning---the
dominant production pattern and cost-of-not-adapting reference;
(2)~\textbf{Naive Bandit ($\gamma{=}1.0$)}: adds online learning
with infinite memory---the approach taken by existing bandit-based
LLM routers (PILOT~\cite{panda2025pilot},
BaRP~\cite{wang2025barp});
(3)~\textbf{SW-UCB ($W{=}200$)}: Sliding-Window
LinUCB~\cite{garivier2011switching} without warmup priors---a
purpose-built non-stationary algorithm with known minimax regret
bounds, included to ensure comparison against a method specifically
designed for changing environments;
(4)~\textbf{ParetoBandit ($\gamma{=}0.995$)}: warmup priors +
geometric forgetting, combining all three ingredients.

The four conditions produce clearly separated cumulative regret
trajectories across 40~seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}a; Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative_regret}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Fixed Policy (Offline):} Because it cannot learn,
    it continues routing \rsFixedMistralPct\% to Mistral-Large even after the shift
    makes it the worst arm.  It accumulates $\rsFixedPhaseTwo$ Phase~2 regret,
    illustrating the cost of deploying a frozen router.
  \item \textbf{Naive Bandit ($\gamma{=}1.0$):} Online learning
    reduces total regret by \rsReductionFixedNaive\% ($\rsNaiveTotal$ vs.\ $\rsFixedTotal$), but
    infinite memory means Phase~1 inertia dilutes the new signal:
    Phase~2 regret is $\rsNaivePhaseTwo$---\rsNaivePhaseTwoExcess\% more than ParetoBandit.
    Every existing bandit-based LLM router operates at this level.
  \item \textbf{SW-UCB ($W{=}200$):} Achieves the \emph{lowest
    Phase~2 regret} ($\rsSWUCBPhaseTwo$), demonstrating fast post-shift
    adaptation.  However, without warmup priors it starts from a
    blank slate ($A{=}\lambda I$, $b{=}0$): during the first
    ${\sim}200$ steps the UCB exploration bonus dominates, routing
    traffic near-uniformly and accumulating $\rsSWUCBPhaseOne$ Phase~1
    regret---\rsSWUCBPhaseOneFactor$\times$ higher than prior-initialised conditions.
    Its cross-seed variance ($\sigma{=}\rsSWUCBStd$) is \rsSWUCBVarFactor$\times$
    ParetoBandit's, making individual deployments unpredictable.
  \item \textbf{ParetoBandit ($\gamma{=}0.995$):} Geometric forgetting
    reduces Phase~2 regret by \rsParetoBanditPhaseTwoReduction\% vs.\ the Naive Bandit
    ($\rsParetoBanditPhaseTwo$ vs.\ $\rsNaivePhaseTwo$).  The ${\sim}200$-step effective memory
    window discounts stale Phase~1 evidence, allowing ParetoBandit to
    re-learn the new optimum within ${\sim}200$ steps of the swap.
    Total regret ($\rsParetoBanditTotal$) is the lowest of any condition, and
    cross-seed variance ($\sigma{=}\rsParetoBanditStd$) is the lowest among all
    learning methods.
\end{itemize}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/cumulative_regret.pdf}
\caption{Cumulative regret under reward drift (4~conditions, 40~seeds, 95\% bootstrap CI).  ParetoBandit ($\gamma{=}0.995$) achieves the lowest total regret by combining warmup priors (low Phase~1 cost) with geometric forgetting (fast Phase~2 adaptation).  SW-UCB adapts fastest post-shift but pays a large cold-start tax.}

\paragraph{Deployment reliability.}
Beyond mean regret, variance reveals a critical distinction.
ParetoBandit's $\sigma{=}\rsParetoBanditStd$ is comparable to the Naive Bandit
($\rsNaiveStd$) but \rsSWUCBVarFactor$\times$ lower than SW-UCB ($\rsSWUCBStd$).
SW-UCB's high variance stems from its cold start: which arms happen
to be sampled in the first ${\sim}200$ steps varies widely across
seeds, producing high-regret outliers.
ParetoBandit's warmup priors eliminate this cold-start lottery by providing
informed routing from the first request, stabilising early routing
and compressing cross-seed variance.
Geometric forgetting then bounds the effective sample size at ${\sim}200$,
preventing any single observation ordering from dominating.
The priors' contribution is transient---after ${\sim}200$ steps online
evidence dominates regardless of initialisation
(Appendix~\ref{appendix:val_burnin_ablation})---but in production a
team gets one deployment, and the priors ensure that \emph{every}
deployment starts well rather than gambling on favourable early
exploration.

\subsection{Budget Pacing under Cost Drift}
\label{sec:exp3_cost_drift}

We now introduce the most complex and realistic scenario: a three-phase cost drift while operating under strict budget constraints. The held-out test split ($n{=}1{,}824$) provides 1,216 unique prompts: 608 randomly-selected prompts for Phase~1 (normal pricing) and a disjoint set of 608 for Phase~2 (Gemini price drop to nearly zero). Phase~3 restores the original pricing and \emph{reuses the same 608 prompts as Phase~1}, making the P1-vs-P3 comparison a controlled within-subject design: any difference is attributable solely to the router's internal state (accumulated Phase~2 history), not prompt-sampling variability. With geometric forgetting $\gamma{=}0.995$, Phase~1 observations are down-weighted by $\gamma^{608}\approx 0.047$ by Phase~3, so the learner retains negligible memory of individual Phase~1 evaluations. The Fixed Policy (no online learning) serves as a null check---its P1 and P3 metrics are identical, confirming this control. We evaluate three budget regimes: Tight (\$0.0003/req), Moderate (\$0.00066/req), and Loose (\$0.0019/req).

\paragraph{Budget compliance is the key differentiator.}
The Fixed Policy (using an offline-tuned static penalty $\lambda_s$) is wildly inconsistent: it under-spends at tight and moderate budgets ($\bdFixedTightPhaseOneRatio$ and $\bdFixedModPhaseOneRatio$) but massively over-spends at the loose budget ($\bdFixedLoosePhaseOneRatio$). A single static penalty cannot simultaneously satisfy different budget regimes. The Naive Bandit is similarly erratic: it overshoots at tight ($\bdNaiveTightPhaseOneRatio$) and moderate ($\bdNaiveModPhaseOneRatio$), and over-spends by $\bdNaiveLoosePhaseOneRatio$ at the loose budget because the static penalty cannot adjust to the actual cost landscape.
To isolate the value of \emph{continuous} online tracking from merely reacting to a price change, we add a \textbf{Recalibrated Bandit}: identical to the Naive Bandit in Phase~1, but at each phase boundary its static penalty is re-tuned offline via a grid search on the validation split with the new phase's pricing. This is the strongest static-penalty baseline, granting oracle knowledge of both change points and two offline calibration passes. Even so, the Recalibrated Bandit inherits Phase~1 non-compliance and cannot track intra-phase spending dynamics (Table~\ref{tab:budget_compliance}).

ParetoBandit is the only system that reliably hits the budget in Phase~1 ($\bdParetoBanditLoosePhaseOneUtil$ to $\bdParetoBanditTightPhaseOneUtil$ across all three targets; Table~\ref{tab:main_results}b). The BudgetPacer's primal-dual mechanism adjusts $\lambda_t$ continuously based on observed costs, providing closed-loop control that no static penalty---even with periodic recalibration---can replicate.
From an SLA perspective, these deviations translate directly to financial risk: the Fixed Policy's $\bdFixedLoosePhaseOneRatio$ overshoot at the loose budget means spending more than~$160\%$ above the agreed-upon target---an overrun that grows linearly with traffic volume---while its $\bdFixedTightPhaseOneRatio$ utilisation at the tight budget means leaving roughly half the quality headroom on the table.

\paragraph{Exploiting the price drop (Phase~2).}
When Gemini becomes nearly free in Phase~2, the BudgetPacer automatically detects the cost reduction via its EMA signal.
The three budget regimes produce qualitatively distinct---but equally principled---dual-variable responses (Figure~\ref{fig:adaptation_dynamics}).
At all three targets, post-drop costs fall below the budget, so $\lambda_t$ decays and the constraint becomes slack.
The decay speed depends on the margin: at moderate and loose targets ($0.53\times$ and $0.19\times$ respectively), $\lambda_t$ drops to near-zero and the router reverts to quality-only optimization, increasing Gemini selection to ${\sim}46$--$51\%$.
At tight target, the margin is narrower ($0.95\times$), so $\lambda_t$ decays from $0.43$ to ${\sim}0.07$---slower, but in the same direction.
The tight condition still achieves the highest Gemini adoption ($72\%$) of any constrained condition, because the residual $\lambda_t$ provides mild cost pressure favoring the now-cheapest arm (Gemini, $\tilde{c}{\approx}0$) over Mistral ($\tilde{c}{\approx}0.49$).
All three regimes show highly significant reward lifts ($\Delta{=}+0.025$ to $+0.069$; $p < 10^{-9}$), with the tight budget exhibiting the largest gain because its Phase~1 constraint was most binding, creating the most room for improvement when costs collapse.

\paragraph{Recovering after price restoration (Phase~3).}
When Gemini's original pricing is restored at step~1217, the BudgetPacer reverses course: $\lambda_t$ rises back toward Phase~1 levels as the cost EMA exceeds the budget target, and Gemini adoption drops to near-zero.
At tight budget, $\lambda_t$ rises from $0.07$ to ${\sim}0.55$; at moderate from $0.04$ to ${\sim}0.36$; at loose from near-zero to ${\sim}0.09$ (Figure~\ref{fig:adaptation_dynamics}a).
Phase~3 $\lambda_t$ does not exactly match Phase~1 values (e.g., tight: $0.55$ vs.\ $0.43$)---this asymmetry reflects the ramp-up transient from near-zero through the slack region and is consistent with the finite 608-step phase length.
Budget compliance recovers to $\bdParetoBanditTightPhaseThreeRatio$, $\bdParetoBanditModPhaseThreeRatio$, and $\bdParetoBanditLoosePhaseThreeRatio$ across the three targets---within $2\%$ of the budget at all levels.
No other condition achieves this round-trip compliance: the Recalibrated Bandit obtains a second oracle recalibration at the Phase~3 boundary yet still under-spends ($\bdRecalTightPhaseThreeRatio$--$\bdRecalLoosePhaseThreeRatio$), while the Naive Bandit cannot recover at moderate and loose budgets ($\bdNaiveModPhaseThreeRatio$ and $\bdNaiveLoosePhaseThreeRatio$).
This Phase~3 recovery is the strongest evidence that the primal-dual mechanism provides genuine closed-loop budget control rather than merely reacting to favorable cost changes.

\paragraph{Quality cost of the pacer.}
An unconstrained ParetoBandit baseline (same $\gamma{=}0.995$, no pacer) isolates the pacer's quality impact.
In Phase~2, the unconstrained baseline reaches reward $0.929$; ParetoBandit at moderate/loose budgets reaches $0.926$--$0.927$---a gap of ${\leq}0.3\%$.
The pacer itself is nearly free in quality terms.
This quality difference is the cost of non-stationarity robustness---validated here by the reward lifts that the forgetting factor enables.

\paragraph{Tight budgets: full round-trip adaptation.}
The tight target ($B{=}\$0.0003$/req) is the most instructive case because it transitions from \emph{binding} to \emph{slack} and back to \emph{binding} across the three phases.
In Phase~1, $\lambda_t$ averages $0.43$, enforcing a Llama/Mistral mix; after the price drop, $\lambda_t$ decays to ${\sim}0.07$ as costs fall below target.
The tight condition achieves the highest Gemini adoption ($72\%$) of any constrained condition.
In Phase~3, when original pricing is restored, $\lambda_t$ rises to ${\sim}0.55$ and Gemini selection returns to near-zero, recovering budget compliance at $\bdParetoBanditTightPhaseThreeRatio$.
This full round-trip---binding $\to$ slack $\to$ binding---demonstrates that the primal-dual mechanism adapts in both directions without operator intervention.
The tight budget also exhibits the largest reward lift ($\Delta{=}+0.069$) because its Phase~1 constraint was most binding, creating the most room for improvement when costs collapse.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection via Epsilon-Constraint}
\label{sec:exp4_hparam_sweep}

Selecting hyperparameters ($\alpha$, $n_\mathit{eff}$, $\gamma$) for a non-stationary router poses a fundamental tension: standard cross-validation maximises stationary quality and selects $\gamma{=}1.0$ (no forgetting) for both variants, yet Section~\ref{sec:exp2_reward_shift} showed that infinite memory fails to adapt under distribution drift.  The core idea of our resolution is simple: first, identify all hyperparameter settings that route well under normal (stationary) conditions; then, among those near-optimal settings, pick the one that adapts fastest when the environment changes---avoiding the trap of optimising purely for stationary performance, which would select settings that catastrophically fail under drift.  We formalise this with the \textbf{epsilon-constraint method}, a principled bi-objective selection procedure.

\paragraph{Two objectives.}
Each configuration in the grid ($\hpGridAlpha \times \hpGridNeff \times \hpGridGamma$ values of $\alpha$, $n_\mathit{eff}$, $\gamma$) is scored on two metrics using the validation split:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Budget-paced Pareto AUC} (primary): the area under the quality--cost Pareto frontier when sweeping budget targets with the BudgetPacer active. Higher is better.
  \item \emph{Phase-2 cumulative regret} (secondary): a two-phase simulation where, at the midpoint, two arms swap their reward \emph{and} cost distributions. Only post-swap regret is recorded, averaged over all $\binom{K}{2}$ swap pairs and 10 seeds. Lower is better.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Selection rule.}
Among all configurations whose budget-paced AUC is within $\epsilon{=}0.25\%$ of the best, we select the one with the lowest Phase-2 regret.  This gives budget-pacing explicit priority---no configuration is chosen that materially degrades stationary routing---while using adaptation speed as the tiebreaker within the near-optimal band.  The tolerance is calibrated to the observed AUC variation: the entire 144-configuration ParetoBandit grid spans only $0.39\%$ in budget-paced AUC, so $\epsilon{=}0.25\%$ covers roughly two-thirds of that range---tight enough to exclude materially worse configurations (32\% of the grid), yet permissive enough to admit a diverse set of $(\alpha, \gamma)$ combinations for the secondary objective to discriminate.

\paragraph{Result.}
Figure~\ref{fig:epsilon_tradeoff} visualises the tradeoff.  Configurations with $\gamma{=}1.0$ (blue) achieve the highest AUC but the worst post-swap regret: they cannot shed stale evidence.  Lower $\gamma$ values shift points downward (better adaptation) at marginal AUC cost.  The $\epsilon$-feasible band retains 98 of 144 ParetoBandit configurations (68\%), excluding primarily those with aggressive forgetting or very high exploration that trade too much stationary quality.  Within this feasible set, Phase-2 regret breaks ties.  The method selects $\gamma{=}\hpParetoBanditGamma$ (star) with $\alpha{=}\hpParetoBanditAlpha$, achieving Phase-2 regret of $\hpParetoBanditRegret$ while sacrificing only $\hpAUCSacrifice\%$ of stationary AUC ($\hpAUCOnlyAUC \to \hpParetoBanditAUC$).  The AUC-only winner ($\gamma{=}\hpAUCOnlyGamma$, red~\textbf{X}) selects $\alpha{=}\hpAUCOnlyAlpha$ with minimal exploration---exactly the configuration that fails under drift.  This ${\sim}200$-step effective memory window is long enough for stable exploitation, short enough to track realistic cost and quality shifts.

On the held-out test split the selected configurations achieve Pareto AUC of $\hpParetoBanditTestAUC$ (ParetoBandit) and $\hpTabulaTestAUC$ (Tabula Rasa) versus $\hpFixedTestAUC$ for the fixed-model envelope---deltas of $\hpParetoBanditTestDelta\%$ and $\hpTabulaTestDelta\%$, both within one standard deviation of seed variance ($\pm\hpParetoBanditTestStd$ and $\pm\hpTabulaTestStd$).  The val-selected hyperparameters thus transfer to test without statistically significant degradation, consistent with the $\epsilon$-constraint design which permits up to $0.25\%$ AUC sacrifice in exchange for adaptation capability.

\subsection{Cold-Start Model Onboarding}
\label{sec:model_onboarding_preview}

The experiments above assume a fixed $K{=}3$ portfolio.  In practice, providers release new models frequently, and platform teams need to integrate them without offline evaluation or policy retraining.  In Appendix~\ref{appendix:model_onboarding} we evaluate this scenario: after Phase~1 learning on the $K{=}3$ portfolio, Gemini-2.5-Flash is added via a single \texttt{register\_model()} call with \emph{no warmup priors}---it receives only a neutral T-shirt prior ($A{=}\lambda I$, $b{=}0$), the coldest possible LinUCB initialisation.

Despite this cold start, ParetoBandit discovers and adopts Flash in \emph{every} trial (80/80 across 20~seeds $\times$ 4~budget tiers), reaching sustained adoption within ${\sim}233$ post-onboarding steps.  LinUCB's exploration bonus---maximal for the uninformed arm---drives initial exploration, and the online learner quickly calibrates Flash's quality--cost profile from live traffic.  Crucially, the BudgetPacer maintains compliance throughout the portfolio expansion without any reconfiguration.  The displacement analysis reveals economically rational behaviour: under budget pressure, Flash takes share from Mistral-Large (similar quality, lower cost); unconstrained, it displaces Gemini-Pro (near-Pro quality at $10\times$ lower cost).

\paragraph{No historical labeled data required.}
A supervised router requires a \emph{pre-collected evaluation dataset} for every model in the portfolio: responses must be gathered on a representative prompt set, labeled by human annotators or an LLM judge, and the routing policy retrained on the expanded dataset---before the new model can receive any live traffic.  The bandit eliminates this offline data-collection campaign entirely.  It does require a reward signal, but that signal comes from the \emph{same} evaluation mechanism (judge, user ratings, or implicit behavioural composites; see Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) already serving the existing portfolio.  When exploration routes a prompt to the new model, the reward pipeline evaluates its response at zero marginal data-collection cost.  This distinction also avoids two risks inherent to offline evaluation: \emph{distribution mismatch}, where a curated eval set may not reflect the live prompt distribution, and \emph{dataset staleness}, where the eval ages as deployment patterns drift.  The bandit's ``training data'' is always the current production distribution, ensuring that the quality--cost profile it learns for the new model is representative from the start.
