\subsubsection{Comparison Across Cost Regimes}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}a reveals two distinct regimes, reflecting the complementary strengths of supervised pre-training and online adaptation:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{RouteLLM} is pre-trained on 100k out-of-distribution (OOD) supervised preference pairs, giving it strong out-of-the-box performance---no deployment data required.
    \item \textbf{banditGPT} combines 80k OOD battle priors with 1,121 in-distribution online learning steps, adapting from its own routing outcomes---no labels required.
\end{itemize}

At \textbf{low-to-moderate budgets} (\$0.0003--\$0.005 per request), RouteLLM's pre-trained classifier is competitive or superior. Its supervised training provides reliable routing without deployment overhead.

At \textbf{higher budgets} (\$0.005+), banditGPT surpasses RouteLLM. Online learning discovers the correct aggregate model ordering for this deployment distribution, achieving a peak quality of $0.914 \pm 0.003$ (95\% CI, $n{=}20$)---a $+3.5\%$ improvement over RouteLLM's peak of 0.883. The Oracle upper bound (0.953) routes only 13\% of prompts to GPT-4-Turbo with perfect per-prompt knowledge, representing the theoretical ceiling for this model pair.

\paragraph{Why RouteLLM wins at low budgets.} At tight budgets, both routers route the vast majority of traffic to Mixtral and allocate only a handful of prompts to GPT-4. Quality depends on selecting the \emph{right} prompts for the expensive model. RouteLLM's MF classifier, pre-trained on 100k preference pairs, encodes fine-grained prompt-level discrimination that banditGPT's 1,121 online examples cannot match at this granularity. In contrast, at higher budgets where 30--70\% of prompts are routed, \emph{aggregate model calibration} dominates per-prompt selection, and banditGPT's online learning---which discovers that Mixtral outperforms GPT-4 on this distribution---provides the larger advantage.

This evaluation uses two models to match RouteLLM's topology and enable a controlled comparison. Multi-model results ($K \geq 3$) with dedicated warmup priors are provided in the supplementary material.

\subsubsection{The Value of Online Adaptation}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}b reveals why this matters in practice. banditGPT starts below RouteLLM (cold-start quality: $0.839 \pm 0.004$ with priors only) but crosses RouteLLM's peak after approximately \textbf{200 in-distribution prompts}. By step 400, the crossover is robust (95\% CI lower bound $>0.898$, well above RouteLLM's 0.883). This crossover demonstrates a fundamental asymmetry: a small amount of in-distribution experience outweighs a large volume of out-of-distribution supervision.

The learning curve shows generally increasing improvement from cold-start ($0.839$) through convergence ($0.911$ by step 750), with minor fluctuations at intermediate checkpoints that remain within the 95\% confidence bands. The Corralling meta-learner decisively shifts $94\%$ of routing weight to the tabula rasa expert, producing consistent convergence across all 20 seeds. By step 750, the variance across trials contracts sharply (CI width $< 0.005$), indicating that the router has converged to a stable, high-quality policy.

\subsubsection{Non-Monotonicity of Static Routing}
\label{sec:nonmonotone}

A striking secondary finding is the non-monotonic behavior of RouteLLM at high cost. Dense threshold coverage (26 points, including 16 in the $[0.0, 0.15]$ range) reveals that when the threshold is lowered to admit more GPT-4 traffic ($\tau < 0.10$), RouteLLM's quality \emph{degrades}: reward drops from the peak of 0.883 (at $\tau{\approx}0.12$, cost \$0.007) to 0.812 at $\tau{=}0$ (cost \$0.013). At maximum cost, RouteLLM pays $43\times$ more than static Mixtral for $1.3\%$ lower quality on this distribution, consistent with model preference heterogeneity.

This non-monotonicity is not a pathology of RouteLLM's MF model specifically; it is inherent to any pre-trained router deployed on a distribution where the assumed ``strong'' model is not uniformly superior. In specialized domains (legal, code review, structured data extraction), smaller fine-tuned models frequently outperform general-purpose frontier models, making this scenario practically common.

\subsubsection{Deployment guidance (brief)}
These results suggest a simple pattern for practice: supervised static routers can be strong at tight budgets when their training data matches deployment, while online adaptation can be valuable when (i) prompt distributions drift, (ii) model ordering is uncertain, or (iii) the portfolio grows beyond two models. A more detailed decision guide and monitoring protocol is included in the supplementary material; quantitative crossover points are deployment-dependent.

\subsubsection{Gap Closure Analysis}

On the frozen holdout set ($N{=}750$), banditGPT closes \textbf{70.0\%} of the optimality gap ($\frac{0.914 - 0.823}{0.953 - 0.823}$), compared to 46.2\% for RouteLLM at its best threshold. At step 0 (priors only, before online learning), banditGPT achieves $0.839 \pm 0.004$, above static Mixtral but below RouteLLM's peak.
Operationally, banditGPT incurs inference cost for only the selected model per request; it requires no parallel evaluation of all candidates, unlike offline data collection for supervised routers, which must query every model on every prompt to obtain training labels.

Design ablations and hyperparameter sensitivity sweeps are reported in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Limitations (brief)}
Our controlled RouteLLM comparison uses a single model pair and a single prompt distribution; deployments with different portfolios and traffic will shift the quantitative cost crossover points. Our robustness experiments are stress tests rather than guarantees, and our current treatment of non-stationarity primarily operates through meta-level mixing rather than explicit forgetting within each expert.
