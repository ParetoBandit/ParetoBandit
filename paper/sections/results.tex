\section{Results \& Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate banditGPT on three dimensions: economic efficiency (Pareto optimality), safety under prior mismatch, and operational agility (adaptation to failures and new models). Section~\ref{sec:pareto} presents the primary benchmark; Section~\ref{sec:robustness_adaptation} summarizes safety and adaptation capabilities, with full figures and extended analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:adaptation_details}.

\subsubsection{Comparison Across Cost Regimes}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}a reveals two distinct regimes, reflecting the complementary strengths of supervised pre-training and online adaptation:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{RouteLLM} is pre-trained on 100k out-of-distribution (OOD) supervised preference pairs, giving it strong out-of-the-box performance---no deployment data required.
    \item \textbf{banditGPT} combines 80k OOD battle priors with 1,121 in-distribution online learning steps, adapting from its own routing outcomes---no labels required.
\end{itemize}

At \textbf{low-to-moderate budgets} (\$0.0003--\$0.005 per request), RouteLLM's pre-trained classifier is competitive or superior. Its supervised training provides reliable routing without deployment overhead.

At \textbf{higher budgets} (\$0.005+), banditGPT surpasses RouteLLM. Online learning discovers which prompts genuinely benefit from the expensive model, achieving a peak quality of $0.915 \pm 0.004$ (95\% CI, $n{=}20$)---a $+3.7\%$ improvement over RouteLLM's peak of 0.883. The Oracle upper bound (0.953) routes only 13\% of prompts to GPT-4-Turbo with perfect per-prompt knowledge, representing the theoretical ceiling for this model pair.

This evaluation uses two models to match RouteLLM's topology and enable a controlled comparison; the contextual bandit formulation naturally extends to larger portfolios ($K \geq 3$), where per-model uncertainty diverges and exploration becomes more informative. Factors affecting the crossover point across deployments are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{The Value of Online Adaptation}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}b reveals why this matters in practice. banditGPT starts below RouteLLM (cold-start quality: 0.795 with priors only) but crosses RouteLLM's peak after just \textbf{400 in-distribution prompts}. This crossover demonstrates a fundamental asymmetry: a small amount of in-distribution experience outweighs a large volume of out-of-distribution supervision.

The learning curve also reveals non-monotonic dynamics: quality temporarily plateaus around steps 200--300 before resuming improvement, reflecting the stochastic expert selection of the Corralling meta-learner as it re-weights the warmup and tabula rasa experts. By step 700, the variance across trials contracts sharply (CI width $< 0.01$), indicating that the router has converged to a stable, high-quality policy.

\subsubsection{Non-Monotonicity of Static Routing}

A secondary finding is the non-monotonic behavior of RouteLLM at high cost. When its threshold is lowered to admit more GPT-4 traffic ($\tau < 0.10$), RouteLLM's quality \emph{degrades}. At maximum cost (\$0.013), it achieves 0.812---worse than static Mixtral (0.823)---paying $43\times$ more for $1.3\%$ \emph{lower} quality. This is a predictable consequence of model preference heterogeneity (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}): static routers cannot distinguish the ${\sim}14\%$ of prompts where the cheaper model is empirically better, and over-provision GPT-4 on this subset. banditGPT avoids this trap by learning the preference structure online.

\subsubsection{When to Use banditGPT}

These results suggest clear deployment guidelines:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Use RouteLLM} when deployment is immediate, the prompt distribution is well-represented by available supervised data, and moderate quality suffices.
    \item \textbf{Use banditGPT} when:
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
        \item \emph{No labeled routing data exists} for the target domain---banditGPT requires zero labels, learning from its own routing outcomes.
        \item \emph{The prompt distribution shifts} over time (new users, new tasks, seasonal changes)---banditGPT adapts continuously while RouteLLM is frozen at training time.
        \item \emph{Multiple models ($K \geq 3$)} span different cost-quality tiers---banditGPT's contextual bandit formulation natively handles multi-model portfolios, while threshold-based routers require $O(K^2)$ pairwise comparisons.
        \item \emph{Maximum quality is the priority}---after a short burn-in (${\sim}400$ prompts), banditGPT's adaptive policy surpasses what supervised pre-training can achieve with $250\times$ more data.
        \item \emph{Bounded exploration overhead}---even during the burn-in period, banditGPT routes the majority of traffic to high-performing candidates (quality starts at 0.795 with priors alone and improves monotonically), so the system provides value from the first request while continuously improving.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

The 400-prompt crossover means a new deployment can surpass a state-of-the-art pre-trained router within minutes of production traffic, with no human annotation effort. The structural advantages---no labels required, continuous adaptation, native multi-model support---are general, though the quantitative crossover point is deployment-dependent (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations} for factors affecting generalizability).

\paragraph{Key Findings.}
\textbf{(1)~Learning matters.} All learning-based methods substantially outperform random routing ($>10\%$ quality improvement), confirming that adaptive allocation provides real value over static policies.

\textbf{(2)~Tabula rasa achieves the highest peak quality} (0.923 vs.\ 0.912 for the full system). This result is expected, not paradoxical: in this ablation, all 1,121 dev prompts flow through a single expert with zero prior bias. The warmup priors---trained on out-of-distribution RouteLLM data---introduce a small bias that online learning must overcome. In production settings where prior quality is unknown or deployment distribution differs from training, this bias risk is exactly what Corralling is designed to hedge against.

\textbf{(3)~Corralling excels when priors are reasonable.} The prior degradation sweep (Section~\ref{sec:prior_degradation}) shows that Corralling achieves 30--40\% lower regret than both alternatives when priors range from correct to moderately corrupted ($\alpha \leq 0.5$). However, under adversarial priors ($\alpha \geq 0.6$), Corralling's regret exceeds both warmup-only and tabula rasa, with high variance ($\sigma{\approx}23$). The value of Corralling is therefore not worst-case insurance across \emph{all} prior qualities, but superior performance in the operating regime that covers most practical deployments---moderate distribution shift from offline training data.

\textbf{(4)~Why Corralling outperforms warmup-only even with perfect priors.}
The ``UCB Only (w/ priors)'' ablation isolates the warmup expert---which uses constant exploration ($\alpha{=}2.0$, no decay) to preserve distribution-shift detection. This perpetual exploration incurs regret even after the optimal policy is well-characterized: the warmup expert keeps second-guessing its own correct beliefs. The tabula rasa expert, by contrast, decays its exploration ($\alpha: 1.0 \to 0.01$) and eventually \emph{commits} to the best policy it has learned. Corralling exploits this complementarity: it relies on the warmup expert's informed early selections, then shifts meta-weight toward the converging tabula rasa expert, capturing the benefits of both fast initialization \emph{and} eventual exploitation. The warmup-only ablation pays the full cost of perpetual exploration, which is why Corralling dominates it across the entire corruption spectrum (Section~\ref{sec:prior_degradation}).

\subsubsection{Prior Degradation Sweep}
\label{sec:prior_degradation}

To understand when Corralling helps and when it hurts, we sweep prior quality from \emph{correct} ($\alpha{=}0$: original priors) through \emph{uninformative} ($\alpha{=}0.5$: averaged between models) to \emph{adversarial} ($\alpha{=}1.0$: model preferences fully swapped). Figure~\ref{fig:prior_degradation} shows cumulative regret for Corralling, warmup-only, and tabula rasa ($N{=}100$ seeds per condition).

\paragraph{Two regimes, one crossover.}
The sweep reveals two distinct operating regimes separated by a crossover near $\alpha{\approx}0.55$:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Good-to-moderate priors ($\alpha \leq 0.5$):} Corralling achieves the lowest regret (32--38), substantially beating both warmup-only (52--58) and tabula rasa (52). The meta-learner exploits the complementary alpha schedules (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}): it leverages the warmup expert's informed early selections, then shifts weight toward the converging tabula rasa expert.
\item \textbf{Adversarial priors ($\alpha \geq 0.6$):} Corralling's regret rises sharply (65--78) with high variance ($\sigma{\approx}23$), exceeding both baselines. The warmup expert's constant exploration ($\alpha{=}2.0$) amplifies systematically wrong prior beliefs, and some seeds fail to shift weight to the tabula rasa expert quickly enough. Tabula rasa, which ignores priors entirely, maintains flat regret (${\approx}52$) regardless of corruption.
\end{enumerate}

Warmup-only degrades monotonically with corruption (52 to 67) but is \emph{never} the best strategy at any corruption level---Corralling dominates it across the entire spectrum, and tabula rasa matches or beats it for $\alpha \geq 0.0$.

\paragraph{Practical interpretation.}
The corruption level $\alpha$ measures how much the deployment distribution has shifted from the offline training data. In concrete terms:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
\item $\alpha{=}0$: deployment matches training (e.g., same user population, same prompt types).
\item $\alpha{\approx}0.3$: moderate shift (e.g., training on general chat, deploying on code-heavy workloads). Corralling is at its \emph{best} here---it adapts faster than warmup-only and starts better than tabula rasa.
\item $\alpha{=}0.5$: priors are roughly uninformative (the offline data tells you nothing useful about which model to prefer). Corralling still outperforms alternatives but with diminishing advantage.
\item $\alpha{>}0.6$: priors are actively misleading (e.g., training on English data, deploying on a language where model rankings reverse). Corralling's variance becomes unacceptable; tabula rasa is the safer choice.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Is the instability worth the improvement?}
We frame this as an expected-value decision.
At good-to-moderate priors ($\alpha \leq 0.4$), Corralling saves 17.7 cumulative regret (34\% reduction vs.\ tabula rasa), with low variance ($\sigma{\approx}4$).
At adversarial priors ($\alpha \geq 0.6$), Corralling costs 23.2 extra regret (45\% increase), with high variance ($\sigma{\approx}22$).
The breakeven occurs when a practitioner believes there is roughly a 57\% chance that priors are useful ($\alpha \leq 0.4$).
Above this threshold, Corralling's expected regret is lower; below it, tabula rasa is safer.

\noindent In practice, most deployments use priors derived from a related domain (e.g., training on general chat, deploying on customer support), placing them in the $\alpha \leq 0.4$ regime.  Truly adversarial priors---where model preferences are \emph{systematically reversed}---require an active distribution mismatch (e.g., training on English, deploying on a language where model quality rankings invert).  This is rare and typically known in advance.

\paragraph{Detecting and escaping instability.}
Crucially, the adversarial regime is \emph{detectable at runtime}. Two mechanisms already built into the router provide partial protection:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
\item \textbf{$\gamma$-floor mixing} ($\gamma{=}0.1$): the meta-learner always allocates ${\geq}5\%$ of traffic to each expert via the mixed distribution $P_t = (1{-}\gamma)\,w_t + \gamma/K$, ensuring the tabula rasa expert continues learning even when the meta-learner favors the misleading warmup expert.
\item \textbf{Loss decay}: stale expert losses are forgotten, so early mistakes from bad priors do not permanently anchor the meta-learner.
\end{itemize}

\noindent For additional robustness, practitioners can monitor the first 50--100 prompts and compare the warmup expert's predictions against observed rewards.  If the warmup expert's running accuracy falls below random chance, switching to tabula rasa eliminates the downside while sacrificing only the modest burn-in already invested.  Automating this check---freezing warmup-expert weight when its running reward drops below the tabula rasa expert's---is a natural extension that would cap worst-case regret near the tabula rasa baseline (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Default recommendation.}
Use Corralling unless you have strong reason to believe your offline priors are adversarial.  The 57\% breakeven threshold is easily cleared for most deployments, and early monitoring provides an escape hatch for the rare case where it is not.

\subsubsection{Catastrophic Failure Detection}

We evaluated Corralling's response to sudden model degradation on a \textbf{5-model portfolio} ($K{=}5$, $N{=}20$ seeds) where GPT-4-Turbo quality drops from $\mu{=}0.82$ to $\mu{=}0.15$ (Cohen's $d{\approx}5.0$) while four alternatives remain healthy. Corralling detects the failure in 95\% of seeds (median 34 steps) and partially recovers---but a simple EMA tracker reacts faster (reward $0.735$ vs.\ $0.646$ during failure, $\Delta{=}{-}0.090$). Notably, this gap \textbf{narrows monotonically with portfolio size}: $\Delta{=}{-}0.286$ at $K{=}2$, ${-}0.154$ at $K{=}3$, ${-}0.090$ at $K{=}5$. EMA degrades as $K$ grows because its exploration budget ($\epsilon/K$) spreads thinner, while Corralling's contextual routing becomes more valuable when post-failure routing is a multi-model decision.

\emph{Practical distinction:} The two methods differ not only in speed but in \emph{how} they redistribute traffic after failure. EMA concentrates 63\% of failure-phase traffic on a single fallback model, creating a new single point of failure. Corralling distributes traffic roughly uniformly (${\sim}20\%$ each) across the four healthy models based on per-prompt context, providing both load balancing and context-dependent quality. This suggests a complementary deployment architecture: lightweight EMA or CUSUM detectors for rapid model-level alerting, with Corralling handling the actual contextual traffic redistribution.
A further operational benefit is that Corralling's continuous exploration provides model regression detection as an intrinsic byproduct of the learning process: if a previously strong model degrades after a vendor update, the exploration budget detects the change without requiring a separate canary or monitoring pipeline.
Full dynamics and practical deployment recommendations in Appendix~\ref{app:catastrophic_failure}.

\subsubsection{Semantic Transfer: A Null Result}
\label{sec:transfer_null}

We evaluated semantic transfer for cold-starting new models (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}) in a leave-one-out design with 10 shuffled seeds at $K{=}5$ and $K{=}10$.

\paragraph{Result.} Semantic transfer provides \textbf{no statistically significant advantage} over tabula rasa. Paired $t$-tests across all horizons ($N{=}10$ to $500$) yield $p > 0.07$ at both portfolio sizes. A ``direction-only'' ablation (transferring $\thetavec$ while preserving full exploration, $\Amat{=}\mathbf{I}$) similarly shows no significant effect.

\paragraph{Diagnosis.} The new model is selected for $<1\%$ of prompts in most configurations, because established models dominate routing decisions. In adversarial conditions (cross-family transfer), the mechanism is \emph{harmful}, degrading reward by 0.6--0.9\% with higher variance. All initialization strategies converge within a few hundred interactions regardless, making cold-start integration an open problem where no shortcut via prior transfer currently exists.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Two-Model Evaluation Scope.}
All Pareto and learning curve experiments use a single model pair (Mixtral / GPT-4-Turbo), chosen to match RouteLLM's evaluation topology for controlled comparison. With only two models, the UCB exploration bonus is symmetric at initialization, making $\alpha$ largely irrelevant for model selection. The crossover dynamics reported in Section~\ref{sec:pareto} are specific to this model pair and prompt distribution. The crossover point depends on: (1)~training data coverage---for model pairs well-represented in RouteLLM's training set, its advantage persists longer; (2)~preference complexity---when preferences follow a simple difficulty threshold, supervised classification captures them efficiently; and (3)~portfolio size---with $K \geq 3$, model uncertainties diverge and contextual exploration becomes genuinely informative. Practitioners should expect different quantitative results with larger portfolios.

\paragraph{Prior Quality Dependency and Automatic Fallback.}
The prior degradation sweep (Section~\ref{sec:prior_degradation}) reveals a sharp phase transition at $\alpha \approx 0.55$: Corralling's advantage collapses and its variance increases $5\times$ ($\sigma{\approx}23$ vs.\ ${\approx}4$). The bimodal variance indicates that some seeds successfully shift weight to the tabula rasa expert while others remain trapped on the misleading warmup expert. The $\gamma$-floor and loss decay provide partial mitigation, but are insufficient to guarantee recovery in all seeds. An \emph{automatic warmup-expert freeze}---monitoring the warmup expert's running reward and clamping its meta-weight to zero when it falls below the tabula rasa expert's running average---would cap worst-case regret near the tabula rasa baseline while preserving the 34\% upside when priors are useful. Implementing and validating this mechanism is a priority for future work.

\paragraph{Non-Stationarity Scope.}
Our corralling meta-learner incorporates loss decay to adapt expert weights under non-stationary conditions, but each expert bandit is itself trained under a stationary-reward assumption (monotone accumulation without forgetting). Thus, our treatment of non-stationarity operates at the meta level only: if a model's reward distribution drifts gradually within an expert's arm, the expert cannot adapt except via meta-level reweighting or replacement. Extending expert-level updates with explicit forgetting (e.g., sliding windows, discounted UCB) or change-point detection mechanisms is an interesting direction for future work.

\paragraph{Corralling Learning Rate.}
In principle, the corralling learning rate admits an optimal schedule $\eta^* \propto \sqrt{\ln K / T}$ for a given horizon, but our implementation uses a fixed $\eta$ (0.1 or 1.0) tuned for the typical horizon in our application. The staleness-aware loss decay mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) handles non-stationarity via forgetting of stale feedback; it partially masks horizon mis-specification but is not a substitute for an optimal corralling learning-rate schedule. Adaptive or horizon-aware $\eta_t$ schedules are left for future work.

\paragraph{Isotropic Prior in PCA Space.}
We apply PCA for dimensionality reduction and initialize the precision matrix as $\Amat_0 = \lambda \mathbf{I}$, which corresponds to an isotropic regularizer in the PCA space. This does not distinguish between high- and low-variance principal components and may over-regularize the latter. Designing anisotropic or variance-matched regularization in the PCA basis (e.g., diagonal $\Amat_0$ scaled by component variance, or full whitening prior to the bandit layer) is a natural extension.

\paragraph{Variance and Reproducibility.}
Multi-seed evaluation reveals substantial variance in expert weight evolution (final warmup weight: $0.382 \pm 0.471$, standard deviation exceeds mean). Outcomes are influenced by stochastic request ordering; practitioners should monitor deployment-specific dynamics rather than relying on population-level predictions.
