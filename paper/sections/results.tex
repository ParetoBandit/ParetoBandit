\section{Results \& Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate banditGPT on three dimensions: economic efficiency (Pareto optimality), safety under prior mismatch, and operational agility (adaptation to failures and new models). Section~\ref{sec:pareto} presents the primary benchmark; Section~\ref{sec:robustness_adaptation} summarizes safety and adaptation capabilities, with full figures and extended analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:adaptation_details}.

\subsubsection{Honest Comparison: Two Regimes}

An honest reading of Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}a reveals that \emph{neither method dominates everywhere}. The comparison is between two fundamentally different paradigms with complementary data advantages:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{RouteLLM} is pre-trained on 100k out-of-distribution (OOD) supervised preference pairs from the LMSYS Chatbot Arena, giving it strong out-of-the-box performance at moderate cost levels---no deployment data required.
    \item \textbf{banditGPT} combines 80k OOD battle priors with 1,121 in-distribution online learning steps, where it observes its own routing outcomes and adapts---no labels required.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Note on model portfolio size.}
This comparison uses only two models (Mixtral and GPT-4-Turbo) to match RouteLLM's primary evaluation topology and enable a controlled comparison. This is a disadvantage for banditGPT: its contextual bandit formulation is designed for multi-model portfolios where per-model uncertainty guides exploration. With only two models, the exploration bonus is symmetric and contributes minimally to routing decisions.
In practice, production deployments typically involve three or more models spanning different cost-quality tiers. The contextual bandit formulation naturally extends to larger portfolios, where per-model uncertainty guides exploration across heterogeneous cost-quality tiers.

At \textbf{low-to-moderate budgets} (\$0.0003--\$0.005 per request), RouteLLM's pre-trained threshold classifier is competitive or superior. Its supervised training on 100k pairs provides reliable moderate-quality routing without any deployment overhead.

At \textbf{higher budgets} (\$0.005+), banditGPT surpasses RouteLLM. Online learning discovers which prompts genuinely benefit from the expensive model, achieving a peak quality of $0.915 \pm 0.004$ (95\% CI, $n{=}20$)---a +3.7\% improvement over RouteLLM's peak of 0.883. The Oracle upper bound (0.953) is achieved by a clairvoyant router with perfect per-prompt knowledge; it routes only 13\% of prompts to GPT-4-Turbo at a cost of \$0.002, representing the theoretical ceiling for this model pair.

\subsubsection{The Value of Online Adaptation}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}b reveals why this matters in practice. banditGPT starts below RouteLLM (cold-start quality: 0.795 with priors only) but crosses RouteLLM's peak after just \textbf{400 in-distribution prompts}. This crossover demonstrates a fundamental asymmetry: a small amount of in-distribution experience outweighs a large volume of out-of-distribution supervision.

The learning curve also reveals non-monotonic dynamics: quality temporarily plateaus around steps 200--300 before resuming improvement, reflecting the stochastic expert selection of the Corralling meta-learner as it re-weights the warmup and tabula rasa experts. By step 700, the variance across trials contracts sharply (CI width $< 0.01$), indicating that the router has converged to a stable, high-quality policy.

\subsubsection{Non-Monotonicity of Static Routing}

A secondary finding is the non-monotonic behavior of RouteLLM at high cost. When its threshold is lowered to admit more GPT-4 traffic ($\tau < 0.10$), RouteLLM's quality \emph{degrades}. At maximum cost (\$0.013), it achieves 0.812---worse than static Mixtral (0.823)---paying $43\times$ more for $1.3\%$ \emph{lower} quality. This is a predictable consequence of model preference heterogeneity (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}): static routers cannot distinguish the ${\sim}14\%$ of prompts where the cheaper model is empirically better, and over-provision GPT-4 on this subset. banditGPT avoids this trap by learning the preference structure online.

\subsubsection{When to Use banditGPT}

These results suggest clear deployment guidelines:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Use RouteLLM} when deployment is immediate, the prompt distribution is well-represented by available supervised data, and moderate quality suffices.
    \item \textbf{Use banditGPT} when:
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
        \item \emph{No labeled routing data exists} for the target domain---banditGPT requires zero labels, learning from its own routing outcomes.
        \item \emph{The prompt distribution shifts} over time (new users, new tasks, seasonal changes)---banditGPT adapts continuously while RouteLLM is frozen at training time.
        \item \emph{New models enter the portfolio}---banditGPT's semantic transfer mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) integrates new models online via learned priors from semantic neighbors, while RouteLLM requires retraining.
        \item \emph{Maximum quality is the priority}---after a short burn-in (${\sim}400$ prompts), banditGPT's adaptive policy surpasses what supervised pre-training can achieve with $250\times$ more data.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

The 400-prompt crossover is particularly striking: it means a new deployment can surpass a state-of-the-art pre-trained router within minutes of production traffic, with no human annotation effort.

\paragraph{Generalizability caveat.}
The two-regime pattern in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}a---RouteLLM ahead at low budgets, banditGPT ahead at high budgets---should \emph{not} be assumed to hold universally across model pairs.
The crossover point depends on at least three factors:
\textbf{(1)~Training data coverage.}
RouteLLM-MF learns latent factors per model from LMSYS Arena data. For model pairs well-represented in its training set \emph{and} a deployment distribution close to LMSYS Arena, RouteLLM's advantage persists longer and the crossover may require thousands of prompts rather than hundreds.
For model pairs absent from RouteLLM's training data---or entirely new models---it has no signal and requires retraining, while banditGPT begins learning immediately.
\textbf{(2)~Preference complexity.}
When model preference follows a simple ``difficulty'' threshold, a supervised classifier captures it efficiently and online learning adds little. When preferences are context-dependent and counter-intuitive (as we observe here---Section~\ref{sec:motivation}), per-prompt adaptive learning has a structural advantage.
\textbf{(3)~Portfolio size.}
Our evaluation uses only two models to match RouteLLM's setup, where the UCB exploration bonus is symmetric at initialization and contributes minimally to model selection. With larger portfolios ($K \geq 3$), model uncertainties diverge, exploration becomes genuinely informative, and the contextual bandit formulation should provide a larger advantage over threshold-based routing.
The claim we can support from this experiment is specific: \emph{on this model pair and prompt distribution, 400 in-distribution prompts of label-free online learning surpass 100k out-of-distribution supervised pairs.} The structural advantages of banditGPT---no labels required, continuous adaptation, automatic new-model integration---are general, but the quantitative crossover point is deployment-dependent.

\subsection{Robustness and Adaptation}
\label{sec:robustness_adaptation}

Production systems additionally require safety under prior mismatch and rapid failure detection. We summarize the key findings here; full experimental details, figures, and statistical tests appear in Appendix~\ref{app:adaptation_details}.

\subsubsection{Catastrophic Failure Detection}

We evaluated Corralling's response to sudden model degradation on a \textbf{5-model portfolio} ($K{=}5$, $N{=}20$ seeds) where GPT-4-Turbo quality drops from $\mu{=}0.82$ to $\mu{=}0.15$ (Cohen's $d{\approx}5.0$) while four alternatives remain healthy. Corralling detects the failure in 95\% of seeds (median 34 steps) and partially recovers---but a simple EMA tracker reacts faster (reward $0.735$ vs.\ $0.646$ during failure, $\Delta{=}{-}0.090$). Notably, this gap \textbf{narrows monotonically with portfolio size}: $\Delta{=}{-}0.286$ at $K{=}2$, ${-}0.154$ at $K{=}3$, ${-}0.090$ at $K{=}5$. EMA degrades as $K$ grows because its exploration budget ($\epsilon/K$) spreads thinner, while Corralling's contextual routing becomes more valuable when post-failure routing is a multi-model decision.

\emph{Practical distinction:} The two methods differ not only in speed but in \emph{how} they redistribute traffic after failure. EMA concentrates 63\% of failure-phase traffic on a single fallback model, creating a new single point of failure. Corralling distributes traffic roughly uniformly (${\sim}20\%$ each) across the four healthy models based on per-prompt context, providing both load balancing and context-dependent quality. This suggests a complementary deployment architecture: lightweight EMA or CUSUM detectors for rapid model-level alerting, with Corralling handling the actual contextual traffic redistribution. Full dynamics and practical deployment recommendations in Appendix~\ref{app:catastrophic_failure}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Two-Model Evaluation Scope.}
All Pareto and learning curve experiments use a single model pair (Mixtral / GPT-4-Turbo), chosen to match RouteLLM's primary evaluation topology rather than to showcase banditGPT's strengths. With only two models, the LinUCB exploration bonus is symmetric at initialization---both models have identical uncertainty---making the exploration parameter $\alpha$ largely irrelevant for model selection (it scales both scores equally). This limits our ability to evaluate UCB exploration, which is designed for settings where model uncertainties diverge ($K \geq 3$). The crossover dynamics, regime boundaries, and optimal hyperparameters reported here are specific to this model pair and prompt distribution; practitioners should expect different quantitative results---likely more favorable to banditGPT---with larger portfolios.

\paragraph{Prior Quality Dependency.}
Our exploration parameter ($\alpha_0{=}1.0$, decaying to $\alpha_{\text{end}}{=}0.1$) is validated under domain mismatch between offline training and holdout evaluation data. In low-mismatch deployments with accurate priors, adaptive exploration schedules may improve sample efficiency. Future work should investigate mismatch-aware $\alpha$ schedules.

\paragraph{Strategy Selection Trade-offs.}
Corralling incurs modest overhead relative to the tabula rasa baseline under moderate distribution shift, and warmup-only outperforms both when priors are well-calibrated. The hedging overhead is justified only under genuine prior uncertainty. When prior quality can be validated on held-out deployment data, direct strategy selection (tabula rasa for known-bad priors, warmup-only for validated-good priors) outperforms hedging.

\paragraph{Non-Stationarity Scope.}
Our corralling meta-learner incorporates loss decay to adapt expert weights under non-stationary conditions, but each expert bandit is itself trained under a stationary-reward assumption (monotone accumulation without forgetting). Thus, our treatment of non-stationarity operates at the meta level only: if a model's reward distribution drifts gradually within an expert's arm, the expert cannot adapt except via meta-level reweighting or replacement. Extending expert-level updates with explicit forgetting (e.g., sliding windows, discounted UCB) or change-point detection mechanisms is an interesting direction for future work.

\paragraph{Corralling Learning Rate.}
In principle, the corralling learning rate admits an optimal schedule $\eta^* \propto \sqrt{\ln K / T}$ for a given horizon, but our implementation uses a fixed $\eta$ (0.1 or 1.0) tuned for the typical horizon in our application. The staleness-aware loss decay mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) handles non-stationarity via forgetting of stale feedback; it partially masks horizon mis-specification but is not a substitute for an optimal corralling learning-rate schedule. Adaptive or horizon-aware $\eta_t$ schedules are left for future work.

\paragraph{Isotropic Prior in PCA Space.}
We apply PCA for dimensionality reduction and initialize the precision matrix as $\Amat_0 = \lambda \mathbf{I}$, which corresponds to an isotropic regularizer in the PCA space. This does not distinguish between high- and low-variance principal components and may over-regularize the latter. Designing anisotropic or variance-matched regularization in the PCA basis (e.g., diagonal $\Amat_0$ scaled by component variance, or full whitening prior to the bandit layer) is a natural extension.

\paragraph{Variance and Reproducibility.}
Multi-seed evaluation reveals substantial variance in expert weight evolution (final warmup weight: $0.382 \pm 0.471$, standard deviation exceeds mean). Outcomes are influenced by stochastic request ordering; practitioners should monitor deployment-specific dynamics rather than relying on population-level predictions.
