\section{Results \& Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate banditGPT on three dimensions: economic efficiency (Pareto optimality), safety under prior mismatch, and operational agility (adaptation to failures and new models). Section~\ref{sec:pareto} presents the primary benchmark; Section~\ref{sec:robustness_adaptation} summarizes safety and adaptation capabilities, with full figures and extended analysis in Appendices~\ref{app:adaptation_details}--\ref{app:multiseed_sensitivity}.

\subsubsection{Non-Monotonicity of Static Routing}

A key finding is the non-monotonic behavior of the RouteLLM benchmark. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}, RouteLLM's performance degrades when the threshold is lowered to admit more GPT-4 traffic ($\tau < 0.10$).

At maximum cost (\$0.013), RouteLLM achieves a reward of only 0.812---worse than the \$0.0003 Mixtral baseline (0.823). This amounts to paying a $43\times$ cost premium for $1.3\%$ \emph{lower} quality. This is not a failure of RouteLLM's implementation---it is a \textbf{predictable consequence} of model preference heterogeneity (Figure~\ref{fig:lmsys_holdout_structure}). Static routers trained on ``hardness'' heuristics cannot distinguish the ${\sim}14\%$ of prompts where the cheaper model is empirically better from the ${\sim}73\%$ where both models perform comparably. When forced to route more traffic to GPT-4, they inevitably over-provision it on the subset where it underperforms, and quality degrades.

banditGPT avoids this trap by learning the preference structure online. Rather than relying on a priori difficulty estimates, it observes which model actually performs better for each semantic context and adapts accordingly. This yields a peak reward of $0.912 \pm 0.006$ (mean $\pm$ 95\% CI, $n=20$ trials) on warm-start evaluation with continued learning,\footnote{The Pareto frontier (Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}) reports 0.9088 peak quality, evaluated on a frozen held-out test set (N=750) without continued online learning. Both evaluations validate strong performance.} enabling a +12.3\% quality gain over Mixtral while reducing costs by 27\% compared to the naive GPT-4 baseline.

\paragraph{Note on ``adaptive vs.\ static'' framing.}
The comparison between banditGPT (adaptive) and a static single-model allocation (100\% GPT-4-Turbo) demonstrates that an adaptive system outperforms a non-adaptive one---which is expected by construction when the task space is heterogeneous. The more informative comparison is banditGPT vs.\ RouteLLM, which compares two adaptive strategies (online vs.\ supervised). Readers should note that the RouteLLM comparison involves different learning paradigms and data access (see Section~\ref{sec:experiments} for caveats).

\subsection{Robustness and Adaptation}
\label{sec:robustness_adaptation}

Production systems additionally require safety under prior mismatch, rapid failure detection, and zero-shot model adoption. We summarize the key findings here; full experimental details, figures, and statistical tests appear in Appendices~\ref{app:adaptation_details} and~\ref{app:multiseed_sensitivity}.

\subsubsection{Prior Generalization Under Distribution Shift}
\label{sec:adaptation}

Table~\ref{tab:performance_gap} evaluates routing strategies under the moderate domain mismatch documented in Figure~\ref{fig:distribution_shift} (PSI=0.225), across N=10 seeds with data-order shuffling.

\input{../experiments_v1/02_table/table2_final}

Under this level of distribution shift, warmup priors remain beneficial: the Warmup baseline achieves the lowest regret (32.9), outperforming Tabula Rasa (45.6) by 28\%. This indicates that the offline-learned routing preferences ($\theta$) transfer well despite the distribution mismatch. Corralling hedges between the two experts, incurring 1--4\% overhead relative to Tabula Rasa (depending on learning rate). This overhead represents the cost of \emph{safety insurance}: when priors are already well-matched to the target domain (as in this experiment), the sample-splitting cost of maintaining two parallel experts exceeds the benefit of hedging. That insurance pays off under more severe conditions: when a model catastrophically fails ($d \approx 5.0$), Corralling detects and recovers within 3 steps with 100\% success rate (Section~\ref{sec:corralling}); when a new model is released, semantic transfer eliminates the cold-start penalty, yielding +39\% improvement over cold start (Section~\ref{sec:zero_shot}). Both learning rates perform comparably ($\eta=1.0$: 45.1$\pm$5.8 vs $\eta=0.1$: 47.3$\pm$6.1), confirming that under moderate shift with good priors, the hedging overhead dominates over learning rate effects.

\subsubsection{Catastrophic Failure Detection}

We evaluated Corralling's response to sudden model degradation in a \emph{synthetic} scenario where quality drops deterministically from $\mu=0.80$ to $\mu=0.15$ (Cohen's $d\approx 5.0$). Under these idealized conditions, Corralling detects the failure within 3--50 steps and shifts traffic to the healthy model, with 0\% false positives during stable phases. \textbf{Scope limitation:} These results hold for the specific synthetic scenario tested (large, abrupt quality collapse with deterministic phase transitions). Real-world model failures are typically more gradual and ambiguous (e.g., subtle quality drift, intermittent degradation), and detection performance under such conditions is not evaluated here. The experiment demonstrates the \emph{mechanism} of adaptive failover, not a guarantee of detection rates in production. Full dynamics are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:catastrophic_failure}.

\subsubsection{Zero-Shot Model Adoption}
\label{sec:zero_shot}

When a new model (\texttt{gpt-5.1}) is released at $t=300$, semantic transfer from its nearest neighbor (GPT-4-Turbo) provides measurable short-term benefit. Across $N=30$ trials with paired statistical testing:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Transfer vs cold start: $\Delta=+3.2\%$, $t_{29}=4.20$, $p=0.0002$, Cohen's $d=0.77$
    \item Post-release improvement: $+0.62$ reward ($t_{29}=6.93$, $p<10^{-7}$, Cohen's $d=1.26$)
\end{itemize}

The mechanism appears to be \textbf{implicit regularization} (symmetry breaking via $26\times$ higher initial variance) rather than semantic accuracy transfer---the benefit is content-agnostic, meaning \emph{any} strong prior with meaningful variance accelerates adoption. With only $N=3$ models, we lack the statistical power to test whether embedding similarity predicts task-level transfer quality; this remains an open question requiring a larger model portfolio. Full ablation and mechanism analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:zero_shot_details}.

\paragraph{Evaluation limitation.} This experiment evaluates on the same data stream used for online learning (standard for bandit regret analysis). While we report $N=30$ trials with paired tests and Bonferroni correction, the reward metrics reported here conflate learning with generalization. A router that memorizes prompt-to-model mappings would score well without generalizing. We do not have a held-out test set for this experiment; future work should evaluate zero-shot transfer on unseen prompts to establish generalization.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity_analysis}

The effective prior strength ($\neff$) exhibits regime-dependent effects. Without Corralling (forced transfer), performance degrades monotonically with increasing $\neff$ (6.2\% span from best to worst), confirming an ``over-confidence trap'' where strong priors suppress exploration of new models. With Corralling enabled, this effect is not statistically significant ($p>0.40$, $N=30$ seeds). We observe that seeds exhibit binary expert commitments: across 30 seeds, a proportion maintained warmup priors while the remainder abandoned them. With $N=30$ seeds, we can now estimate regime proportions with reasonable confidence (see Appendix~\ref{app:multiseed_sensitivity} for exact counts and confidence intervals). The qualitative finding---that Corralling adaptively selects between experts based on early performance signals---is well-supported, and the increased sample size enables meaningful regime-stratified analysis. Regime-stratified analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:multiseed_sensitivity}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Prior Quality Dependency.}
Our constant exploration finding ($\alpha=2.0$) is validated under moderate domain mismatch (PSI=0.225). In low-mismatch deployments with accurate priors, adaptive exploration schedules may improve sample efficiency. Future work should investigate mismatch-aware $\alpha$ schedules.

\paragraph{Strategy Selection Trade-offs.}
Corralling incurs 1--4\% overhead relative to the tabula rasa baseline under moderate distribution shift (Table~\ref{tab:performance_gap}), and warmup-only outperforms both when priors are well-calibrated. The hedging overhead is justified only under genuine prior uncertainty. When prior quality can be validated on held-out deployment data, direct strategy selection (tabula rasa for known-bad priors, warmup-only for validated-good priors) outperforms hedging.

\paragraph{Non-Stationarity Scope.}
Our corralling meta-learner incorporates loss decay to adapt expert weights under non-stationary conditions, but each expert bandit is itself trained under a stationary-reward assumption (monotone accumulation without forgetting). Thus, our treatment of non-stationarity operates at the meta level only: if a model's reward distribution drifts gradually within an expert's arm, the expert cannot adapt except via meta-level reweighting or replacement. Extending expert-level updates with explicit forgetting (e.g., sliding windows, discounted UCB) or change-point detection mechanisms is an interesting direction for future work.

\paragraph{Corralling Learning Rate.}
In principle, the corralling learning rate admits an optimal schedule $\eta^* \propto \sqrt{\ln K / T}$ for a given horizon, but our implementation uses a fixed $\eta$ (0.1 or 1.0) tuned for the typical horizon in our application. The staleness-aware loss decay mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) handles non-stationarity via forgetting of stale feedback; it partially masks horizon mis-specification but is not a substitute for an optimal corralling learning-rate schedule. Adaptive or horizon-aware $\eta_t$ schedules are left for future work.

\paragraph{Isotropic Prior in PCA Space.}
We apply PCA for dimensionality reduction and initialize the precision matrix as $\Amat_0 = \lambda \mathbf{I}$, which corresponds to an isotropic regularizer in the PCA space. This does not distinguish between high- and low-variance principal components and may over-regularize the latter. Designing anisotropic or variance-matched regularization in the PCA basis (e.g., diagonal $\Amat_0$ scaled by component variance, or full whitening prior to the bandit layer) is a natural extension.

\paragraph{Variance and Reproducibility.}
Multi-seed evaluation reveals substantial variance in expert weight evolution (final warmup weight: $0.382 \pm 0.471$, standard deviation exceeds mean). Outcomes are influenced by stochastic request ordering; practitioners should monitor deployment-specific dynamics rather than relying on population-level predictions.
