\subsubsection{The ``Over-Provisioning Penalty'' of Static Routing}

A critical finding is the non-monotonic behavior of the RouteLLM benchmark. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}, RouteLLM's performance collapses when the threshold is lowered to admit more GPT-4 traffic ($\tau < 0.10$).

\textbf{The Phenomenon}: Users paying \$0.013 (Maximum Cost) receive a reward of 0.812---worse than the \$0.0003 Mistral baseline (0.823). We term this the ``Over-Provisioning Penalty'': paying a $43\times$ cost premium to \emph{decrease} quality by 1.3\%.

\textbf{The Cause}: Static routers interpolate based on general ``hardness.'' They fail to distinguish the Alignment Tax Cluster (High PC1)---where GPT-4's verbosity degrades performance---from the Natural Language Cluster (Low PC1) where GPT-4 is genuinely superior.

\textbf{The Solution}: banditGPT (Blue Line) learns this inversion online. By using GPT-4 only when statistically justified, it achieves a peak reward of 0.909, enabling a +10.4\% quality gain while still reducing costs by 27\% compared to the naive GPT-4 baseline.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Readiness: Accelerating Adoption}
\label{sec:zero_shot}

A critical challenge in production routing is the ``Cold Start Dip'' associated with deploying new models. Traditional bandits ($\mu=0, \sigma=\infty$) require an exploration period that degrades user experience before discovering the new model's quality.

\paragraph{Experimental Setup.}
We simulated the release of \texttt{gpt-5.1} at $t=300$. To ensure methodological rigor, we filtered performance metrics using a \textbf{covariate-based complexity filter} (selecting prompts with logic/code keywords) rather than outcome-based filtering, avoiding circular reasoning.

\paragraph{The ``Dominance'' Challenge.}
Analysis of the holdout set revealed that \texttt{gpt-5.1} is trivially dominant on complex tasks (99.0\% win rate), rendering traditional task-correlation metrics undefined ($r \approx 0$). In such a ``flat'' reward landscape, the challenge is not learning \textit{nuanced} preferences, but achieving \textbf{Adoption Velocity}---how quickly can the system identify and switch to the new dominant model?

\paragraph{Results.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} compares three admission strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cold Start:} The router explores randomly. Reward converges slowly as the bandit accumulates evidence of \texttt{gpt-5.1}'s superiority.
    \item \textbf{Warmup Only:} The router has priors for old models but treats \texttt{gpt-5.1} as unknown. It suffers the same exploration penalty as Cold Start.
    \item \textbf{Latent Semantic Initialization (Ours):} By initializing \texttt{gpt-5.1} with the preference vector ($\thetavec$) of its semantic neighbor (\texttt{gpt-4-turbo}), we establish a \textbf{``Prior of Competence.''}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/figure6_ablation_final.png}
\caption{\textbf{Zero-Shot Readiness: Accelerating Adoption of Dominant Models.} When \texttt{gpt-5.1} is released at $t=300$, Cold Start (Red) and Warmup Only (Orange) require extensive exploration to discover the new model's superiority. In contrast, Latent Semantic Initialization (Green) achieves instant convergence by transferring a ``Prior of Competence'' from the semantic neighbor (\texttt{gpt-4-turbo}). Even in a flat reward landscape where task correlation is undefined ($r \approx 0$), the transferred magnitude enables immediate adoption, eliminating exploration lag. (N=30 trials, 95\% CI, Complex subset via covariate filter)}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}, our method bypasses the exploration phase entirely. Even though the transferred $\thetavec$ does not capture perfect task correlation (due to the target's dominance), it successfully transfers the \textit{magnitude} of belief---identifying the new model as a ``Flagship-tier'' candidate immediately. This results in near-instant convergence to the optimal policy, eliminating the adoption lag seen in baselines.

\subsubsection{Robustness: Prior Strength Calibration}

A potential concern with semantic transfer is brittleness to the \textit{effective prior strength} $\neff$, which governs confidence in the transferred preference vector. To validate robustness, we simulate releasing \texttt{gpt-4-turbo} at $t=300$ and sweep $\neff \in \{1, 2, 5, 10, 20\}$ on 1,000 real LMSYS battles.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/figure8_sensitivity_hybrid.png}
\caption{%
\textbf{Prior Strength Calibration.} 
Weak priors ($\neff=1.0$, green) achieve +17.6\% over Cold Start (red), outperforming stronger configurations due to preserved exploration flexibility. 
The narrow robustness band (shaded, $\neff \in [2,20]$) maintains $\geq$+12.4\%, confirming the method is not brittle. 
Strong priors ($\neff=20$, blue) underperform due to over-confident exploitation of cheaper incumbents, delaying adoption of the superior new model.
Gray: shared warmup phase.
}
\label{fig:sensitivity}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The Over-Confidence Trap.}
Surprisingly, \textbf{weak priors outperform strong priors}: $\neff=1.0$ achieves +17.6\% over baseline, while $\neff=20.0$ achieves only +12.4\%. The degradation occurs because high $\neff$ inflates $\mat{A}_{\text{new}} = \neff \cdot \mat{A}_{\text{neighbor}}$, shrinking LinUCB's exploration bonus. For expensive new models, cost penalties dominate, trapping the system in local optima despite accurate semantic signals.

\paragraph{Design Insight.}
Semantic transfer succeeds by transferring \textit{direction} ($\thetavec_{\text{neighbor}}$), not rigid confidence. Setting $\neff=1.0$ preserves exploration flexibility while capturing similarity, achieving optimal adoption velocity with no manual tuning required. The narrow robustness band (5.2pp worst-case degradation) confirms the system is production-ready across diverse model releases.
