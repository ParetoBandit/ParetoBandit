\subsection{Stationary Budget Pacing}
\label{sec:exp1_budget_pacing}

We first ask: \emph{can the router accept a dollar budget directly and automatically achieve it, without sacrificing routing quality compared to an offline-tuned static penalty?}

We evaluate the $K{=}3$ portfolio under stationary conditions.  Following the standard online-learning protocol, the policy continues to adapt during the test trajectory---metrics reflect cumulative performance over the stream, not a frozen snapshot.  The \textbf{Static baseline} uses a fixed cost-penalty $\lambda_s \in \{0, 0.05, \dots, 1.0\}$, producing a 7-point Pareto frontier. \textbf{ParetoBandit (BudgetPacer)} is evaluated at seven log-spaced targets, ranging from the cheapest model's empirical mean cost (\$0.000029/req) to the most expensive (\$0.0150/req).

\paragraph{Pareto frontier.} Both methods trace a quality--cost Pareto curve on the test set. At tight budgets (cost $< \$0.001$/req), the pacer achieves higher reward at lower cost: at a target of \$0.00008/req, the pacer reaches a mean reward of $0.813 \pm 0.0004$. The nearest static point ($\lambda_s{=}1.0$) achieves only $0.804 \pm 0.0003$ while spending $4.4\times$ more (\$0.00037/req). The pacer dominates because its adaptive $\lambda_t$ creates an implicit \emph{spend-when-valuable, save-when-cheap} policy: when easy prompts allow cheap routing, $\lambda_t$ decreases, freeing budget for harder prompts where expensive models provide greater quality uplift. The static penalty applies the same cost tax uniformly, forgoing this temporal budget allocation. In operational terms, this Pareto dominance means the pacer delivers $1.1\%$ higher reward than the best static penalty at $\bpStaticOneCostRatio\times$ lower cost---a gap that widens as budgets tighten, precisely where cost control matters most.

Notably, the two frontiers differ not only in quality at shared operating points but in \emph{coverage}: the static curve terminates at \$0.00037/req ($\lambda_s{=}1.0$, 93\% Llama), while the pacer extends to \$0.00003/req (99.9\% Llama)---a $12\times$ gap.  This is structural, not a sweep artefact: for any finite $\lambda_s$, the LinUCB exploration bonus on expensive models occasionally exceeds the fixed penalty, leaking traffic to costlier arms.  The adaptive $\lambda_t$ faces no such ceiling---it ratchets upward until the budget constraint binds, providing continuous Pareto coverage across the full cost spectrum without offline calibration.

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows that ParetoBandit equipped with the {\tt BudgetPacer} perfectly traces this optimal penalty Pareto frontier. At unconstrained budgets (cost $> \$0.005$), both methods asymptote toward a quality ceiling of ${\sim}0.922$---itself $95.7\%$ of the per-prompt oracle ($0.963$)---and the dual variable correctly decays to zero, so the pacer gracefully becomes a no-op.  The annotated oracle gaps frame the practitioner's tradeoff concretely: a ${\sim}4\times$ increase in oracle gap ($4.3\% \to 17.7\%$) buys a $300\times$ cost reduction; a more moderate $80\%$ cost saving (\$0.0095 $\to$ \$0.0018/req) widens the gap by only $1.4$\,pp.

\subsection{Budget Pacing under Cost Drift}
\label{sec:exp2_cost_drift}

We now evaluate the pacer's ability to track cost changes in real time: a three-phase cost drift while operating under strict budget constraints. The held-out test split ($n{=}1{,}824$) provides 1,216 unique prompts: 608 randomly-selected prompts for Phase~1 (normal pricing) and a disjoint set of 608 for Phase~2 (Gemini price drop to nearly zero). Phase~3 restores the original pricing and \emph{reuses the same 608 prompts as Phase~1}, making the P1-vs-P3 comparison a controlled within-subject design: any difference is attributable solely to the router's internal state (accumulated Phase~2 history), not prompt-sampling variability. With geometric forgetting $\gamma{=}0.995$, Phase~1 observations are down-weighted by $\gamma^{608}\approx 0.047$ by Phase~3, so the learner retains negligible memory of individual Phase~1 evaluations. The Fixed Policy (no online learning) serves as a null check---its P1 and P3 metrics are identical, confirming this control. We evaluate three budget regimes: Tight (\$0.0003/req), Moderate (\$0.00066/req), and Loose (\$0.0019/req).

\paragraph{Budget compliance is the key differentiator.}
The Fixed Policy (using an offline-tuned static penalty $\lambda_s$) is wildly inconsistent: it under-spends at tight and moderate budgets ($\bdFixedTightPhaseOneRatio$ and $\bdFixedModPhaseOneRatio$) but massively over-spends at the loose budget ($\bdFixedLoosePhaseOneRatio$). A single static penalty cannot simultaneously satisfy different budget regimes. The Naive Bandit is similarly erratic: it overshoots at tight ($\bdNaiveTightPhaseOneRatio$) and moderate ($\bdNaiveModPhaseOneRatio$), and over-spends by $\bdNaiveLoosePhaseOneRatio$ at the loose budget because the static penalty cannot adjust to the actual cost landscape.
To isolate the value of \emph{continuous} online tracking from merely reacting to a price change, we add a \textbf{Recalibrated Bandit}: identical to the Naive Bandit in Phase~1, but at each phase boundary its static penalty is re-tuned offline via a grid search on the validation split with the new phase's pricing. This is the strongest static-penalty baseline, granting oracle knowledge of both change points and two offline calibration passes. Even so, the Recalibrated Bandit inherits Phase~1 non-compliance and cannot track intra-phase spending dynamics (Table~\ref{tab:budget_compliance}).

ParetoBandit is the only system that reliably hits the budget in Phase~1 ($\bdParetoBanditLoosePhaseOneUtil$ to $\bdParetoBanditTightPhaseOneUtil$ across all three targets; Table~\ref{tab:main_results}b). The BudgetPacer's primal-dual mechanism adjusts $\lambda_t$ continuously based on observed costs, providing closed-loop control that no static penalty---even with periodic recalibration---can replicate.
From an SLA perspective, these deviations translate directly to financial risk: the Fixed Policy's $\bdFixedLoosePhaseOneRatio$ overshoot at the loose budget means spending more than~$160\%$ above the agreed-upon target---an overrun that grows linearly with traffic volume---while its $\bdFixedTightPhaseOneRatio$ utilisation at the tight budget means leaving roughly half the quality headroom on the table.

\paragraph{Exploiting the price drop (Phase~2).}
When Gemini becomes nearly free in Phase~2, the BudgetPacer automatically detects the cost reduction via its EMA signal.
The three budget regimes produce qualitatively distinct---but equally principled---dual-variable responses (Figure~\ref{fig:adaptation_dynamics}).
At all three targets, post-drop costs fall below the budget, so $\lambda_t$ decays and the constraint becomes slack.
The decay speed depends on the margin: at moderate and loose targets ($0.53\times$ and $0.19\times$ respectively), $\lambda_t$ drops to near-zero and the router reverts to quality-only optimization, increasing Gemini selection to ${\sim}46$--$51\%$.
At tight target, the margin is narrower ($0.95\times$), so $\lambda_t$ decays from $0.41$ to ${\sim}0.07$---slower, but in the same direction.
The tight condition still achieves the highest Gemini adoption ($72\%$) of any constrained condition, because the residual $\lambda_t$ provides mild cost pressure favoring the now-cheapest arm (Gemini, $\tilde{c}{\approx}0$) over Mistral ($\tilde{c}{\approx}0.49$).
All three regimes show highly significant reward lifts ($\Delta{=}+0.024$ to $+0.071$; $p < 10^{-3}$), with the tight budget exhibiting the largest gain because its Phase~1 constraint was most binding, creating the most room for improvement when costs collapse.

\paragraph{Recovering after price restoration (Phase~3).}
When Gemini's original pricing is restored at step~1217, the BudgetPacer reverses course: $\lambda_t$ rises back toward Phase~1 levels as the cost EMA exceeds the budget target, and Gemini adoption drops to near-zero.
At tight budget, $\lambda_t$ rises from $0.07$ to ${\sim}0.51$; at moderate from $0.01$ to ${\sim}0.40$; at loose from near-zero to ${\sim}0.10$ (Figure~\ref{fig:adaptation_dynamics}b).
Phase~3 $\lambda_t$ does not exactly match Phase~1 values (e.g., tight: $0.51$ vs.\ $0.41$)---this asymmetry reflects the ramp-up transient from near-zero through the slack region and is consistent with the finite 608-step phase length.
Budget compliance recovers to $\bdParetoBanditTightPhaseThreeRatio$, $\bdParetoBanditModPhaseThreeRatio$, and $\bdParetoBanditLoosePhaseThreeRatio$ across the three targets---within $2\%$ of the budget at all levels.
No other condition achieves this round-trip compliance: the Recalibrated Bandit obtains a second oracle recalibration at the Phase~3 boundary yet still under-spends ($\bdRecalTightPhaseThreeRatio$--$\bdRecalLoosePhaseThreeRatio$), while the Naive Bandit cannot recover at moderate and loose budgets ($\bdNaiveModPhaseThreeRatio$ and $\bdNaiveLoosePhaseThreeRatio$).
This Phase~3 recovery is the strongest evidence that the primal-dual mechanism provides genuine closed-loop budget control rather than merely reacting to favorable cost changes.

\paragraph{Quality cost of the pacer.}
An unconstrained ParetoBandit baseline (same $\gamma{=}0.995$, no pacer) isolates the pacer's quality impact.
In Phase~2, the unconstrained baseline reaches reward $0.928$; ParetoBandit at moderate/loose budgets reaches $0.927$---a gap of ${\leq}0.1\%$.
The pacer itself is nearly free in quality terms.
This quality difference is the cost of non-stationarity robustness---validated here by the reward lifts that the forgetting factor enables.

\paragraph{The cost--quality tradeoff under normal operation.}
Phase~1 (before any cost perturbation) reveals the practical value of the dual-variable mechanism under stable conditions.
In this phase, the unconstrained baseline spends \bdUncPhaseOneCostEng/req and achieves reward $\bdUncPhaseOneReward$.
The loose budget constraint (\$1.9${\times}10^{-3}$/req target) achieves reward $\bdParetoBanditLooseRewardPhaseOne$---only $\bdParetoBanditLooseRewardGapPct\%$ below unconstrained---while spending $\bdParetoBanditLooseCostRatioVsUnc{\times}$ less, a $\bdParetoBanditLooseCostSavingPct\%$ cost reduction.
The pacer accomplishes this by routing the majority of traffic through Mistral-Large ($76\%$) and Llama-8B ($15\%$), reserving Gemini-Pro ($9\%$) for prompts where its quality advantage justifies the cost.
Tighter budgets push the tradeoff further: at moderate, cost drops $\bdParetoBanditModCostSavingPct\%$ for a $\bdParetoBanditModRewardGapPct\%$ quality reduction; at tight, $\bdParetoBanditTightCostSavingPct\%$ savings for a $\bdParetoBanditTightRewardGapPct\%$ quality gap.
This gradient is the pacer operating as designed: even a generous constraint avoids the unconstrained router's tendency to over-route to expensive models when cheaper alternatives are nearly as good for the given context, while tighter constraints progressively restrict access to high-cost arms.
In production settings, these savings compound with traffic volume---a deployment routing one million requests per month would reduce costs from ${\sim}\$9{,}400$ (unconstrained) to ${\sim}\$1{,}800$ (loose) with negligible user-perceptible quality difference.

\paragraph{Tight budgets: full round-trip adaptation.}
The tight target ($B{=}\$0.0003$/req) is the most instructive case because it transitions from \emph{binding} to \emph{slack} and back to \emph{binding} across the three phases.
In Phase~1, $\lambda_t$ averages $0.41$, enforcing a Llama/Mistral mix; after the price drop, $\lambda_t$ decays to ${\sim}0.07$ as costs fall below target.
The tight condition achieves the highest Gemini adoption ($72\%$) of any constrained condition.
In Phase~3, when original pricing is restored, $\lambda_t$ rises to ${\sim}0.51$ and Gemini selection returns to near-zero, recovering budget compliance at $\bdParetoBanditTightPhaseThreeRatio$.
This full round-trip---binding $\to$ slack $\to$ binding---demonstrates that the primal-dual mechanism adapts in both directions without operator intervention.
The tight budget also exhibits the largest reward lift ($\Delta{=}+0.071$) because its Phase~1 constraint was most binding, creating the most room for improvement when costs collapse.

\paragraph{Ecological validity of the cost perturbation.}
The near-total price collapse used in Phase~2 is deliberately extreme: it stress-tests the pacer at the boundary of its operating range, where the dual variable must traverse from binding to fully slack and back.
Under milder cost perturbations, the same EMA-driven dual update produces proportionally smaller $\lambda_t$ adjustments with faster convergence, since the required policy shift is smaller---the mechanism's response is continuous and monotonic in the magnitude of the cost signal.
Importantly, discrete step-change pricing is the norm in production LLM deployments, not the exception: OpenAI reduced GPT-4~Turbo input prices by 67\% in a single announcement~\cite{openai2025turbopricing}, and Google cut Gemini~1.5~Flash input pricing by 78\% overnight~\cite{google2024flashpricing}.
The experimental design therefore mirrors the structure of real-world cost shocks---abrupt, provider-initiated, and often large---while testing at an extreme that bounds the pacer's worst-case adaptation latency.

\subsection{Resilience to Catastrophic Model Failure}
\label{sec:exp3_catastrophic_failure}

Having established the pacer's precision under cost drift, we now stress-test the system against the most severe non-stationarity a production router can face: a \emph{catastrophic model failure} where a previously top-performing model begins returning garbage responses.
Cloud API outages, 5xx errors, timeout cascades, and silent quality degradation after provider-side model updates are routine in production multi-LLM deployments.
When such a failure occurs, a static router continues sending traffic to the dead model---degrading user experience and wasting latency---until a human detects the problem, re-evaluates alternatives, and redeploys.
We ask: \emph{can ParetoBandit detect a catastrophic failure through its reward signal alone, redistribute traffic across the remaining $K{>}2$ models, and maintain budget compliance throughout?}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We construct a three-phase scenario on the $K{=}3$ portfolio using the held-out test split ($n{=}\cfPhaseN$ prompts per phase):

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Phase~1} (steps 1--\cfPhaseN): Normal operation.
    All models are healthy; the router has converged via online training on the validation split.
  \item \textbf{Phase~2} (steps \cfPhaseN--$2{\times}$\cfPhaseN): \textbf{Catastrophic failure}---Mistral-Large's reward drops to $\cfFailureReward$ (the API returns errors or garbage) and per-request cost drops to \$0 (the provider does not charge for failed requests).
    The router must detect the quality collapse through its reward signal and redistribute traffic to Llama-8B and Gemini-Pro.
  \item \textbf{Phase~3} (steps $2{\times}$\cfPhaseN--$3{\times}$\cfPhaseN): \textbf{Recovery}---Mistral-Large is restored to normal quality and pricing.
    Phase~3 reuses Phase~1 prompts so that any P1-vs-P3 difference is attributable solely to the router's internal state, not prompt-sampling variability.
\end{itemize}

The model registry is \emph{not} mutated at phase boundaries: the router's internal cost penalty continues to reflect Mistral's normal pricing.
Adaptation is driven purely by the observed reward signal, as it would be in production.

\paragraph{Why these baselines.}
Three algorithm variants are compared at each of three budget targets (Tight, Moderate, Loose), plus an unconstrained baseline (\cfNseeds~seeds):
(1)~\textbf{Fixed Policy}: frozen warmup priors, static cost penalty, no online learning---the counterfactual that keeps routing to the dead model;
(2)~\textbf{Naive Bandit ($\gamma{=}1.0$)}: online learning with infinite memory and a static cost penalty---the approach taken by existing bandit-based LLM routers~\cite{panda2025pilot,wang2025barp};
(3)~\textbf{ParetoBandit ($\gamma{=}0.995$)}: geometric forgetting with an active BudgetPacer---the proposed method.

\paragraph{Results (Figure~\ref{fig:catastrophic_failure}; Table~\ref{tab:failure_response}).}
The three conditions produce starkly different failure responses:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Fixed Policy} cannot learn; it continues routing \cfFixedModPhaseTwoReward-quality traffic through Mistral at 61\% allocation (moderate budget), suffering the most severe reward collapse.
    At the loose budget, where the static penalty favours Mistral most heavily, the Fixed Policy routes $70\%$ of traffic to the dead model, driving Phase~2 reward down to $\cfFixedLoosePhaseTwoReward$.
  \item \textbf{Naive Bandit ($\gamma{=}1.0$)} detects the failure and reduces Mistral traffic, but without a BudgetPacer its budget explodes: the static cost penalty that was calibrated for Phase~1 pricing cannot adjust to the changed cost landscape.
    At the moderate budget, the Naive Bandit overshoots by $\cfNaiveModPhaseTwoRatio$ in Phase~2 and $\cfNaiveModPhaseThreeRatio$ in Phase~3---a persistent budget violation that worsens over time as online learning shifts traffic toward expensive arms without cost feedback.
  \item \textbf{ParetoBandit ($\gamma{=}0.995$)} detects the failure, redistributes traffic (Mistral drops to $\cfParetoBanditModMistralPhaseTwo\%$ at moderate budget), \emph{and} maintains budget compliance: $\cfParetoBanditModPhaseOneRatio$, $\cfParetoBanditModPhaseTwoRatio$, and $\cfParetoBanditModPhaseThreeRatio$ across the three phases.
    The BudgetPacer's dual variable $\lambda_t$ adjusts automatically as the cost landscape changes, providing closed-loop cost control that the Naive Bandit's static penalty structurally cannot achieve.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Budget compliance is the key differentiator.}
The most striking result is not the reward comparison but the budget compliance column in Table~\ref{tab:failure_response}.
ParetoBandit maintains budget ratios within $0.97$--$1.03\times$ across \emph{all} nine phase--budget combinations.
The Naive Bandit, despite detecting the failure and recovering reward, violates the budget by up to $\cfNaiveTightPhaseThreeRatio$ (tight, Phase~3)---a persistent overrun that grows linearly with traffic volume.
This demonstrates that online learning alone is insufficient for budget-constrained non-stationary routing: the dual-variable mechanism is essential for maintaining cost invariance through disruptions.

\paragraph{Adaptation dynamics and the cost--quality tradeoff.}
Figure~\ref{fig:catastrophic_failure} reveals the mechanism behind budget compliance.
When Mistral fails, reward collapses within ${\sim}\cfParetoBanditModTroughOffset$~steps as the 50-step trailing window fills with near-zero rewards.
At moderate budget, the trough reaches $\cfParetoBanditModTroughReward$ (a $\cfParetoBanditModTroughDropPct\%$ drop), with deeper troughs at looser budgets ($\cfParetoBanditLooseTroughReward$, $-\cfParetoBanditLooseTroughDropPct\%$ for loose) because these conditions route more traffic to Mistral pre-failure.
ParetoBandit detects the collapse and adapts: reward stabilises within ${\sim}\cfParetoBanditModAdaptSteps$~steps (moderate) to ${\sim}\cfParetoBanditLooseAdaptSteps$~steps (loose), reaching Phase~2 steady-state levels of $\cfParetoBanditModP2SteadyReward$--$\cfParetoBanditLooseP2SteadyReward$ across budget regimes.

The unconstrained baseline reveals the cost of this stability.
Without a BudgetPacer, the unconstrained router freely shifts ${\sim}80\%$ of traffic to Gemini-Pro---the remaining high-quality model---and recovers reward to $\cfUncP2SteadyRewardShort$ during the failure (Figure~\ref{fig:catastrophic_failure}a).
However, this comes at a $+\cfUncCostSpikePct\%$ cost spike (\cfUncP1Cost/req $\to$ \cfUncP2SteadyCost/req; Figure~\ref{fig:catastrophic_failure}c).
Budget-constrained conditions cannot follow this strategy: their BudgetPacer penalises expensive Gemini-Pro traffic, forcing adaptation through cheaper Llama-8B and yielding a $\cfParetoBanditModP2RewardGapPct$--$\cfParetoBanditLooseP2RewardGapPct\%$ quality gap relative to Phase~1.
The tradeoff is principled: cost compliance costs ${\sim}10\%$ quality during a catastrophic failure, while the unconstrained alternative maintains quality at the price of a $45\%$ cost overrun---a budget violation that in production would breach SLAs and accumulate linearly with traffic volume.

\paragraph{Recovery dynamics.}
When Mistral is restored in Phase~3, the geometric forgetting factor ($\gamma{=}\cfGamma$) allows ParetoBandit to re-discover the recovered model via UCB exploration as the Phase~2 failure memory is discounted.
Phase~3 reward approaches but does not fully match Phase~1 levels within the 608-step phase length---consistent with the ${\sim}200$-step effective memory window requiring time to accumulate sufficient positive evidence for Mistral.
The BudgetPacer tracks this transition smoothly, with $\lambda_t$ adjusting to maintain compliance as the traffic mix evolves.

\subsection{Cold-Start Model Onboarding}
\label{sec:exp4_model_onboarding}

The experiments above assume a fixed $K{=}3$ portfolio.  In practice, providers release new models frequently---Gemini~2.5~Flash launched five months after Gemini~2.5~Pro, GPT-4o-mini arrived three months after GPT-4o---and platform teams need to integrate them without offline evaluation or policy retraining.
We evaluate whether a single \texttt{register\_model()} call suffices to onboard a new model into a \emph{budget-constrained} router.

\paragraph{Setup.}
After Phase~1 online learning on the $K{=}3$ portfolio (validation split, $n{=}1{,}785$), Gemini-2.5-Flash is added as a fourth arm via \texttt{register\_model()} with \emph{no warmup priors}---it receives only a neutral T-shirt prior ($A{=}\lambda I$, $b{=}0$), the coldest possible LinUCB initialisation.
Phase~2 continues online on the $K{=}4$ test split ($n{=}1{,}824$), where Flash's rewards are judged by DeepSeek-R1 using the same continuous rubric as all other arms.
Three budget targets are evaluated (tight, moderate, loose), plus an unconstrained ceiling (20~seeds).
A Fixed Policy (uniform $1/4$) serves as the budget-oblivious baseline.

\paragraph{Flash is discovered in every trial.}
Across all 80 ParetoBandit trials (20~seeds $\times$ 4~budget tiers), Flash is first selected within ${\sim}210$ post-onboarding steps and reaches sustained adoption (windowed share ${>}10$\% for 50 consecutive steps) by step~${\sim}233$.
LinUCB's exploration bonus---maximal for the uninformed arm ($\mathrm{tr}(A_a^{-1}) = d/\lambda = 26$ versus ${\sim}0.6$ for warm-started incumbents)---drives initial exploration, and the online learner quickly calibrates Flash's quality--cost profile from live traffic.

\paragraph{Budget determines adoption speed, not whether adoption occurs.}
At loose budgets, Flash reaches 38\% of traffic in the final 200 steps, exceeding the unconstrained ceiling (25\%).
At tight budgets, the pacer limits Flash to 14\%, just above the sustained-adoption threshold---the router correctly identifies that Flash (blended \$1.40/M) is cheaper than Mistral (\$1.00/M) but more expensive than Llama (\$0.10/M), and allocates accordingly.

\paragraph{Flash displaces the right arm.}
Displacement analysis reveals economically rational behaviour (Figure~\ref{fig:model_onboarding}b):
under budget pressure (tight and moderate), Flash takes share primarily from \textbf{Mistral-Large} ($-27.3$\,pp at tight, $-22.9$\,pp at moderate)---Flash offers Mistral-level quality at lower cost.
Unconstrained, Flash displaces \textbf{Gemini-Pro} ($-20.8$\,pp)---the bandit identifies Flash as a near-Pro-quality alternative at $10\times$ lower cost.

\paragraph{Budget compliance through portfolio expansion.}
The BudgetPacer maintains compliance throughout the $K{=}3 \to K{=}4$ transition without any reconfiguration (Figure~\ref{fig:model_onboarding}c): ParetoBandit meets each budget target to within 1\% across both phases.
The Fixed Policy (uniform~$1/4$), by contrast, exceeds all targets by 2--14$\times$ due to giving Gemini-Pro 25\% of traffic.

\paragraph{No historical labeled data required.}
A supervised router requires a \emph{pre-collected evaluation dataset} for every model in the portfolio: responses must be gathered on a representative prompt set, labeled by human annotators or an LLM judge, and the routing policy retrained on the expanded dataset---before the new model can receive any live traffic.  The bandit eliminates this offline data-collection campaign entirely.  It does require a reward signal, but that signal comes from the \emph{same} evaluation mechanism (judge, user ratings, or implicit behavioural composites; see Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) already serving the existing portfolio.  When exploration routes a prompt to the new model, the reward pipeline evaluates its response at zero marginal data-collection cost.  This distinction also avoids two risks inherent to offline evaluation: \emph{distribution mismatch}, where a curated eval set may not reflect the live prompt distribution, and \emph{dataset staleness}, where the eval ages as deployment patterns drift.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection via Epsilon-Constraint}
\label{sec:exp5_hparam_sweep}

Selecting hyperparameters ($\alpha$, $n_\mathit{eff}$, $\gamma$) for a non-stationary router poses a fundamental tension: standard cross-validation maximises stationary quality and selects $\gamma{=}1.0$ (no forgetting) for both variants, yet Section~\ref{sec:exp3_catastrophic_failure} showed that infinite memory fails to adapt under distribution shift.  The core idea of our resolution is simple: first, identify all hyperparameter settings that route well under normal (stationary) conditions; then, among those near-optimal settings, pick the one that adapts fastest when the environment changes---avoiding the trap of optimising purely for stationary performance, which would select settings that catastrophically fail under drift.  We formalise this with the \textbf{epsilon-constraint method}, a principled bi-objective selection procedure.

\paragraph{Two objectives.}
Each configuration in the grid ($\hpGridAlpha \times \hpGridNeff \times \hpGridGamma$ values of $\alpha$, $n_\mathit{eff}$, $\gamma$) is scored on two metrics using the validation split:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Budget-paced Pareto AUC} (primary): the area under the quality--cost Pareto frontier when sweeping budget targets with the BudgetPacer active. Higher is better.
  \item \emph{Phase-2 cumulative regret} (secondary): a two-phase simulation where, at the midpoint, two arms swap their reward \emph{and} cost distributions. Only post-swap regret is recorded, averaged over all $\binom{K}{2}$ swap pairs and 10 seeds. Lower is better.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Selection rule.}
Among all configurations whose budget-paced AUC is within $\epsilon{=}0.25\%$ of the best, we select the one with the lowest Phase-2 regret.  This gives budget-pacing explicit priority---no configuration is chosen that materially degrades stationary routing---while using adaptation speed as the tiebreaker within the near-optimal band.  The tolerance is calibrated to the observed AUC variation: the entire 144-configuration ParetoBandit grid spans only $0.39\%$ in budget-paced AUC, so $\epsilon{=}0.25\%$ covers roughly two-thirds of that range---tight enough to exclude materially worse configurations (32\% of the grid), yet permissive enough to admit a diverse set of $(\alpha, \gamma)$ combinations for the secondary objective to discriminate.

\paragraph{Result.}
Figure~\ref{fig:epsilon_tradeoff} visualises the tradeoff.  Configurations with $\gamma{=}1.0$ (blue) achieve the highest AUC but the worst post-swap regret: they cannot shed stale evidence.  Lower $\gamma$ values shift points downward (better adaptation) at marginal AUC cost.  The $\epsilon$-feasible band retains 98 of 144 ParetoBandit configurations (68\%), excluding primarily those with aggressive forgetting or very high exploration that trade too much stationary quality.  Within this feasible set, Phase-2 regret breaks ties.  The method selects $\gamma{=}\hpParetoBanditGamma$ (star) with $\alpha{=}\hpParetoBanditAlpha$, achieving Phase-2 regret of $\hpParetoBanditRegret$ while sacrificing only $\hpAUCSacrifice\%$ of stationary AUC ($\hpAUCOnlyAUC \to \hpParetoBanditAUC$).  The AUC-only winner ($\gamma{=}\hpAUCOnlyGamma$, red~\textbf{X}) selects $\alpha{=}\hpAUCOnlyAlpha$ with minimal exploration---exactly the configuration that fails under drift.  This ${\sim}200$-step effective memory window is long enough for stable exploitation, short enough to track realistic cost and quality shifts.

On the held-out test split the selected configurations achieve Pareto AUC of $\hpParetoBanditTestAUC$ (ParetoBandit) and $\hpTabulaTestAUC$ (Tabula Rasa) versus $\hpFixedTestAUC$ for the fixed-model envelope---deltas of $\hpParetoBanditTestDelta\%$ and $\hpTabulaTestDelta\%$, both within one standard deviation of seed variance ($\pm\hpParetoBanditTestStd$ and $\pm\hpTabulaTestStd$).  The val-selected hyperparameters thus transfer to test without statistically significant degradation, consistent with the $\epsilon$-constraint design which permits up to $0.25\%$ AUC sacrifice in exchange for adaptation capability.
