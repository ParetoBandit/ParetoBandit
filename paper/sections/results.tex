\subsection{Stationary Budget Pacing}
\label{sec:exp1_budget_pacing}

We first ask: \emph{can the router accept a dollar budget directly and automatically achieve it, without sacrificing routing quality compared to an offline-tuned static penalty?}

We evaluate the $K{=}3$ portfolio under stationary conditions. The \textbf{Static baseline} uses a fixed cost-penalty $\lambda_s \in \{0, 0.05, \dots, 1.0\}$, producing a 7-point Pareto frontier. \textbf{BanditGPT (BudgetPacer)} is evaluated at seven log-spaced targets, ranging from the cheapest model's empirical mean cost (\$0.000029/req) to the most expensive (\$0.0150/req).

\paragraph{Pareto frontier.} Both methods trace a quality--cost Pareto curve on the test set. At tight budgets (cost $< \$0.001$/req), the pacer achieves higher reward at lower cost: at a target of \$0.00008/req, the pacer reaches a mean reward of $0.813 \pm 0.0004$. The nearest static point ($\lambda_s{=}1.0$) achieves only $0.804 \pm 0.0003$ while spending $4.4\times$ more (\$0.00037/req). The pacer dominates because its adaptive $\lambda_t$ creates an implicit \emph{spend-when-valuable, save-when-cheap} policy: when easy prompts allow cheap routing, $\lambda_t$ decreases, freeing budget for harder prompts where expensive models provide greater quality uplift. The static penalty applies the same cost tax uniformly, forgoing this temporal budget allocation. Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows that BanditGPT equipped with the {\tt BudgetPacer} perfectly traces this optimal penalty Pareto frontier. At unconstrained budgets (cost $> \$0.005$), the pacer matches the static baseline's maximum quality ($0.923$); the dual variable correctly decays to zero, and the pacer gracefully becomes a no-op.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/pareto_frontier.pdf}
\caption{The Pareto frontier under stationary conditions. BanditGPT with BudgetPacer perfectly traces the theoretical optimal curve achieved by offline $\lambda$-tuning.}
\label{fig:pareto}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Budget compliance.} The pacer's primary advantage is its ability to hit a target. For the six tightest targets, actual spend ranges from $0.97\times$ to $1.02\times$ of the budget---effectively perfect compliance. Figure~\ref{fig:model_mix} illustrates the resulting steady-state model allocation under a strict budget, dynamically balancing between Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-Large-2512 to maximize quality. The loosest target ($B{=}\$0.015$) shows under-utilisation ($0.64\times$) because the unconstrained router's natural spend (\$0.0094/req) is already below this target; the pacer constrains spend \emph{from above} but never forces the router to spend more than its quality-optimal policy requires.

\subsection{Adapting to Model Quality Shifts}
\label{sec:exp2_reward_shift}

We next evaluate robustness to non-stationarity (Reward Drift). At step $t{=}893$ (Phase~2), Llama-8B and Mistral-Large exchange their reward and cost distributions, simulating a scenario where Mistral degrades to the worst option while Llama becomes the best. All conditions use a shared static cost penalty ($\lambda_s{=}0.20$) to isolate the effect of the learning mechanism.

\paragraph{Why these baselines.}
We compare four conditions forming an \emph{ablation ladder}---each
adds exactly one capability---plus an external baseline from the
non-stationary bandit literature:
(1)~\textbf{Fixed Policy}: frozen priors, no online learning---the
dominant production pattern and cost-of-not-adapting reference;
(2)~\textbf{Naive Bandit ($\gamma{=}1.0$)}: adds online learning
with infinite memory---the approach taken by existing bandit-based
LLM routers (PILOT~\cite{panda2025pilot},
BaRP~\cite{wang2025barp});
(3)~\textbf{SW-UCB ($W{=}200$)}: Sliding-Window
LinUCB~\cite{garivier2011switching} without warmup priors---a
purpose-built non-stationary algorithm with known minimax regret
bounds, included to ensure comparison against a method specifically
designed for changing environments;
(4)~\textbf{BanditGPT ($\gamma{=}0.995$)}: warmup priors +
geometric forgetting, combining all three ingredients.

The four conditions produce clearly separated cumulative regret
trajectories across 40~seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}a; Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative_regret}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Fixed Policy (Offline):} Because it cannot learn,
    it continues routing \rsFixedMistralPct\% to Mistral-Large even after the shift
    makes it the worst arm.  It accumulates $\rsFixedPhaseTwo$ Phase~2 regret,
    illustrating the cost of deploying a frozen router.
  \item \textbf{Naive Bandit ($\gamma{=}1.0$):} Online learning
    reduces total regret by \rsReductionFixedNaive\% ($\rsNaiveTotal$ vs.\ $\rsFixedTotal$), but
    infinite memory means Phase~1 inertia dilutes the new signal:
    Phase~2 regret is $\rsNaivePhaseTwo$---\rsNaivePhaseTwoExcess\% more than BanditGPT.
    Every existing bandit-based LLM router operates at this level.
  \item \textbf{SW-UCB ($W{=}200$):} Achieves the \emph{lowest
    Phase~2 regret} ($\rsSWUCBPhaseTwo$), demonstrating fast post-shift
    adaptation.  However, without warmup priors it starts from a
    blank slate ($A{=}\lambda I$, $b{=}0$): during the first
    ${\sim}200$ steps the UCB exploration bonus dominates, routing
    traffic near-uniformly and accumulating $\rsSWUCBPhaseOne$ Phase~1
    regret---\rsSWUCBPhaseOneFactor$\times$ higher than prior-initialised conditions.
    Its cross-seed variance ($\sigma{=}\rsSWUCBStd$) is \rsSWUCBVarFactor$\times$
    BanditGPT's, making individual deployments unpredictable.
  \item \textbf{BanditGPT ($\gamma{=}0.995$):} Geometric forgetting
    reduces Phase~2 regret by \rsBanditGPTPhaseTwoReduction\% vs.\ the Naive Bandit
    ($\rsBanditGPTPhaseTwo$ vs.\ $\rsNaivePhaseTwo$).  The ${\sim}200$-step effective memory
    window discounts stale Phase~1 evidence, allowing BanditGPT to
    re-learn the new optimum within ${\sim}200$ steps of the swap.
    Total regret ($\rsBanditGPTTotal$) is the lowest of any condition, and
    cross-seed variance ($\sigma{=}\rsBanditGPTStd$) is the lowest among all
    learning methods.
\end{itemize}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/cumulative_regret.pdf}
\caption{Cumulative regret under reward drift (4~conditions, 40~seeds, 95\% bootstrap CI).  BanditGPT ($\gamma{=}0.995$) achieves the lowest total regret by combining warmup priors (low Phase~1 cost) with geometric forgetting (fast Phase~2 adaptation). \paragraph{Deployment reliability.}
Beyond mean regret, variance reveals a critical distinction.
BanditGPT's $\sigma{=}\rsBanditGPTStd$ is comparable to the Naive Bandit
($\rsNaiveStd$) but \rsSWUCBVarFactor$\times$ lower than SW-UCB ($\rsSWUCBStd$).
SW-UCB's high variance stems from its cold start: which arms happen
to be sampled in the first ${\sim}200$ steps varies widely across
seeds, producing high-regret outliers.
BanditGPT's warmup priors stabilise early routing, and geometric
forgetting bounds the effective sample size at ${\sim}200$,
preventing any single observation ordering from dominating.
A production team gets one deployment---BanditGPT's low variance
means every deployment adapts reliably.

\subsection{Budget Pacing under Cost Drift}
\label{sec:exp3_cost_drift}

We now introduce the most complex and realistic scenario: a mid-stream price drop (Cost Drift) while operating under strict budget constraints. In Phase 2, Gemini-Pro's price drops to nearly zero. We evaluate three budget regimes: Tight (\$0.00023/req), Moderate (\$0.00066/req), and Loose (\$0.0019/req).

\paragraph{Budget compliance is the key differentiator.}
The Fixed Policy (using an offline-tuned static penalty $\lambda_s$) is wildly inconsistent: it under-spends at tight and moderate budgets ($\bdFixedTightPhaseOneRatio$ and $\bdFixedModPhaseOneRatio$) but massively over-spends at the loose budget ($\bdFixedLoosePhaseOneRatio$). A single static penalty cannot simultaneously satisfy different budget regimes. The Naive Bandit is similarly erratic: it under-spends at tight budgets ($\bdNaiveTightPhaseOneRatio$) but over-spends by $\bdNaiveLoosePhaseOneRatio$ at the loose budget because the static penalty cannot adjust to the actual cost landscape.
To isolate the value of \emph{continuous} online tracking from merely reacting to a price change, we add a \textbf{Recalibrated Bandit}: identical to the Naive Bandit in Phase~1, but at the Phase~2 boundary its static penalty is re-tuned offline via a grid search on the validation split with Phase~2 pricing. This is the strongest static-penalty baseline, granting oracle knowledge of the change point and an offline calibration pass. Even so, the Recalibrated Bandit inherits Phase~1 non-compliance and cannot adjust within Phase~2 as spending conditions evolve (Table~\ref{tab:budget_compliance}).

BanditGPT is the only system that reliably hits the budget in Phase~1 ($\bdBanditGPTLoosePhaseOneUtil$ to $\bdBanditGPTTightPhaseOneUtil$ across all three targets; Table~\ref{tab:main_results}b). The BudgetPacer's primal-dual mechanism adjusts $\lambda_t$ continuously based on observed costs, providing closed-loop control that no static penalty---even with periodic recalibration---can replicate.

\paragraph{Exploiting the price drop.}
When Gemini becomes nearly free in Phase~2, the BudgetPacer automatically detects the cost reduction via its EMA signal.
The three budget regimes produce qualitatively distinct---but equally principled---dual-variable responses (Figure~\ref{fig:adaptation_dynamics}).
At moderate and loose targets, post-drop costs fall well below the budget ($0.75\times$ and $0.58\times$ respectively), so $\lambda_t$ decays to near-zero: the constraint becomes slack and the router reverts to quality-only optimization, increasing Gemini selection to ${\sim}47$--$48\%$.
At the tight target, the opposite occurs: post-drop costs (\$2.37e-4/req) slightly exceed the budget (\$2.34e-4), so $\lambda_t$ \emph{rises} from $0.44$ to $1.53$.
Paradoxically, this elevated dual variable produces the \emph{highest} Gemini adoption ($87\%$) of any constrained condition, because the soft penalty $\lambda_t \times \tilde{c}_m$ penalizes the now-expensive Mistral ($\tilde{c}{=}0.38$) far more than the now-cheap Gemini ($\tilde{c}{\approx}0$).
All three regimes show highly significant reward lifts ($\Delta{=}+0.017$ to $+0.067$; $p < 10^{-9}$), with the tight budget exhibiting the largest gain precisely because its binding constraint concentrates traffic on the cost-efficient frontier.

\paragraph{Tight budgets: binding constraints as a feature.}
A particularly instructive result emerges at the tight target ($B{=}\$0.00023$/req).
Rather than being a limitation, the tight budget produces the \emph{strongest} adaptation:
BanditGPT achieves $87\%$ Gemini selection in Phase~2 (vs.\ $47$--$48\%$ for moderate/loose) and the largest reward lift.
This occurs because the dual variable $\lambda_t$ rises to $1.53$ when costs slightly exceed the tight target, creating a large soft-penalty gap between the now-cheap Gemini and the now-expensive Mistral.
Meanwhile, the Fixed Policy and Naive Bandit---using static cost penalties---consistently undershoot the tight budget ($0.68\times$ and $0.91\times$ respectively in Phase~2) because their open-loop penalties cannot exploit the new cost landscape.
The Recalibrated Bandit re-tunes $\lambda_s$ at Phase~2 but cannot adjust within Phase~2.
BanditGPT stays within $2\%$ of target, demonstrating that the primal-dual mechanism provides precise closed-loop budget control under non-stationary pricing.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection via Epsilon-Constraint}
\label{sec:exp4_hparam_sweep}

Selecting hyperparameters ($\alpha$, $n_\mathit{eff}$, $\gamma$) for a non-stationary router poses a fundamental tension: standard cross-validation maximises stationary quality and selects near-stationary $\gamma$ values ($\gamma{=}1.0$ for BanditGPT, $0.997$ for Tabula Rasa), yet Section~\ref{sec:exp2_reward_shift} showed that infinite or near-infinite memory fails to adapt under distribution drift.  We resolve this with the \textbf{epsilon-constraint method}, a principled bi-objective selection procedure.

\paragraph{Two objectives.}
Each configuration in the grid ($\hpGridAlpha \times \hpGridNeff \times \hpGridGamma$ values of $\alpha$, $n_\mathit{eff}$, $\gamma$) is scored on two metrics using the validation split:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Budget-paced Pareto AUC} (primary): the area under the quality--cost Pareto frontier when sweeping budget targets with the BudgetPacer active. Higher is better.
  \item \emph{Phase-2 cumulative regret} (secondary): a two-phase simulation where, at the midpoint, two arms swap their reward \emph{and} cost distributions. Only post-swap regret is recorded, averaged over all $\binom{K}{2}$ swap pairs and 10 seeds. Lower is better.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Selection rule.}
Among all configurations whose budget-paced AUC is within $\epsilon{=}0.25\%$ of the best, we select the one with the lowest Phase-2 regret.  This gives budget-pacing explicit priority---no configuration is chosen that materially degrades stationary routing---while using adaptation speed as the tiebreaker within the near-optimal band.  The tolerance is calibrated to the observed AUC variation: the entire 144-configuration BanditGPT grid spans only $0.39\%$ in budget-paced AUC, so $\epsilon{=}0.25\%$ covers roughly two-thirds of that range---tight enough to exclude materially worse configurations (32\% of the grid), yet permissive enough to admit a diverse set of $(\alpha, \gamma)$ combinations for the secondary objective to discriminate.

\paragraph{Result.}
Figure~\ref{fig:epsilon_tradeoff} visualises the tradeoff.  Configurations with $\gamma{=}1.0$ (blue) achieve the highest AUC but the worst post-swap regret: they cannot shed stale evidence.  Lower $\gamma$ values shift points downward (better adaptation) at marginal AUC cost.  The $\epsilon$-feasible band retains 98 of 144 BanditGPT configurations (68\%), excluding primarily those with aggressive forgetting or very high exploration that trade too much stationary quality.  Within this feasible set, Phase-2 regret breaks ties.  The method selects $\gamma{=}\hpBanditGPTGamma$ (star) with $\alpha{=}\hpBanditGPTAlpha$, achieving Phase-2 regret of $\hpBanditGPTRegret$ while sacrificing only $\hpAUCSacrifice\%$ of stationary AUC ($\hpAUCOnlyAUC \to \hpBanditGPTAUC$).  The AUC-only winner ($\gamma{=}\hpAUCOnlyGamma$, red~\textbf{X}) selects $\alpha{=}\hpAUCOnlyAlpha$ with minimal exploration---exactly the configuration that fails under drift.  This ${\sim}200$-step effective memory window is long enough for stable exploitation, short enough to track realistic cost and quality shifts.
