\section{Recovery Limit under Quality Degradation}
\label{appendix:recovery_limit}

Experiment~03 (\S\ref{sec:eval_catastrophic}) demonstrates that the system recovers from a silent quality regression (Mistral-Large reward dropping to $\cfFailureReward$) within a \rlPhaseN-prompt Phase~3 horizon.  Here we show that the system trends toward recovery at all tested severity levels---including near-total degradation---but that deeper corruption requires more prompts routed under normal conditions to fully converge, and our evaluation horizon is insufficient to observe complete recovery at the most extreme levels.  We characterise the \emph{finite-horizon recovery envelope}: for a given Phase~3 length, the degradation severity up to which the system reaches near-Phase~1 performance, and how extending the horizon shifts that boundary.

\vspace{-0.5ex}
\paragraph{Setup.}
We sweep Mistral-Large's degraded reward from $0.05$ to $0.85$ (Mistral's normal reward is ${\sim}\rlMistralNormalReward$).  \emph{Degradation severity}, the x-axis in Figure~\ref{fig:recovery_limit}a, measures the fractional gap between the degraded reward and the Phase~1 system baseline (${\sim}0.89$), ranging from ${\sim}4$\% (mild) to ${\sim}94$\% (near-total).  Degradation is modelled as a mean-shift: per-prompt rewards are shifted so the degraded arm's mean reward equals the target level while retaining prompt-dependent variation (clipped to $[0,1]$).  This is more realistic than constant-reward replacement because real regressions affect prompts heterogeneously.  Cost remains unchanged during Phase~2 so only the reward signal reveals the regression.  All runs use the moderate budget (\rlBudgetTarget/prompt), \rlNseeds~seeds, and the same hyperparameters as the main experiment.  We define \emph{full recovery} as a Phase~3/Phase~1 reward ratio ${\geq}97$\%.

For the extended-horizon evaluation, Phase~3 uses all non-Phase-2 holdout prompts ($2{\times}$ the base horizon) without recycling, ensuring the i.i.d.\ prompt arrival assumption is preserved and extended-horizon results reflect genuine recovery on fresh prompts.

\paragraph{Recovery mechanisms.}
Recovery operates through two complementary mechanisms.  \emph{Geometric forgetting} ($\gamma{<}1$) exponentially down-weights corrupted Phase-2 observations so that fresh Phase-3 data dominates the mean estimate; this is the primary driver of recovery.  \emph{Staleness-driven exploration} inflates the UCB variance for non-selected arms, potentially triggering re-exploration.  However, under budget-constrained routing the cost penalty can exceed the exploration bonus, limiting this mechanism's independent contribution.  Both mechanisms act jointly, but the forgetting half-life (${\sim}\rlHalfLife$ steps at $\gamma{=}\rlGamma$) sets the characteristic timescale.

\paragraph{Finite-horizon recovery envelope (Figure~\ref{fig:recovery_limit}a).}
Figure~\ref{fig:recovery_limit}a plots the P3/P1 reward ratio against degradation severity for two Phase-3 lengths.  At the \rlPhaseN-prompt horizon (matching Experiment~03, solid blue), full recovery (${\geq}97$\%) is achieved for degradations up to ${\sim}\rlStdRecoveryThresholdPct$\%, after which the ratio declines gradually to a floor of ${\sim}\rlStdFloorPct$\%.  Doubling Phase~3 to \rlExtPhaseThreeN{} unique prompts (dashed orange, no prompt recycling) uniformly lifts the entire curve: the 97\% boundary shifts to ${\sim}\rlExtRecoveryThresholdPct$\% degradation and the severe-degradation floor rises to ${\sim}\rlExtFloorPct$\%.

This uniform lift is consistent with the floor being a finite-horizon artefact---geometric forgetting exponentially erases Phase-2 corruption, so more Phase-3 prompts yield more recovery---rather than a fundamental limit on recoverable severity.  Within either fixed horizon, severity beyond ${\sim}50$\% has diminishing marginal impact because the forgetting dynamics are the same; only the initial bias differs.  At the other extreme, mild degradation (${\leq}10$\%) can produce P3/P1 ratios above $100$\%: Phase~2 drives the policy away from the degraded arm, and for some prompts the alternative routing discovered during degradation turns out to be genuinely better.

\paragraph{Convergence dynamics (Figure~\ref{fig:recovery_limit}b).}
The extended-horizon time-series (50-prompt rolling window) confirm the finite-horizon interpretation.  All trajectories rise monotonically toward the Phase~1 baseline with no sign of plateauing, but the convergence \emph{rate} is severity-dependent: milder degradations reach baseline well before the horizon limit, while more severe degradations are still climbing at \rlExtPhaseThreeN{} prompts.  In all cases the confidence bands narrow over time, indicating stable convergence rather than high-variance oscillation.  The monotonic, non-plateauing trajectories are consistent with recovery being rate-limited by Phase-3 length rather than fundamentally bounded by severity, though we cannot confirm full convergence at the most extreme levels within our evaluation budget.

\paragraph{Limitations and deployment implications.}
This study evaluates only the moderate budget level and single-arm, quality-only degradation; different budget levels or simultaneous multi-arm regressions may behave differently.
For typical silent regressions (${\leq}20$\%), ParetoBandit recovers automatically within the \rlPhaseN-prompt observation window.  For severe regressions, recovery still proceeds but requires a longer Phase-3 horizon; operators can either allow additional routing time or intervene with an explicit model demotion to accelerate convergence.
