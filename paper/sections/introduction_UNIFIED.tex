\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language model (LLM) inference increasingly draws from multi-provider portfolios~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt}: ten or more models spanning a $600\times$ cost range (Section~\ref{sec:multimodel}), from lightweight open-weights models to frontier APIs. Because the optimal model varies across tasks, domains, and individual prompts, \textbf{LLM routing}, which dynamically selects the best model per request, has emerged as a critical lever for managing cost and quality at scale~\cite{ong2024routellm, panda2025pilot}.

The dominant approach trains a static classifier on offline evaluation data~\cite{ong2024routellm, chen2024routerdc, feng2025graphrouter}, but frozen policies risk degradation in production due to three factors. First, \textbf{model preference is highly heterogeneous}: prompt embeddings predict per-prompt model preference (Spearman $\rho=-0.370$, $p<0.0001$, $N{=}750$). At $K{=}10$, optimal routing distributes traffic across all ten models with no single model exceeding 17\% of selections, rendering ``best-on-average'' static policies suboptimal. Second, \textbf{deployment distributions drift} from offline training data. We measure substantial feature-space divergence (PSI\,$=$\,0.763) between cross-distribution priors and deployment prompts. Third, \textbf{model portfolios are non-stationary}, with rapid frontier releases~\cite{thompson2026timeline} requiring routers to adapt without costly retraining.

While recent bandit-based routers~\cite{panda2025pilot, wang2025barp, li2025llmbandit} adapt online to deployment feedback, they lack explicit hedging against misspecified or misleading priors. Our ablation confirms a single-expert bandit leaves $+1.3$\,pp of statistically significant reward on the table compared to architectures that can safely escape bad priors.

To address these gaps, we present \textbf{banditGPT}, an open-source, cost-aware contextual bandit framework for dynamic LLM routing. \textit{banditGPT} makes three core contributions, each validated across portfolio sizes ($K{\in}\{2, 5, 10\}$) and prior sources:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Cost-aware LinUCB with source-agnostic offline-to-online transfer.} A modular pipeline converts arbitrary offline preference data, such as 80k cross-distribution battles ($K{=}2$) or 355 same-distribution evaluations ($K{\in}\{5, 10\}$), into LinUCB sufficient statistics. This eliminates the cold-start penalty, delivering 0.839--0.903 holdout quality from the first request. A continuous cost penalty traces a Pareto frontier, enabling the system to close \textbf{61--75\%} of the gap to a theoretical oracle within a few hundred online interactions (300--400 at $K{=}2$; 533 at $K{\in}\{5, 10\}$), yielding up to a 91\% cost reduction with only 6\% quality loss compared to frontier-only routing.
    
    \item \textbf{Corralling meta-learning for prior safety.} To safely hedge against distribution shift, \textit{banditGPT} employs a Corralling~\cite{agarwal2017corralling} meta-learner that dynamically balances an expert initialized with warmup priors against a \textit{tabula rasa} expert learning from scratch. A controlled factorial ablation confirms that online learning recovers $+7.0$\,pp from frozen-prior collapse, while the Corralling architecture provides a further $+1.3$\,pp steady-state advantage over a single-expert bandit ($p < 0.0001$, Cohen's $d{=}0.98$), achieving 7--13\% lower cumulative regret than warmup-only under good-to-moderate priors and providing detectable degradation signals under adversarial priors. Notably, the source of the offline prior has no significant effect ($p{>}0.18$).
    
    \item \textbf{Multi-model portfolio routing with family parameter sharing.} For larger portfolios ($K \geq 3$), \textit{banditGPT} supports hard per-request budget and latency constraints alongside Hybrid LinUCB, which pools observations across semantically related models via data-driven family assignments. At $K{=}5$, family sharing yields a $+3.6$\,pp holdout quality advantage over independent per-model learning ($p{=}0.028$) with $4.7\times$ narrower confidence intervals. Conversely, a leave-one-out ablation confirms one-shot semantic transfer provides no statistically significant benefit over continuous family sharing ($p{>}0.5$, $d{<}0.05$ at $K{=}10$), establishing the latter as a highly effective abstraction for cross-model knowledge transfer.
\end{enumerate}
