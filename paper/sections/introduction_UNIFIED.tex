\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deploying Large Language Models (LLMs) raises a practical question: \textbf{how should a system route user prompts across models with different costs and capabilities?} Always using an expensive model wastes budget, but purely static routing can be brittle when prompt distributions shift and new models are introduced.

% ============================================================================
% PART 1: THE PROBLEM (Two Core Challenges)
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Two Core Challenges}

\paragraph{Challenge 1: Expensive Models Aren't Always Better}
Many routing approaches implicitly assume a monotone relationship between ``difficulty'' and ``model strength.'' In our evaluation, this assumption does not always hold. We observe \textbf{model preference heterogeneity}: on held-out LMSYS prompts ($N=750$), model preference varies by prompt and the router's PCA features predict this variation (Spearman $\rho=-0.370$, $p<0.0001$, exceeding 100 random projections). A meaningful fraction of prompts favor the cheaper model over GPT-4-Turbo; qualitative inspection suggests these often involve stricter output constraints (see supplementary material for details). As a result, spending more can reduce average quality on this distribution: at its highest-cost operating point, RouteLLM pays about ${\sim}44\times$ more per request than the cheaper model alone for 1.3\% lower quality than that baseline.

\paragraph{Challenge 2: Training Data Doesn't Match Deployment}
In production, the distribution of user prompts inevitably differs from the offline training data---a fundamental concern in any deployed ML system~\cite{shimodaira2000improving}. When training and deployment distributions diverge, warmup priors can degrade or invert in quality. Static routers like RouteLLM~\cite{ong2024routellm} and FrugalGPT~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt} have no mechanism to detect or adapt to such shifts. Recent bandit-based routers~\cite{panda2025pilot, wang2025barp, li2025llmbandit} address this through online adaptation, but none provides formal safety guarantees against prior mismatch---a single misspecified bandit can silently degrade. Meanwhile, new models release frequently, requiring routers to incorporate them without costly retraining.

\textbf{Implication.} A practical router should (i) learn prompt-dependent preferences and (ii) adapt online while being robust to imperfect priors.

% PART 2: OUR SOLUTION
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Our Approach}

We present \textbf{banditGPT}, a cost-aware contextual bandit framework that addresses these challenges through:

\paragraph{1. Cost-Aware Online Learning from Warmup Priors}
We initialize LinUCB with offline priors from 80k RouteLLM battles, then adapt online from the router's own reward observations via bandit feedback. A Lagrangian penalty $\lambda$ balances quality and cost, tracing a cost--quality Pareto frontier. In our two-model benchmark, banditGPT exceeds RouteLLM's peak quality after $\sim$200 in-distribution prompts; at low budgets, RouteLLM's supervised discriminator remains competitive (Section~\ref{sec:pareto}).

\paragraph{2. Corralling Meta-Learning for Adaptive Complementarity}
We use Corralling~\cite{agarwal2017corralling} to combine two experts with complementary exploration schedules: a warmup expert informed by priors (constant $\alpha$) and a tabula rasa expert that decays exploration. The meta-learner shifts weight over time, which improves regret when priors are moderately reliable and provides a simple fallback when they appear misleading (Section~\ref{sec:prior_degradation}).

\paragraph{3. Multi-Model Routing ($K \geq 3$)}
We support multi-model routing ($K\geq 3$) via hard budget constraint filtering (max cost, latency, and quality floors) and cost-penalized LinUCB to discover useful choices across cost tiers.

\subsection{Contributions}

Our work makes the following contributions, validated through comprehensive experimentation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Model Preference Heterogeneity Analysis.}
    We show that prompt embeddings contain signal for routing decisions: PCA features correlate with model preference on held-out prompts ($\rho=-0.370$, $p<0.0001$, $N=750$), exceeding 100 random projections.
    
    \item \textbf{Cost-Aware Online Learning with Safety.}
    A cost-aware contextual bandit initialized from offline priors improves with online feedback and reaches 70.0\% gap closure to oracle on frozen holdout (vs.\ 46.2\% for RouteLLM). We find a consistent pattern across cost regimes: RouteLLM is strong at low budgets, while online adaptation improves quality at moderate-to-high budgets (Section~\ref{sec:pareto}). We further evaluate Corralling as a hedge against prior mismatch (Section~\ref{sec:prior_degradation}).
    
    \item \textbf{Open-Source Multi-Model Framework.}
    We release a modular implementation that supports multi-model portfolios, budget constraint filtering, and prior calibration. In cold-start ablations, we find limited benefit from per-model semantic transfer (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_null}), clarifying an open challenge for routing with limited traffic to new models.
\end{enumerate}
