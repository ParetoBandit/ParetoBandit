\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language model (LLM) inference now accounts for a significant and growing share of cloud compute budgets. Production deployments increasingly draw from multi-provider portfolios: ten or more models spanning a $600\times$ cost range, from lightweight open-weights models at fractions of a cent to frontier APIs costing orders of magnitude more per request. The best model for a given prompt depends on the task, the domain, and a rapidly shifting model landscape; these preferences vary not only across cost tiers but across individual prompts within the same tier. \textbf{LLM routing}, the problem of dynamically selecting which model from an available portfolio to serve each request, has emerged as a critical lever for managing this cost and quality trade-off at scale~\cite{ong2024routellm, chen2023frugalgpt, panda2025pilot}.

The dominant approach trains a static classifier on offline preference data and applies it at inference time~\cite{ong2024routellm, chen2024routerdc, feng2025graphrouter}. This works well when training data perfectly matches deployment traffic, but production environments regularly violate this assumption in three ways. First, \textbf{model preference is heterogeneous across the portfolio}: in our evaluation on 750 held-out LMSYS Arena prompts, prompt embeddings predict per-prompt model preference with Spearman $\rho=-0.370$ ($p<0.0001$), exceeding all 100 random-projection baselines. At $K{=}2$, 14.1\% of prompts favor the cheaper model over the frontier model; at $K{=}10$, the router distributes traffic across all ten models based on prompt context, with no single model exceeding 17\% of selections (Section~\ref{sec:multimodel}). Consequently, a na\"ive ``always route to the best-on-average model'' policy backfires at every portfolio size, producing lower quality at higher cost. Second, \textbf{deployment distributions drift} from offline training data, silently degrading static routing policies. This is a fundamental concern in deployed ML systems~\cite{shimodaira2000improving}. In a controlled $K{=}2$ factorial ablation (Appendix~\ref{app:distribution_shift}), we measure substantial feature-space divergence (PSI\,$=$\,0.763) between cross-distribution offline priors and deployment prompts, yet find that any online bandit recovers $+7.0$\,pp from frozen-prior collapse regardless of the prior source ($p{=}0.18$ for the prior-source effect); layering a Corralling meta-learner yields a further $+1.3$\,pp ($p < 0.0001$, Cohen's $d{=}0.98$). Third, \textbf{model portfolios are non-stationary}: the pace of frontier model releases has accelerated sharply, from approximately 12 major releases in 2023 to over 21 in 2025 alone~\cite{thompson2026timeline}. Existing models are regularly deprecated or superseded, requiring the router to adapt without costly retraining.

Recent bandit-based routers~\cite{panda2025pilot, wang2025barp, li2025llmbandit} address distribution shift through online adaptation. However, to our knowledge, none explicitly hedges against misspecified priors. Our ablation confirms that this matters: a single-expert bandit leaves $+1.3$\,pp of statistically significant reward on the table compared to a two-expert meta-learner that can escape bad priors.

We present \textbf{banditGPT}, an open-source cost-aware contextual bandit framework for LLM routing that closes this gap through three mechanisms:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Cost-aware LinUCB with source-agnostic offline-to-online transfer.}
    A modular prior pipeline converts any collection of (prompt, model, reward) triples into LinUCB sufficient statistics, which are then refined via label-free bandit feedback at deployment.
    We validate this with two prior sources: 80k cross-distribution preference battles ($K{=}2$) and 355 same-distribution evaluation prompts covering all portfolio models ($K{=}5, 10$; Section~\ref{sec:warmup_prior_construction}).
    Both eliminate cold-start, delivering holdout quality of 0.839--0.903 from the first request depending on prior calibration.
    A cost penalty $\lambda$ traces a continuous Pareto frontier; across portfolio sizes, banditGPT closes \textbf{69--75\%} of the gap to an oracle router after 300 to 500 online interactions, without requiring any supervised labels during the online phase.

    \item \textbf{Corralling meta-learning for prior safety.}
    A Corralling~\cite{agarwal2017corralling} meta-learner maintains two expert bandits, one initialized from warmup priors and one starting from scratch, and dynamically shifts weight toward whichever performs better.
    Under good-to-moderate priors, Corralling achieves 30 to 40\% lower cumulative regret than either expert alone; under adversarial priors, it falls back gracefully to the tabula rasa expert (Section~\ref{sec:prior_degradation}).
    A paired factorial analysis confirms that this architecture provides a statistically significant $+1.3$\,pp steady-state advantage over a single-expert bandit ($p < 0.0001$, Cohen's $d{=}0.98$; Appendix~\ref{app:distribution_shift}).

    \item \textbf{Multi-model portfolio routing with family parameter sharing ($K \geq 3$).}
    banditGPT supports portfolios of arbitrary size; we evaluate at $K{=}5$ and $K{=}10$ models spanning six providers and a $600\times$ cost range.
    Hard budget constraints (cost ceiling and quality floors) filter ineligible models per request, while cost-penalized LinUCB discovers useful choices across cost tiers.
    Hybrid LinUCB further pools observations across semantically related models via data-driven family assignments (within-provider tetrachoric correlation), improving both quality and convergence reliability.
    At $K{=}5$, family sharing yields a statistically significant $+3.6$~pp holdout quality advantage over independent per-model learning ($p{=}0.028$) with $4.6\times$ narrower confidence intervals; this benefit diminishes at $K{=}10$ and $K{=}20$ as per-model data becomes relatively more abundant.
    The system achieves 0.954 peak reward at $K{=}5$ and up to 89\% cost reduction with less than 6\% quality loss at $K{=}10$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
Our work makes three contributions, each validated across portfolio sizes ($K{=}2, 5, 10$) and prior sources with comprehensive multi-seed experiments on held-out data:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Empirical analysis of model preference heterogeneity.}
    We demonstrate that prompt embeddings predict per-prompt model preference ($\rho{=}{-}0.370$, $p{<}0.0001$, $N{=}750$) and that this heterogeneity causes non-monotonic cost and quality behavior in static routers.
    At $K{=}10$, contextual routing distributes traffic across all ten models based on prompt features, confirming that preference heterogeneity is a portfolio-wide phenomenon.

    \item \textbf{A cost-aware online routing framework with modular priors and meta-learning safety.}
    banditGPT accepts warmup priors from any offline source and refines them online.
    We validate two regimes: cross-distribution priors ($K{=}2$, 80k battles; 70.0\% oracle gap closure) and same-distribution priors ($K{=}5, 10$, 355 prompts across all portfolio models; 69--75\% gap closure).
    A factorial ablation confirms that online adaptation ($+7.0$\,pp) and Corralling ($+1.3$\,pp, $p < 0.0001$) drive performance, while prior source has no significant effect ($p{=}0.18$; Appendix~\ref{app:distribution_shift}).
    We stress-test Corralling under prior corruption and catastrophic model failure, reporting both strengths and limitations.

    \item \textbf{Open-source multi-model implementation and honest evaluation.}
    We release a modular codebase supporting portfolios of two or more models, dynamic model onboarding, and configurable constraints.
    Hybrid LinUCB's family parameter sharing provides $4.6\times$ narrower confidence intervals and a $+3.6$~pp quality improvement at $K{=}5$ ($p{=}0.028$), demonstrating that data-driven model grouping is a practical lever for routing reliability, particularly under data scarcity.
    A leave-one-out ablation (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_null}) confirms that one-shot per-model semantic transfer provides no statistically significant benefit beyond hybrid family sharing ($p{>}0.5$, Cohen's $d{<}0.05$ at $K{=}10$), suggesting that the continuous family $\beta_F$ mechanism is an effective abstraction level for cross-model knowledge transfer in our evaluation setting.
\end{enumerate}
