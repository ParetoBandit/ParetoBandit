\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) presents a fundamental challenge: \textbf{how do we route user queries across models with vastly different costs and capabilities?} Naively routing all traffic to expensive "frontier" models (e.g., GPT-4) wastes resources, while static routing policies fail to adapt to distribution shifts and new model releases.

% ============================================================================
% PART 1: THE PROBLEM (Two Core Challenges)
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Two Core Challenges}

\paragraph{Challenge 1: Expensive Models Aren't Always Better}
The assumption underlying most routing systems---that ``harder'' tasks require ``stronger'' models---is empirically wrong. We observe \textbf{model preference heterogeneity}: on held-out LMSYS prompts ($N=750$), model preference varies by prompt and the router's PCA features predict this variation (Spearman $\rho = -0.370$, $p < 0.0001$, exceeding all 100 random projections). About 14\% of prompts explicitly favor the cheaper model (Mixtral, \$0.50/1M tokens) over GPT-4-Turbo (\$10/1M tokens), correlating with strict formatting constraints where the frontier model's conversational tendencies become a liability (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}). The practical consequence is severe: when the state-of-the-art static router (RouteLLM) is given maximum budget, it pays $43\times$ more for $1.3\%$ \emph{lower} quality---because it cannot distinguish prompts where spending more money helps from those where it hurts.

\paragraph{Challenge 2: Training Data Doesn't Match Deployment}
In production, the distribution of user prompts inevitably differs from the offline training data---a fundamental concern in any deployed ML system~\cite{shimodaira2000improving}. When training and deployment distributions diverge, warmup priors can degrade or invert in quality. Static routers like RouteLLM~\cite{ong2024routellm} and FrugalGPT~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt} have no mechanism to detect or adapt to such shifts. Recent bandit-based routers~\cite{panda2025pilot, wang2025barp, li2025llmbandit} address this through online adaptation, but none provides formal safety guarantees against prior mismatch---a single misspecified bandit can silently degrade. Meanwhile, new models release frequently, requiring routers to incorporate them without costly retraining.

\textbf{The Key Insight:} These challenges are \emph{interconnected}. Learning semantic structure (Challenge 1) doesn't help if priors fail under distribution shift (Challenge 2), and standard bandit approaches lack mechanisms to detect and recover from prior mismatch.

\subsection{Our Approach}

We present \textbf{banditGPT}, a cost-aware contextual bandit framework that addresses both challenges through:

\paragraph{1. Cost-Aware Online Learning from Warmup Priors}
We initialize LinUCB bandits with offline priors from 80k RouteLLM battle outcomes, then adapt online using only the router's own reward observations---no labels required. The cost-aware UCB objective balances quality and cost through a user-defined penalty $\lambda$, enabling continuous Pareto optimization across the full cost-quality spectrum. After approximately 200 in-distribution prompts, banditGPT surpasses RouteLLM's peak quality (trained on 100k supervised pairs), demonstrating that a small amount of in-distribution experience outweighs a large volume of out-of-distribution supervision. At low budgets, RouteLLM's pre-trained prompt-level discrimination remains competitive, reflecting a natural trade-off between supervision scale and deployment adaptation (Section~\ref{sec:pareto}).

\paragraph{2. Corralling Meta-Learning for Adaptive Complementarity}
We employ Corralling~\cite{agarwal2017corralling}, a meta-algorithm that adaptively combines two experts with complementary exploration schedules: a warmup expert (constant $\alpha{=}2.0$, informed by priors) and a tabula rasa expert (decaying $\alpha$, converging to exploitation). Through exponential weight updates, the meta-learner dynamically shifts weight between them, capturing the benefits of both fast prior-informed initialization \emph{and} eventual exploitation convergence. When priors are useful ($\alpha \leq 0.5$), Corralling achieves 29--39\% lower regret than either expert alone; when priors degrade, the meta-learner's runtime signals enable detection and fallback to tabula rasa.

\paragraph{3. Multi-Model Routing ($K \geq 3$)}
While several recent routers support multi-model selection~\cite{feng2025graphrouter, chen2024routerdc, panda2025pilot}, banditGPT is the first to combine multi-model support with online adaptation and meta-learning safety. Dynamic Pareto Filtering prunes dominated models per-request, and the exploration bonus guides discovery across heterogeneous cost-quality tiers.

\subsection{Contributions}

Our work makes the following contributions, validated through comprehensive experimentation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Model Preference Heterogeneity Analysis.}
    We demonstrate empirically that prompt structure predicts model preference: the router's PCA features show significant rank correlation with model preference ($\rho = -0.370$, $p < 0.0001$, $N = 750$), exceeding all 100 random projections. This establishes that routing is learnable through contextual features.
    
    \item \textbf{Cost-Aware Online Learning with Safety.}
    A contextual bandit router combining offline warmup priors with online adaptation achieves $70.0\%$ gap closure to oracle on frozen holdout (vs.\ $46.2\%$ for RouteLLM) after ${\sim}200$ label-free prompts. At moderate-to-high budgets, banditGPT Pareto-dominates RouteLLM; at low budgets, RouteLLM's pre-trained discrimination is competitive, providing an honest delineation of when online adaptation adds value. Corralling meta-learning with complementary alpha schedules achieves 29--39\% lower regret than either expert alone when priors are useful, with catastrophic failure response as a secondary benefit (banditGPT matches EMA at $K{=}10$).
    
    \item \textbf{Open-Source Multi-Model Framework.}
    A modular, open-source system supporting arbitrary portfolio sizes with continuous cost-quality Pareto optimization, Dynamic Pareto Filtering, and prior calibration. We additionally report a rigorously validated \emph{null result} on semantic transfer for cold-starting new models (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_null}), establishing that this remains an open problem for bandit routers.
\end{enumerate}
