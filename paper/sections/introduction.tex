\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language model (LLM) inference increasingly draws from multi-provider portfolios~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt}, spanning lightweight open-weights models to frontier APIs. Because the optimal model varies across tasks, domains, and individual prompts, \textbf{LLM routing}---dynamically selecting the best model per request---has emerged as a critical lever for managing cost and quality at scale~\cite{ong2024routellm, chen2024routerdc, feng2025graphrouter, panda2025pilot}. In production, an engineering team may route millions of requests daily across models spanning a $500\times$ price range (e.g., \$0.00003/req for Llama-3.1-8B vs.\ \$0.015/req for Gemini-2.5-Pro) and must keep average spend within a strict API budget.

Crucially, real enterprise portfolios are not binary ``strong-vs.-weak'' pairs.  Organizations typically maintain three or more model tiers---budget, mid-range, and frontier---and often deploy multiple models per tier for redundancy, latency, or specialization (e.g., a code-focused model alongside a general-purpose model at the same price point).  The number of viable options continues to grow: major providers now offer 5--15 distinct model endpoints each, and multi-provider deployments routinely span $K{=}5$--$10$ or more candidates.  The value of a larger portfolio is not that more models necessarily produce a better quality frontier---a single frontier API may dominate on raw quality---but rather that more models at distinct price points give the router \emph{more degrees of freedom to find quality within a budget}.  When budgets are tight, a $K{=}2$ router can only choose ``cheap'' or ``expensive''; with $K{\geq}3$ it can discover that a mid-tier model matches frontier quality on easy prompts at a fraction of the cost, stretching the budget to afford frontier calls where they matter most.  The same logic applies to non-stationarity: when a model degrades, a richer portfolio increases the chance that an alternative maintains quality at a comparable cost; when a provider cuts prices, more models mean more opportunities for the router to reallocate freed budget toward quality improvements.

Many existing routers operate as static policies trained offline on evaluation data, employing diverse architectures from matrix factorization~\cite{ong2024routellm} and graph neural networks~\cite{feng2025graphrouter} to contrastive representation learning~\cite{chen2024routerdc} and cascading heuristics~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt}. While highly effective in stationary environments, frozen policies risk degradation when deployed in real-world scenarios due to three critical factors. First, \textbf{deployment distributions drift}: prompt mixes vary by use case and drift over time from offline training data~\cite{ailem2024benchmarkrobustness}. Second, \textbf{model quality is non-stationary}: providers silently update APIs in ways that can subtly shift downstream performance on specific tasks~\cite{chen2023chatgptdrift, ma2024prompt}, meaning a router trained on historical data inherits a \emph{stale policy}. Third, \textbf{model costs drop frequently}: rapid frontier releases often trigger price cuts~\cite{thompson2026timeline}. If a static router maintains a fixed routing distribution during a price drop, it merely pockets the savings, leaving potential quality gains---such as upgrading a larger percentage of traffic to the now-cheaper frontier model---unexploited.

To address the need for online adaptation, recent work has formulated routing as a contextual bandit or reinforcement learning problem~\cite{panda2025pilot, wang2025barp, li2025llmbandit, qian2025xrouter}. These approaches learn dynamically from feedback (e.g., user ratings or automated judge scores) to adjust their policies over time, utilizing preference-conditioned bandits~\cite{wang2025barp, li2025llmbandit} or acting as complex orchestration agents~\cite{qian2025xrouter}. However, three critical challenges remain for deploying these adaptive systems in budget-constrained enterprise environments:
1. \textbf{Budget Enforcement}: Existing cost-aware systems typically either scalarize cost into the reward via a static penalty weight $\lambda$~\cite{wang2025barp, ong2024routellm} or rely on finite-horizon knapsack algorithms~\cite{panda2025pilot}. Static weights require manual offline tuning to translate an abstract penalty into a dollar outcome whenever pricing changes, while knapsack approaches require knowing the total number of queries in advance, making them unsuitable for continuous, open-ended production streams.
2. \textbf{Catastrophic Memory vs. Agility}: Standard algorithms like LinUCB often use infinite memory (forgetting factor $\gamma=1.0$). While optimal for stationary settings, this causes the bandit to react far too slowly when a previously high-performing model suddenly degrades in quality, accumulating massive regret during the transition.
3. \textbf{Contending Contribution Requirements}: Tuning an adaptive system to simultaneously maintain stable stationary performance (which favors infinite memory and low exploration) and agile non-stationary adaptation (which favors aggressive forgetting and high exploration) creates conflicting optimization objectives. Standard cross-validation optimizes solely for stationary metrics, leading to brittle real-world deployments.

Left unaddressed, these gaps carry direct financial consequences: a static penalty that drifts from its budget target either wastes quality headroom (under-spend) or triggers cost overruns (over-spend), and a router that cannot detect a price drop leaves freed budget idle rather than reinvesting it in application quality.

We address these practical deployment challenges with \textbf{ParetoBandit}, an open-source contextual bandit framework explicitly designed for budget-constrained, non-stationary LLM routing. We make four key contributions, evaluated on a dataset of 1,785 prompts scored by a continuous three-factor rubric (cross-judge robustness validated in Appendix~\ref{sec:judge_robustness}) across a canonical three-tier model portfolio:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Direct Budget Pacing via Primal-Dual Lagrangian Relaxation:} We implement an online Lagrangian budget pacer (\texttt{BudgetPacer}) that accepts a direct dollar-denominated rate target (e.g., ``\$0.005 per request'') and enforces it over an open-ended request stream with no known horizon.  Unlike finite-horizon knapsack approaches~\cite{panda2025pilot} that require knowing the total number of requests in advance, our rate-constraint formulation---adapted from the Primal-Dual mechanism of Agrawal and Devanur~\cite{agrawal2014bandits}---self-regulates regardless of traffic volume.  By applying exponential moving average (EMA) smoothing to handle the extreme variance in LLM pricing, the system dynamically adjusts penalties to achieve exact budget compliance while automatically tracing the Pareto frontier, eliminating offline $\lambda$ tuning.
    \item \textbf{Robust Adaptation via Geometric Forgetting:} To combat silent model updates (reward drift), we integrate a geometric forgetting mechanism ($\gamma=0.995$) coupled with offline-to-online warmup priors. This enables the router to adapt rapidly to quality shifts without suffering catastrophic forgetting of the initial prompt-preference mapping, drastically reducing cumulative regret under drift compared to naive bandits ($\gamma=1.0$) and frozen policies.
    \item \textbf{Exploiting Cost Drift under Budget Constraints:} We demonstrate that when a model provider drops its price mid-cycle, the \texttt{BudgetPacer} automatically detects and exploits the opportunity. Unlike static baselines that fail to adapt, the primal-dual mechanism produces qualitatively distinct responses across budget regimes: when the constraint becomes slack, $\lambda_t$ decays to zero and the router reverts to quality-only optimization; when the constraint remains binding, $\lambda_t$ rises and concentrates traffic on the cost-efficient frontier. In both cases, ParetoBandit maintains precise budget compliance ($0.98$--$1.02\times$ target) while delivering significant reward lifts ($+0.017$ to $+0.065$).
    \item \textbf{Rigorous Hyperparameter Optimization for Shift:} We adapt joint hyperparameter tuning (exploration $\alpha$, prior strength $n_{eff}$, forgetting factor $\gamma$) to the LLM routing domain. While utilized in other fields, this approach directly resolves the uniquely contending requirements of our system, structurally balancing stable stationary exploitation with rapid agility under distribution shifts where standard cross-validation fails.
\end{enumerate}

Beyond these four contributions, we demonstrate that ParetoBandit's exploration mechanism enables \emph{cold-start model onboarding}: a single \texttt{register\_model()} call integrates a previously unseen model---with no offline evaluation or warmup priors---into a budget-constrained router, and the bandit discovers its quality--cost niche from live traffic within hundreds of requests (Section~\ref{sec:model_onboarding_preview}; Appendix~\ref{appendix:model_onboarding}).

By releasing the framework open-source, we aim to provide an immediate pathway for practitioners managing enterprise LLM gateways to replace manual recalibration with autonomous Primal-Dual pacing, and to offer the research community a reproducible testbed for non-stationary routing.
