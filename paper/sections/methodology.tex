\section{Methodology: The ParetoBandit System}
\label{sec:methodology}

We present the architecture of ParetoBandit, an open-source contextual bandit routing framework.  At its core, LLM routing is an \emph{exploration--exploitation problem}: the router must balance selecting models it already knows to be good (exploitation) against trying uncertain or newly added models that might be better (exploration).  Maximising long-term expected reward requires getting this tradeoff right---and in production the tradeoff is complicated by two additional forces: \emph{budget constraints} that limit how much exploration the router can afford, and \emph{non-stationarity} that forces continuous re-exploration as model quality and pricing change.

ParetoBandit exposes a small set of interpretable hyperparameters, each governing a distinct facet of this tradeoff:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item $\alpha$ (exploration rate): how aggressively the router tries uncertain models versus exploiting known-good ones (Section~\ref{sec:budget_pacing}).
  \item $n_\mathit{eff}$ (prior strength): how strongly the router trusts its offline priors versus new online evidence---controlling how quickly exploration begins (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).
  \item $\gamma$ (forgetting factor): how quickly the router discounts stale evidence, determining when it \emph{re-explores} arms whose quality may have changed (Section~\ref{sec:forgetting}).
  \item $B$ (budget target): a dollar constraint that bounds the cost of exploration, forcing the router to explore efficiently within its financial envelope (Section~\ref{sec:budget_pacing}).
\end{itemize}
Every experiment in this paper examines how these knobs interact under the realities of budget-constrained, non-stationary deployment: stationary budget pacing (Section~\ref{sec:exp1_budget_pacing}), cost drift (Section~\ref{sec:exp2_cost_drift}), catastrophic model failure (Section~\ref{sec:exp3_catastrophic_failure}), cold-start model onboarding (Section~\ref{sec:exp4_model_onboarding}), and joint hyperparameter selection (Section~\ref{sec:exp5_hparam_sweep}).

\paragraph{Design rationale.}
No single existing algorithm simultaneously learns reward surfaces from bandit feedback, enforces a long-run budget constraint, and adapts to non-stationarity.
ParetoBandit's architecture is therefore an evolution that composes four foundational results, each addressing one facet of the problem.
The architectural cornerstone is the Primal-Dual Lagrangian relaxation of Agrawal and Devanur~\cite{agrawal2014bandits}, whose decomposition of a constrained bandit into unconstrained subproblems coupled through a dual variable $\lambda_t$ that encodes budget pressure.
This composability is what makes the design modular: the inner reward learner and the outer budget mechanism evolve independently, and the choice of inner algorithm is a free design parameter.

For the inner learner we adopt LinUCB~\cite{li2010contextual}, which models expected reward as a linear function of the context and maintains closed-form sufficient statistics ($A_a$, $b_a$) per arm.
Standard LinUCB, however, implicitly assumes two conditions that fail in production LLM routing: \emph{stationarity} (reward distributions do not change) and \emph{prior correctness} (any initialisation is either uninformative or accurate).
In practice, providers silently update models, pricing shifts, and prompt distributions drift---the canonical concept drift problem~\cite{gama2014survey,chen2023chatgptdrift}---invalidating both assumptions.
Offline warmup priors, trained on historical data, may already be partially stale by the time the router is deployed.
We therefore extend LinUCB with two robustness mechanisms, a \emph{forgetting factor} $\gamma$ and a \emph{tunable prior strength} $n_\mathit{eff}$, that address each failure mode.
Crucially, LinUCB's sufficient-statistic representation is what makes these extensions lightweight---the principal alternatives (Thompson Sampling, neural bandits, policy-gradient methods~\cite{wang2025barp}) do not jointly support them without substantial algorithmic redesign:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Non-stationary adaptation via $\gamma$.}  Geometric forgetting reduces to a scalar multiply ($A_a \leftarrow \gamma A_a$, $b_a \leftarrow \gamma b_a$), directly realising the discounted-UCB principle of Garivier and Moulines~\cite{garivier2011switching} without redesigning the update rule.  The parameter $\gamma \in (0,1]$ controls the effective memory horizon: recent evidence dominates, allowing the router to track reward drift, while historical observations are geometrically discounted rather than erased.
  \item \emph{Prior-strength control via $n_\mathit{eff}$.}  Warm-start initialization is a single matrix addition to $(A_a, b_a)$, instantiating the framework of Kveton et~al.~\cite{kveton2023warmstart}.  The tunable prior strength $n_\mathit{eff}$ acts as a safety valve against prior--reality mismatch: it sets how many pseudo-observations the offline priors contribute, controlling how quickly online evidence can override potentially stale offline estimates.  A conservative $n_\mathit{eff}$ lets the router begin learning from live traffic almost immediately; a large $n_\mathit{eff}$ trusts priors longer, trading adaptability for early stability.  Appendix~\ref{appendix:prior_mismatch} quantifies this trade-off: directionally correct priors reduce regret at every $n_\mathit{eff}$, but systematically inverted priors cause harm that scales with $n_\mathit{eff}$, making the choice of prior strength a critical deployment decision.
  \item \emph{Computational efficiency} follows from Sherman--Morrison rank-1 updates at $O(d^2)$ per step, keeping the router on the critical path at ${<}\,40\,\mu$s per decision (Appendix~\ref{sec:latency_benchmark}).
\end{enumerate}

While each ingredient is well-established individually, their composition introduces interactions that no single foundational paper analyses.
Geometric forgetting shrinks $A_a$ toward singularity over time, which inflates LinUCB's confidence bonus, which increases the probability of selecting expensive arms, which in turn drives up $\lambda_t$---a feedback loop between the forgetting mechanism and the budget pacer that must be jointly calibrated.
The EMA smoothing in the dual update (Eq.~\ref{eq:cost_ema}) addresses a further practical challenge---the $500{\times}$ cost variance across LLM tiers---that the original finite-horizon analysis does not consider.
The hyperparameter sweep in Section~\ref{sec:exp5_hparam_sweep} exists precisely to resolve these cross-component interactions under the contending demands of stability and agility.

\subsection{System Architecture Overview}

ParetoBandit operates as an asynchronous, closed-loop routing system (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). At inference time (step $t$), a user query $x_t$ is processed by a lightweight feature extractor (e.g., a SentenceTransformer embedding model), which passes the query vector to the \texttt{BanditRouter}. The router maintains a set of sufficient statistics for each model $a \in \mathcal{A}$, optionally initialized via \emph{offline-to-online warmup priors} that provide informed routing from the first request, eliminating the cold-start transient that would otherwise occur during initial deployment. The router computes a score for each model, incorporating uncertainty (LinUCB) and subtracting a dynamic cost penalty provided by the \texttt{BudgetPacer}. The router then selects the model $a_t$ with the highest score, returning its response to the user.

After the query completes and is evaluated (either synchronously by an automated judge, or asynchronously via delayed user feedback), the reward $r_t$ and exact cost $c_t$ are fed back into the system. The \texttt{BanditRouter} updates its reward estimates (incorporating geometric forgetting), while the \texttt{BudgetPacer} updates its internal dual variable $\lambda_t$, adjusting the cost penalties for future requests to steer the system back toward the target budget $B$.

\subsection{Primal-Dual Budget Pacing}
\label{sec:budget_pacing}

To enforce strict, dynamically adjusting budget constraints without manual offline tuning of a static penalty weight $\lambda_s$, ParetoBandit employs the \texttt{BudgetPacer}, an online Primal-Dual Lagrangian relaxation inspired by Agrawal and Devanur~\cite{agrawal2014bandits} but adapted from a cumulative knapsack constraint to a per-request rate constraint suitable for open-ended production streams (Section~\ref{sec:problem}).

At each step $t$, the router selects model $a_t$ by maximizing a budget-augmented utility function:
\begin{equation}
    a_t = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}_t} \Bigl[
        \underbrace{\hat\theta_a^\top x_t + \alpha \sqrt{x_t^\top A_a^{-1} x_t}}_{\text{LinUCB score~\cite{li2010contextual}}}
        \;-\; \underbrace{\lambda_s \,\tilde{c}_a}_{\text{static penalty}}
        \;-\; \underbrace{\lambda_t \,\tilde{c}_a}_{\text{dual penalty}}
    \Bigr]
    \label{eq:budget_ucb}
\end{equation}
The first term, $\hat\theta_a^\top x_t$, is the router's current best estimate of the expected reward for model~$a$ on prompt~$x_t$ (\emph{exploitation}).  The second term, $\alpha \sqrt{x_t^\top A_a^{-1} x_t}$, is a confidence bonus proportional to the router's \emph{uncertainty} about that estimate: models that have been selected rarely, or on dissimilar prompts, have larger bonuses, encouraging the router to try them (\emph{exploration}).
The exploration rate $\alpha > 0$ directly controls this tradeoff: higher values widen the confidence interval, driving the router to explore uncertain arms more aggressively at the cost of short-term reward; lower values concentrate traffic on the current best arm.
The cost penalty terms ($\lambda_s\tilde{c}_a + \lambda_t\tilde{c}_a$) reduce the effective score of expensive models, constraining exploration to arms the budget can sustain---a mechanism that becomes critical when an uninformed, newly onboarded model also happens to be expensive (Section~\ref{sec:exp4_model_onboarding}).

Here $\mathcal{A}_t \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ is the set of models surviving a hard cost ceiling (detailed below), $\lambda_t$ is a Lagrange multiplier updated after observing the realized cost $c_t$, and $\tilde{c}_a \in [0,1]$ is the log-normalized unit cost defined as
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{c}_a = \frac{\log c_a - \log c_{\min}}{\log c_{\max} - \log c_{\min}},
    \label{eq:cost_norm}
\end{equation}
with $c_{\min}$ and $c_{\max}$ fixed to the market-floor and market-ceiling blended rates of the model portfolio, respectively. Values at or below $c_{\min}$ map to 0; values at or above $c_{\max}$ map to 1. The logarithmic scale compresses the $500\times$ cost differential into a bounded penalty that is commensurate with the $[0,1]$ reward scale.  Intuitively, the log captures the fact that jumping from a \$0.00003 model to a \$0.003 model ($100\times$) is a comparable ``tier leap'' to jumping from \$0.003 to \$0.015 ($5\times$); on a linear scale the first gap would be invisible while the second would dominate the penalty, distorting the router's cost--quality reasoning.

\paragraph{Dual-Variable Dynamics and EMA Smoothing.}
The core innovation of the \texttt{BudgetPacer} is how it tracks and updates the dual variable $\lambda_t$ in response to the extreme variance in LLM pricing.  The mechanism works like a cruise-control system: when recent spending runs above the budget target, $\lambda_t$ increases, making expensive models less attractive and pushing the router toward cheaper options; when spending dips below target, $\lambda_t$ decreases, releasing the router to choose higher-quality (costlier) alternatives.  Rather than updating $\lambda_t$ directly from the raw per-request cost $c_t$, the pacer computes an \emph{EMA-smoothed} cost signal:
\begin{align}
    \bar{c}_t &= (1 - \alpha_{\mathrm{ema}})\,\bar{c}_{t-1}
                 + \alpha_{\mathrm{ema}}\,c_t
    \label{eq:cost_ema} \\
    \lambda_{t+1} &= \min\!\Bigl(\bar\lambda,\;
    \max\bigl(0,\;\lambda_t + \eta\,(\bar{c}_t / B - 1)\bigr)\Bigr)
    \label{eq:dual_update}
\end{align}
where $B$ is the user's desired average cost per request, $\alpha_{\mathrm{ema}}=0.05$ is the smoothing factor, $\eta=0.05$ is the step size, and $\bar\lambda=5$ is a capacity cap.

Using the EMA $\bar{c}_t$ is critical. If a single $\$0.015$ Gemini request were routed, the raw ratio $c_t/B$ could spike to $500\times$ the target $B$ (e.g., if $B=\$0.00003$). This would produce massive, sawtooth oscillations in $\lambda_t$ that never converge, rendering the penalty useless. The EMA smooths this variance, generating a stable gradient signal whose magnitude reflects the \emph{average} cost regime over recent history. 

Furthermore, normalizing the gradient by $B$ (i.e., $\bar{c}_t / B$) makes the step size $\eta$ portfolio-independent. The same $\eta$ produces equivalent $\lambda_t$ trajectories whether the cheapest model costs \$0.00003 or \$0.03, eliminating the need to retune hyperparameters when the pricing scale changes.

\paragraph{Two-Layer Enforcement (Adaptive Mode).}
The \texttt{BudgetPacer} combines two enforcement mechanisms simultaneously:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Hard ceiling (Safety Net):} When $\lambda_t > 0$, the candidate set $\mathcal{A}_t$ actively excludes models whose blended unit price exceeds the dynamic ceiling. This acts as a circuit breaker, preventing catastrophic overspend on any single request when the budget is severely constrained.
  \item \textbf{Soft penalty (Optimizer):} The dual penalty $\lambda_t\tilde{c}_a$ biases the UCB score toward cheaper models proportionally to their cost. This allows fine-grained, context-dependent routing within the surviving candidate set, minimizing quality loss within the hard budget envelope.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Non-Stationary Adaptation via Geometric Forgetting}
\label{sec:forgetting}

LLM routing in production is subject to \emph{concept drift}~\cite{gama2014survey}: the joint distribution of prompts, model quality, and pricing shifts over time as providers silently update models~\cite{chen2023chatgptdrift,chen2025silent}, user workloads evolve, and API pricing changes.
A standard LinUCB algorithm ($\gamma=1.0$) accumulates infinite memory, severely slowing adaptation to a shifting reward landscape because it must overcome thousands of historical observations before its estimates reflect the new regime. Conversely, entirely discarding history discards the valuable prior knowledge required for stable, informed routing.

ParetoBandit addresses non-stationarity through a \emph{forgetting factor} $\gamma \in (0, 1]$ applied directly to the disjoint LinUCB sufficient statistics. At each step $t$, before incorporating the new observation $(x_t, a_t, r_t)$, the design matrix and reward accumulator for the chosen arm $a_t$ are exponentially discounted:
\begin{align}
    A_{a_t} &\leftarrow \gamma \, A_{a_t} + x_t x_t^\top
    \label{eq:forget_A} \\
    b_{a_t} &\leftarrow \gamma \, b_{a_t} + r_t \, x_t
    \label{eq:forget_b}
\end{align}
This exponential weighting~\cite{garivier2011switching} gives observations an effective half-life of $\ln 2 / (1 - \gamma)$ steps (${\approx}139$ steps for $\gamma{=}0.995$) and an e-folding time of $1/(1-\gamma){=}200$ steps.  Concretely, an observation from $200$ steps ago retains weight $\gamma^{200} \approx e^{-1} \approx 0.37$.  If a model that was previously excellent suddenly degrades, the router notices within a few hundred requests rather than remaining anchored by thousands of stale observations.  We empirically find that $\gamma=0.995$ strikes an optimal balance, creating an effective memory window of approximately $200$ steps (see Section~\ref{sec:exp5_hparam_sweep} for the formal selection procedure). Recent evidence dominates, allowing the router to adapt to shifts rapidly, while older observations---including the offline warmup priors---are geometrically discounted.  After ${\sim}1{,}000$ steps, the prior's contribution is reduced by $\gamma^{1000} \approx 0.007$; after a full deployment ramp-up (${\sim}1{,}800$ steps), priors are effectively replaced by online evidence.  Warmup priors thus serve as a \emph{deployment latency optimisation}: they provide informed routing from request~\#1, eliminating the cold-start cost that would otherwise accumulate during the initial learning phase, but the router's steady-state quality is determined by online evidence regardless of initialisation (Appendix~\ref{appendix:val_burnin_ablation}).

The parameter $\gamma$ thus controls a bias--variance tradeoff between \emph{stability} (trusting historical evidence to maintain high baseline quality) and \emph{plasticity} (responding quickly to new evidence when models degrade).

\subsection{Computational Efficiency}
\label{sec:efficiency}

Because the router sits on the critical path of every LLM request, per-step latency must be negligible relative to model inference time.  We ensure this through three design choices.

\paragraph{$O(d^2)$ updates via Sherman-Morrison.}
Naive LinUCB recomputes $A_a^{-1}$ from scratch after each observation, costing $O(d^3)$.  ParetoBandit instead maintains a cached inverse $A_a^{-1}$ and applies the Sherman-Morrison rank-1 update formula:
\begin{equation}
    A_a^{-1} \leftarrow A_a^{-1}
        - \frac{(A_a^{-1} x_t)(x_t^\top A_a^{-1})}{1 + x_t^\top A_a^{-1} x_t},
    \label{eq:sherman_morrison}
\end{equation}
which incrementally adjusts the existing inverse to account for the new data point $x_t$, rather than recomputing the entire inverse from scratch---reducing each update to $O(d^2)$.  For the production feature dimension $d{=}26$, this is ${\sim}17{,}500\times$ faster than a full $O(d^3)$ inversion.  A full $O(d^3)$ recomputation is triggered only as a rare numerical-stability safeguard when the regularization floor decays below $10\%$ of its initial value under prolonged forgetting---in practice fewer than one in every thousand updates.

\paragraph{$O(Kd)$ arm selection.}
The expected reward $\hat{\theta}_a^\top x_t$ is a single $O(d)$ dot product per arm because $\hat{\theta}_a = A_a^{-1} b_a$ is cached and refreshed only at update time.  The UCB exploration bonus requires one $O(d^2)$ quadratic form $x_t^\top A_a^{-1} x_t$ per arm.  With $K{=}3$ arms and $d{=}26$, the full selection pass amounts to ${\sim}2{,}100$ floating-point operations---well under a microsecond on commodity hardware.

\paragraph{Constant-time geometric decay.}
When $\gamma < 1$, discounting $A_a \leftarrow \gamma\,A_a$ requires multiplying the cached inverse by the reciprocal: $A_a^{-1} \leftarrow A_a^{-1} / \gamma$---a scalar division that adds $O(d^2)$ element-wise operations but avoids a full matrix rebuild.  Combined with the Sherman-Morrison correction for the new observation, the total per-update cost remains $O(d^2)$ regardless of whether forgetting is active.

\paragraph{Empirical validation.}
A latency microbenchmark (Appendix~\ref{sec:latency_benchmark}) confirms that the production configuration ($d{=}26$, Sherman-Morrison) completes a full route\,+\,update cycle in $39\,\mu$s (p50) and sustains $>$\,24{,}000\,req/s on a single CPU core.  The combined effect of PCA dimensionality reduction and incremental inverse caching yields a $120\times$ throughput improvement over a naive full-inversion baseline operating on raw 384-D embeddings.
Including prompt embedding and PCA projection, the full end-to-end routing pipeline completes in ${\sim}8$\,ms on CPU---adding $<$\,1\% overhead even to the fastest production LLM call (Claude Haiku 4.5 at 952\,ms total latency~\cite{ganglani2026llmlatency}) and $<$\,0.2\% for typical workloads.

Despite the centrality of latency to production routing, no peer-reviewed LLM router---including bandit-based systems (LLM Bandit~\cite{li2025llmbandit}, BaRP~\cite{wang2025barp}, MixLLM~\cite{wang2025mixllm}), classifier-based routers (RouteLLM~\cite{ong2024routellm}, CSCR~\cite{shirkavand2025cscr}), or RL-based routers (PROTEUS~\cite{bhatti2026proteus})---reports per-decision or end-to-end routing latency.
An end-to-end decomposition of ParetoBandit's critical path (Appendix~\ref{sec:latency_benchmark}, Table~\ref{tab:latency_comparison}) reveals that the routing decision contributes $<$\,1\% of total overhead ($22\,\mu$s out of ${\sim}8$\,ms): prompt embedding dominates at 97\%, a cost shared by every contextual router.
The full ${\sim}8$\,ms pipeline runs entirely on CPU and is competitive with the fastest reported LLM routing systems, including GPU-accelerated neural classifiers~\cite{liu2026vllmsr}, without requiring a dedicated accelerator for the routing decision.
