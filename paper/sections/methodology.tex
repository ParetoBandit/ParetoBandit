\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

% TRANSITION from Empirical Motivation
Guided by the three architectural requirements established in Section~\ref{sec:motivation}, we present the design of banditGPT. To exploit semantic structure without relying on rigid static priors, we reformulate LLM routing as a \emph{Cost-Aware Contextual Bandit} problem initialized from offline data. To ensure safe online learning, we introduce a meta-learning layer that hedges against prior mismatch. Our approach combines two core mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Cost-aware contextual bandits} initialized from offline warmup priors, exploiting semantic structure for continuous online learning.
    \item \textbf{Corralling meta-learning} that hedges against prior mismatch via complementary exploration schedules, providing the necessary safety mechanism.
\end{itemize}

While several concurrent \emph{LLM routing} systems also adopt a bandit formulation~\cite{panda2025pilot, wang2025barp, li2025llmbandit}, distinct from the classical bandit-theory literature on Corralling~\cite{agarwal2017corralling}. banditGPT's distinguishing contribution is this hierarchical architecture that layers a Corralling meta-learner over cost-aware contextual bandits. This directly addresses the practical challenges of production routing: maintaining safety against misspecified priors while remaining agile enough to adopt new models.

\subsection{Unified Feature Space}

Building upon the empirical motivation, we embed prompts using \path{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2} (384D) and reduce to 32 dimensions using unsupervised PCA trained on 80k routing prompts from RouteLLM. As demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:motivation}, these features correlate with held-out model preference ($\rho=-0.370$, $p<0.0001$), confirming they capture routing-relevant signals suitable for online adaptation.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the context vector (embedding) for a user prompt at time $t$. We maintain a dynamic registry of models (arms) $\mathcal{M}_t$. For each request, the policy $\pi$ selects an arm $a_t \in \mathcal{M}_t$, observing a scalar reward $r_t \in [0, 1]$ (quality) and incurring a deterministic cost $c_t \in \mathbb{R}^+$ (inference price).

Unlike standard regret minimization, our objective is to maximize quality subject to a budget constraint, formulated as a Lagrangian relaxation with a user-defined penalty $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
\text{Maximize } \sum_{t=1}^T \left( \mathbb{E}[r_t(a_t) | x_t] - \lambda \cdot c_t(a_t) \right)
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ controls the trade-off between performance and economy. A high $\lambda$ forces the router to identify ``easy'' prompts where cheaper models suffice, while a low $\lambda$ prioritizes quality regardless of cost.

\subsubsection{Layer 1: Budget Constraint Filtering}

To enforce operational constraints, we implement a per-request eligibility check before the bandit layer. For each candidate model $m \in \mathcal{M}_t$, the router applies hard filters:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_{x_t} = \{m \in \mathcal{M}_t \mid c_m \leq c_{\max},\; \ell_m \leq \ell_{\max},\; q_m \geq q_{\min}\}
\end{equation}
where $c_{\max}$ and $q_{\min}$ are user-specified cost ceiling and quality floor constraints respectively. Models failing any constraint are excluded before the bandit selects, guaranteeing per-request constraint satisfaction. Latency filtering is supported in the implementation but not evaluated in this paper. Cost and quality trade-offs among eligible models are then handled by the cost-penalized LinUCB objective (Layer 3).

\subsubsection{Layer 2: Expert Corralling for Adaptive Meta-Learning}

Non-stationarity (e.g., a ``good'' model becoming ``bad'' due to drift) and misspecified offline priors pose significant challenges for standard bandit algorithms relying on fixed initializations. We address this \emph{at the meta level} via a strategy inspired by Expert Corralling~\cite{agarwal2017corralling}, which manages a portfolio of expert policies: a ``Warmup Expert'' initialized with static priors (constant $\alpha{=}2.0$) and a ``Tabula Rasa Expert'' with high plasticity (decaying $\alpha{=}1.0$ to $0.01$). Corralling exploits the \emph{complementarity} of these schedules: the warmup expert provides informed early selections, while the tabula rasa expert converges to the best policy over time. The meta-learner dynamically shifts weight between them, achieving 7 to 13\% lower regret than warmup-only and approximately 6\% lower than tabula rasa at low corruption ($\alpha \leq 0.2$), while providing detectable degradation signals when priors are adversarial (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Importantly, each expert bandit is itself trained under a \emph{stationary-reward assumption} (monotone information accumulation without explicit forgetting); prior mismatch is handled solely through the meta-learner's ability to reweight experts whose performance has degraded.

The master algorithm updates the probability distribution over experts $w_{i,t}$ using an exponential weight update based on importance-weighted loss estimates $\hat{\ell}_{i,t}$. To prevent ``Expert Death'' (where a temporarily poor expert's weight vanishes, rendering it unrecoverable), we implement a mixing parameter $\gamma$:
\begin{equation}
p_{i,t} = (1 - \gamma) \frac{w_{i,t}}{\sum_j w_{j,t}} + \frac{\gamma}{K}
\end{equation}

We set $\gamma=0.05$, imposing a uniform exploration floor that enables asymptotic recovery if the optimal expert changes over time.

\subsubsection{Layer 3: Cost-Aware LinUCB}

Each expert $i$ models the reward function using Bayesian Ridge Regression (LinUCB~\cite{li2010contextual}). We approximate the reward surface of model $m$ as a linear function of the context: $\mathbb{E}[r | x] = x^\top \theta^*_m$. The policy selects the arm maximizing the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) of the composite utility:
\begin{equation}
a_t = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{E}_{x_t}} \left( x_t^\top \hat{\thetavec}_a + \alpha \sqrt{x_t^\top \Amat_a^{-1} x_t} - \lambda c_a \right)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Hybrid LinUCB: Family-Level Parameter Sharing.}
When the model portfolio contains models from the same provider family (e.g., multiple iterations of the same foundation model), we extend LinUCB with a \emph{Hybrid} formulation~\cite{li2010contextual} that decomposes the reward coefficient into a shared family component $\betavec_f$ and a per-model residual $\thetavec_m$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[r | x, m] = x^\top (\betavec_{f(m)} + \thetavec_m)
\end{equation}
where $f(m)$ maps model $m$ to its family. The shared $\betavec_f$ is updated by \emph{every} observation from any family member, while $\thetavec_m$ captures model-specific deviations. When all families are singletons, the hybrid formulation reduces to disjoint LinUCB with independent per-model parameters. The family structure is operationally relevant during model onboarding (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}): when a new model joins an existing family, it immediately inherits the family's accumulated $\betavec$, providing a continuously updated reward estimate. A leave-one-out ablation (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_null}) confirms that this continuous family sharing subsumes one-shot per-model $\thetavec$ transfer, suggesting that the hybrid decomposition is an effective abstraction level for cross-model knowledge transfer in our evaluation.

\paragraph{Family Assignment.}
The mapping $f(m)$ is resolved via a three-level cascade, in decreasing order of priority:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Explicit override.} Users may supply a \texttt{family\_map} dictionary at initialization, mapping each model ID to a family label. This allows domain-specific groupings that cannot be inferred automatically.
    \item \textbf{Registry metadata.} If a model's registry entry contains a \texttt{family} field, that value is used. This supports declarative configuration without code changes.
    \item \textbf{Syntactic inference (default).} \texttt{infer\_model\_family()} strips known suffixes (size qualifiers, instruction-tuning tags, date stamps, parameter counts) from OpenRouter-style identifiers to extract a canonical family (e.g., \texttt{openai/gpt-4-turbo} $\to$ \texttt{openai/gpt-4}).
\end{enumerate}
In addition, the library provides \texttt{compute\_correlation\_families()}, which computes within-provider tetrachoric correlations from binary reward vectors and clusters models via connected components at a configurable threshold (default $r_{\text{tet}} \geq 0.6$). This data-driven assignment can be passed as an explicit override when historical reward data is available. In our experiments, we use syntactic inference for $K{=}2$ and $K{=}3$ evaluations and data-driven tetrachoric assignment for $K{\geq}5$ (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{Exploration Parameter Selection.}
The warmup expert uses constant $\alpha{=}2.0$ (no decay) to preserve distribution-shift detection; the tabula rasa expert decays from $\alpha{=}1.0$ to $0.01$, converging to exploitation. This asymmetry is deliberate: the warmup expert maintains exploration so the meta-learner can detect when priors become stale, while the tabula rasa expert converges to the best learned policy.

We select $\alpha{=}2.0$ as a practical default: it falls within common LinUCB confidence choices~\cite{li2010contextual} and we find performance is relatively insensitive to $\alpha$ across a broad range in supplementary sweeps.

To ensure numerical stability during long-running operations, we implement an \emph{Amortized Regularization Floor}. We track the effective decay of the precision matrix $\Amat_a$ and proactively reinject regularization $\lambda_{\text{ridge}}\mathbf{I}$ whenever the smallest eigenvalue risks dropping below a safety threshold, preventing singular matrix inversions without the cost of $O(d^3)$ operations at every step.

\paragraph{Regularization in PCA Space.}
We initialize the precision matrix as $\Amat_0 = \lambda \mathbf{I}$, which corresponds to an isotropic prior in the PCA-transformed feature space. This does not distinguish between high- and low-variance principal components and may over-regularize the latter. We adopt this deliberately simple baseline for stability; designing anisotropic or variance-matched regularization (e.g., diagonal $\Amat_0$ scaled by component variance, or full whitening before the bandit layer) is a natural extension that we leave for future work.

\subsection{Meta-learning and dynamic portfolios (summary)}
\label{sec:transfer}
\label{sec:corralling}

In settings where prior quality is uncertain and model portfolios evolve, banditGPT uses a Corralling-style coordinator~\cite{agarwal2017corralling} to mix two experts: a warmup expert initialized from offline priors (constant exploration) and a tabula rasa expert that decays exploration. The coordinator maintains non-zero probability on each expert to avoid starvation and updates expert weights based on observed rewards. We defer full update rules and onboarding details to the supplementary material.

\subsection{Prior Calibration}

Loading pre-trained priors from offline data can introduce \emph{Scale Explosion} (predictions of 800.0 instead of 0.8) due to mismatched reward scales. We address this with automatic two-pass calibration at initialization: (1)~a bias probe that detects and clamps inflated bias weights via theta-reconstruction, and (2)~a suite probe using basis-independent feature vectors that catches PCA-dimension explosions. Each expert additionally maintains a lightweight $O(1)$-per-observation prediction monitor for runtime anomaly detection.
